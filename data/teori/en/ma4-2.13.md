# Oblique asymptotes

An asymptote that is not parallel to either of the coordinate axes, that is
one that is neither vertical nor horizontal, is called an **oblique asymptote**. Oblique
asymptotes occur for rational functions where the degree of the numerator is exactly one
higher than the degree of the denominator.

To find an oblique asymptote of a function $f(x)$, the aim is to rewrite
$f(x)$ as the sum of a straight line $y = kx + m$ and a "remainder term" $R(x)$ that
disappears when $x$ becomes very large.

::: formel "Finding an oblique asymptote"
Rewrite the function $f(x)$ in the form

$$
f(x) = kx + m + R(x) \qquad \text{where} \quad \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} R(x) = 0
$$

The line $y = kx + m$ is then the oblique asymptote, since the function value approaches
this straight line for large values (positive or negative) of $x$.
:::

Try it yourself in the tool below. Drag the sliders for $k$ and $m$ and see how the
oblique asymptote moves, while the remainder term $\dfrac{4}{x-3}$ only gives a
local "bump" around $x = 3$ that dies away further out. With $k = 2$ and $m = 1$
you get the function $y = 2x + 1 + \dfrac{4}{x-3}$ from Example 2 below.

::: textlosning
We see that the curve makes a jump at $x = 2$, which means that it has a
**vertical asymptote** $x = 2$.

We also see that for large $x$ the curve seems to approach a straight line. We find
the equation of this **oblique asymptote**. Since it is a straight line,
$y = kx + m$, where we read off

$$
k = -1 \quad (\text{the gradient}) \qquad m = 1 \quad (\text{the intersection with the } y\text{-axis})
$$

Substituting $k = -1$ and $m = 1$ into the equation of a straight line gives $y = -x + 1$.

**Answer:** $x = 2$ and $y = -x + 1$
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Find the oblique asymptote"
**Find the oblique asymptote of the curve<br>a)&nbsp;$y = 2x + 1 + \dfrac{4}{x-3}$&emsp;&emsp;b)&nbsp;$y = \dfrac{2x^2 - 4x + 3}{x}$.**

::: handskrift
typ: snedasymptot
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The expression already has the form $y = kx + m + R(x)$, where the remainder term
$R(x) = \dfrac{4}{x-3}$ tends to $0$ as $x$ tends to infinity. The oblique
asymptote can then be written directly as $y = 2x + 1$.

**Answer:** $y = 2x + 1$

**b)** Here we first rewrite the expression as a sum of terms by splitting
the fraction, so that we get it in the form $y = (kx + m) + R(x)$:

$$
y = \frac{2x^2 - 4x + 3}{x} = \frac{2x^2}{x} - \frac{4x}{x} + \frac{3}{x} = 2x - 4 + \frac{3}{x}
$$

Since the remainder term $\dfrac{3}{x} \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$, the oblique
asymptote can be written $y = 2x - 4$.

**Answer:** $y = 2x - 4$
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Oblique asymptote"
- An asymptote that is neither vertical nor horizontal is called **oblique**.
- They occur for rational functions where the degree of the numerator is **exactly one higher** than that of the denominator.
:::

::: sampunkt "The form"
- Rewrite the function as $f(x) = kx + m + R(x)$, where $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} R(x) = 0$.
- The line $y = kx + m$ is then the oblique asymptote.
- The remainder term $R(x)$ only gives a local deviation that dies away further out.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to rewrite"
- If the expression already has the right form, the asymptote can be read off directly.
- Otherwise: **split the fraction** into several terms, one for each term in the numerator.
- $\dfrac{2x^2 - 4x + 3}{x} = 2x - 4 + \dfrac{3}{x}$, so the asymptote is $y = 2x - 4$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Reading from a graph"
- A jump in the curve shows a **vertical** asymptote.
- If the curve approaches a straight line for large $x$, it is an **oblique** asymptote.
- Read off $k$ as the slope and $m$ as the intersection with the $y$-axis.
:::
