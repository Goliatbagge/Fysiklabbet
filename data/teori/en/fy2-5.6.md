# Star formation

A star is an enormous ball of glowing gas that shines with the energy from
**fusion** in its core. But how does a star come into being in the first place?
The answer begins in the thin, cold clouds of gas between the stars, and ends
with a struggle between two forces: **gravity**, which pulls together, and
**pressure**, which strives to resist.

## It all begins in a cloud of gas

The space between the stars is not completely empty. It contains huge
[[molecular clouds|A large, cold and dense cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust in space, where new stars are born.]] of mostly **hydrogen gas** and a little **dust**. The clouds are
very cold, around 10–20 K, and can be many light years across and weigh
thousands of solar masses.

As long as the cloud is undisturbed there is a **balance**: the gas's own pressure holds
out against the weak gravity and nothing happens. But if the cloud is disturbed, by
the pressure wave from a nearby [[supernova|A huge explosion when the core of a massive star collapses. The pressure wave can compress nearby gas clouds and start new star formation.]] or a collision with
another cloud, part of it can become dense enough for
gravity to take over. Then the cloud starts to **collapse**.

## Gravity against pressure

Star formation can be described as a constant tug of war between two forces:

- **Gravity** pulls all the gas inwards towards a common centre.
- **Gas pressure** (and later radiation pressure) pushes outwards and holds
  out.

A cloud starts to contract under its own weight only if it is sufficiently
**massive and cold**. Then gravity wins over pressure. The more
the gas is compressed, the **hotter** it becomes, just like the air in a
bicycle pump warms up when it is compressed. The released
[[potential energy|Energy that an object has because of its position in a force field, here the gravitational one. Released as heat when the gas falls inwards.]] is converted into heat, and in the middle an ever
hotter clump forms: a **protostar**.

::: tips "Why does a collapsing gas heat up?"
When gas falls inwards towards the centre, its **potential energy** is converted into
**kinetic energy**, and the collisions between the particles make the gas
hotter. The same principle as in a bicycle pump: if you compress a gas, its
temperature rises. It is gravity itself that supplies the heat that
eventually ignites the star.
:::

## The protostar ignites

The core becomes hotter and denser over hundreds of thousands of years while more gas
continues to fall in. When the temperature in the middle reaches about
**10 million kelvin**, something decisive happens: the hydrogen nuclei begin
to **fuse** into helium.

::: formel "Hydrogen fusion (simplified)"
$$
4\ {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} \longrightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + \text{energy}
$$

Four hydrogen nuclei (protons) combine into one helium nucleus. The
combined nucleus weighs slightly **less** than the four protons
together. The mass difference is released as energy according to $E = mc^{2}$.
:::

Fusion releases enormous amounts of energy. The heat keeps up the **gas pressure**
in the core, and the radiation adds a **radiation pressure**. Together they can
finally hold out against gravity. When the outward pressure and the inward pull
of gravity come into equilibrium the star stops shrinking. It has become a
**fully formed star** and has reached the stable stage called
**the main sequence**, where the Sun is today.

::: tips "Hydrostatic equilibrium"
A fully formed star is in **hydrostatic equilibrium**: the outward gas pressure
and radiation pressure, kept up by the heat from fusion, exactly balance gravity
pulling inwards. As long as there is
hydrogen fuel left, the equilibrium holds: for the Sun, a total of about 10 billion
years. It is this balance that allows a star to shine steadily for such an
immensely long time.
:::

## How big does the star become?

How much gas collects in the protostar decides what kind of star it
becomes, and how long it lives:

| Mass | Type | Lifetime |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below ~0.08 solar masses | Brown dwarf (never really ignites) | — |
| About 0.1–0.5 solar masses | Small red dwarf | Trillions of years |
| About 1 solar mass (like the Sun) | Yellow star | about 10 billion years |
| Above ~10 solar masses | Hot blue giant star | Only a few million years |

It may seem back to front that the **heaviest** stars live the **shortest**:
after all, they have the most fuel. But they burn their hydrogen so enormously much
faster that they still run out first. A heavy star lives fast and
dies young.

::: kuriosa "The Sun was not born alone"
Stars are almost never formed one at a time, but in **star clusters** from the same
molecular cloud. Our own Sun was probably born together with thousands of
sibling stars about **4.6 billion years** ago. Over time they have
spread out along their orbits around the centre of the Milky Way, so today we do
not know for certain which stars were the Sun's siblings.
:::

::: kuriosa "We are stardust"
The hydrogen gas that forms new stars is almost as old as the universe
itself. But the heavier elements, the carbon, oxygen and iron in your body,
were created inside earlier generations of stars and spread out into space when
they died in supernovae. Every new star system, including our own,
is built partly from the remains of dead stars.
:::
