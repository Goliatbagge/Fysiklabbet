# Summary: Light as wave and particle

This chapter was about light as both an electromagnetic wave and a
particle. You have seen how light spreads, is refracted and interferes as a wave
(diffraction, the grating formula, the law of refraction) and how light at the same time
behaves as particles, photons, in the photoelectric effect and
Compton scattering. The chapter ties this together with black-body radiation,
the energy levels of the atom, spectral lines and de Broglie's hypothesis that all
matter has wave properties.

## Concepts to know

- **Electromagnetic wave**: light as an oscillating electric and
  magnetic field, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation;
  travels at the speed of light *c*.
- **Light intensity**: energy per unit time and area (W/m²); decreases with
  the square of the distance from a point source.
- **Coherent and monochromatic light**: light of the same wavelength (and, for
  coherent light, in phase), a requirement for clear interference.
- **Diffraction**: the bending of waves around obstacles or through a narrow
  opening.
- **Interference and the grating formula**: how waves from several slits
  reinforce or cancel each other in particular directions.
- **Black-body radiation and emittance**: the temperature-dependent radiation of an ideal object,
  described by Wien's displacement law and
  the Stefan–Boltzmann law.
- **Refractive index and the law of refraction**: how much light changes direction
  at the boundary between two media with different optical density.
- **Photon**: a quantum (an "energy packet") of electromagnetic radiation
  with the energy $E = h \cdot f$ and the momentum $p = h/\lambda$.
- **Photoelectric effect**: when a photon with enough energy knocks
  an electron loose from a metal surface; proves the particle nature of light.
- **de Broglie wavelength**: the wavelength that all moving particles (not
  only photons) have, $\lambda = h/(m \cdot v)$.
- **Emission and absorption spectrum**: discrete wavelengths that are
  characteristic (a "fingerprint") of each element.
- **Energy level and quantum jump**: electrons in an atom can only have
  certain energies; a photon is emitted or absorbed when the electron
  changes energy level.

## Formulas

::: formel "The formulas of the chapter"
**Light as an electromagnetic wave**

$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda}
$$

where

- *f* = frequency of the light (Hz)
- *c* = speed of light ($\approx 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}$ m/s in a vacuum)
- *λ* = wavelength of the light (m)

$$
I = \frac{P}{A} = \frac{P}{4\pi \cdot r^{2}}
$$

where

- *I* = light intensity (W/m²)
- *P* = power (W)
- *A* = area that is hit (m²)
- *r* = distance to the point source (m)

**Diffraction and interference**

$$
n \cdot \lambda = d \cdot \sin\alpha_n
$$

where

- *n* = order of the maximum
- *λ* = wavelength of the light (m)
- *d* = slit separation (grating constant) (m)
- $\alpha_n$ = angle of deflection to the *n*th order maximum (degrees)

**Black-body radiation**

$$
M = \frac{P}{A}
$$

where

- *M* = emittance (W/m²)
- *P* = radiated power (W)
- *A* = area (m²)

$$
\lambda_\mathrm{max} \cdot T = 2.8978 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K}
$$

where

- $\lambda_\mathrm{max}$ = wavelength with the greatest emittance/radiation (m)
- *T* = temperature (K)

$$
M = \sigma \cdot T^{4}
$$

where

- *M* = emittance (W/m²)
- *σ* = $5.67 \cdot 10^{-8}$ W/(m²·K⁴) (the Stefan–Boltzmann constant)
- *T* = temperature (K)

**Refraction of light**

$$
n = \frac{c}{v}
$$

where

- *n* = refractive index of the substance
- *c* = speed of light in a vacuum ($2.998\ldots \cdot 10^{8}$ m/s)
- *v* = speed of light in the substance (m/s)

$$
n_1 \cdot \sin i = n_2 \cdot \sin b
$$

where

- $n_1$ = refractive index of medium 1
- *i* = angle of incidence in medium 1
- $n_2$ = refractive index of medium 2
- *b* = angle of refraction in medium 2

$$
i = r
$$

where *i* = angle of incidence and *r* = angle of reflection (the law of reflection).

**Photons and the photoelectric effect**

$$
E = \frac{h \cdot c}{\lambda} = h \cdot f
$$

where

- *E* = energy of the light/photon (J)
- *h* $\approx 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}$ Js (Planck's constant)
- *c* = speed of light (m/s)
- *λ* = wavelength of the light (m)
- *f* = frequency of the light (Hz)

$$
1\ \mathrm{eV} \approx 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}
$$

$$
p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{h \cdot f}{c}
$$

where *p* = momentum of the photon (kg·m/s).

$$
p = m \cdot v
$$

where *p* = momentum (kg·m/s), *m* = mass (kg) and *v* = velocity
(m/s): classical momentum, applies to objects with mass.

$$
h \cdot f = W_\mathrm{u} + E_\mathrm{k}
$$

where

- $h \cdot f$ = energy of the photon (J)
- $W_\mathrm{u}$ = work function (J)
- $E_\mathrm{k}$ = kinetic energy of the electron after it has been freed (J)

**Wave-particle duality**

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{m \cdot v}
$$

where *λ* = de Broglie wavelength of a particle with mass *m* and
velocity *v* (m).

**Bohr's atomic model**

$$
h \cdot f = E_n - E_m = \frac{h \cdot c}{\lambda}
$$

where $E_n$ = energy at the higher energy level and $E_m$ = energy at the
lower energy level (both in J).

$$
E_n \approx -\frac{13.6}{n^{2}}\ \mathrm{eV}
$$

where $E_n$ = energy at energy level *n* (*n* = 1 corresponds to the ground state,
*n* = 2 to the first excited state, and so on); applies to the hydrogen atom.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- The electromagnetic spectrum is ordered by wavelength: radio waves,
  microwaves, infrared, visible light (400–700 nm), ultraviolet,
  X-rays and gamma rays; the shorter the wavelength, the higher the
  frequency and energy.
- Light behaves both as a wave (diffraction, interference, refraction) and
  as a particle (photoelectric effect, Compton scattering);
  wave-particle duality applies to all matter, but the de Broglie wavelength is
  only noticeable for microscopic particles such as electrons, not for
  everyday objects.
- When refracted into an optically denser medium, the ray bends **towards**
  the normal; into an optically less dense medium it bends **away from** the normal.
  The law of reflection ($i = r$) always applies at the same time as refraction.
- It is the frequency (energy) of the photon, not the intensity of the light, that decides
  whether the photoelectric effect occurs: a strong but low-energy light
  (for example red) never knocks out electrons if $h \cdot f$ is smaller than
  the work function.
- Wien's displacement law: the hotter the body, the shorter the peak wavelength.
  The Stefan–Boltzmann law: the emittance grows rapidly (with $T^4$) when
  the temperature increases.
- The emission and absorption spectra of the same element have
  spectral lines at exactly the same wavelengths: atoms can only absorb
  the same wavelengths that they themselves emit.
- In an energy level diagram, the ionisation limit is set to 0 eV, and all
  bound states (the electron remains in the atom) have negative energy.
- eV is a convenient unit of energy on the atomic scale; always convert to
  joules ($1\ \mathrm{eV} \approx 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$) before you
  calculate velocities or momentum.
- Momentum is conserved in photon absorption and Compton scattering, just
  as in an ordinary collision, but the photon has no mass, so its
  momentum is calculated with $p = h/\lambda$, not $p = m \cdot v$.

## Before the test

- Can you describe the structure of the electromagnetic spectrum and order
  radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet,
  X-rays and gamma rays by wavelength?
- Do you know the formula for the frequency of light ($f = c/\lambda$) and for
  light intensity ($I = P/A = P/(4\pi r^2)$), and can you solve for any quantity in
  them?
- Can you explain the difference between coherent, monochromatic and
  incoherent light, and between diffraction and interference?
- Do you know the grating formula ($n \cdot \lambda = d \cdot \sin\alpha_n$), and can you
  use it to determine wavelength, slit separation or angle?
- Do you know Wien's displacement law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law, and can you explain
  what happens to the radiation of a black body when the temperature increases?
- Do you know the law of refraction (Snell's law), and can you decide in which direction a ray
  is refracted: towards or away from the normal?
- Can you calculate the energy of a photon ($E = h \cdot f = hc/\lambda$) and
  its momentum ($p = h/\lambda$), and convert between eV and joules?
- Do you know the formula for the photoelectric effect
  ($h \cdot f = W_\mathrm{u} + E_\mathrm{k}$), and can you explain why only
  light above a certain frequency (not intensity) can knock out electrons?
- Do you know de Broglie's formula ($\lambda = h/(m \cdot v)$), and can you decide whether a
  particle is small enough to show noticeable wave properties?
- Can you use the energy level diagram for hydrogen to determine which
  wavelength is emitted in a given quantum jump ($h \cdot f = E_n - E_m$)?
