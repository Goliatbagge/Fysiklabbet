# Faraday cage

If we apply an [[electric field|The region around a charge where another charge is affected by an electric force.]] across a metal cage, conduction electrons
will move against the field. If we for example apply a field directed to the right
through the cage, conduction electrons move to the left and the left part of
the cage becomes negatively charged while the right part becomes positively charged.

Inside the cage, a field arises instantly (at the [[speed|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of light) in the opposite
direction to the external electric field (field lines go from the cage's positive
side to its negative side, to the left in the figure), and it is equally strong. **Inside
the cage there is therefore no resultant electric field!** A metal
enclosure like this, which cancels electric fields, is called a **Faraday cage**.

The fact that no electric fields arise inside metal structures is the reason
why we are often safe in a car during a thunderstorm: the car's body works
as a Faraday cage. It is not because of insulating rubber tyres, which is a
common misconception.

::: demo "Faraday cage"
If we hang paper strips or metal balls over the edge of a metal cage
and then charge the cage, we see that the objects are strongly affected by the field on
the **outside** of the cage, where they stand straight out, but hang unaffected straight down on
the **inside**. The excess charges in the cage repel each other and gather
on the outside of the cage. This means that no field arises inside the cage.
:::

::: demo "Point discharge"
If you charge a pointed conductor, charges become concentrated at the tip so that
the local electric field strength becomes so strong that a discharge occurs
from the tip to the air molecules, which then become charged. This type of discharge
is called **point discharge**. The air molecules and the tips then repel
each other and the "propeller" starts to spin.
:::

::: kuriosa "Point discharge or St Elmo's fire"
Point discharge is also called **St Elmo's fire** and can occur at pointed
lightning conductors, mast tops and similar objects, for example during thunderstorms when the local
electric field strength is high. In the dark it can be seen as a faint blue
outward glow at the tips of these objects. So it is
not the same thing as lightning. Unlike a lightning strike, which is mainly
a discharge of electrons, St Elmo's fire is a discharge of [[photons|A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Carries energy *E* = *h* · *f*, where *h* is Planck's constant.]]
(particles of light). It is therefore fairly silent (a faint buzzing sound)
compared with a lightning strike.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Faraday cage"
- A metal enclosure that **cancels** electric fields inside it.
- An external field pushes the conduction electrons aside, so one side becomes
  negative and the other positive.
- The charges create an **equally strong opposing field** inside, which
  arises instantly.
- **The resultant field inside the cage becomes zero.**
:::

::: sampunkt "Excess charges end up on the outside"
- Like charges repel each other and move as far apart as
  possible, that is out to the surface.
- That is why paper strips stand straight out on the **outside** but hang unaffected
  on the **inside**.
:::

::: sampunkt "The car in a thunderstorm"
- You are safe in the car because the **body** works as a Faraday
  cage.
- It is **not** the rubber tyres. That is a common misconception.
:::

::: sampunkt "Point discharge"
- In a pointed conductor, the charges become concentrated at the tip, so
  the field strength there becomes very high.
- A discharge then occurs to the air molecules, which become charged and are repelled.
- Also called **St Elmo's fire**, seen as a faint blue glow at
  mast tops and lightning conductors.
- Unlike lightning, it is a discharge of **photons**, and
  therefore almost silent.
:::
:::
