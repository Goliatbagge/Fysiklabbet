# The atomic nucleus

The **atom** or **nuclide** consists of a **nucleus** made up of
**protons** (p) and **neutrons** (n), together with **electrons** (e⁻) around
the nucleus. Protons, neutrons and electrons are called **elementary particles**.
The particles in the nucleus (protons and neutrons) are called
**nuclear particles** or **nucleons**.

According to Bohr's model of the atom, the electrons are in different "shells" at
fixed distances from the nucleus.

In the shell closest to the nucleus, the electron has its lowest energy state. If we supply
the electron with enough energy it can be **excited** and jump out to
an orbit with a higher energy state. After a while the electron will
jump back to its ground state and be **de-excited**. The
corresponding energy is then released in the form of a [[photon|A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Carries energy *E* = *h* · *f*, where *h* is Planck's constant.]] (more about that in the next lesson).

The masses of the elementary particles are very small. As a unit for small masses
we use the atomic mass unit 1 u = 1.66054 · 10⁻²⁷ kg, which roughly corresponds to
the mass of a proton or neutron.

| Particle | Charge | Mass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C | 1.00728 u |
| Neutron | 0 | 1.00866 u |
| Electron | −1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C | 0.00055 u |

If there were no more fundamental [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). Vector quantity.]] than the [[gravitational force|The force that acts between all masses. At the Earth's surface it is called the force of gravity (weight).]] and
the electromagnetic force, the protons in the nucleus should repel each other.
So there is a stronger force that holds them together. The force that holds
the nucleons together is called the **strong nuclear force**, the **strong force** or
the **strong interaction**, and it only acts over short distances (about 10⁻¹⁵ m).
This means that large nuclei with many nucleons can become unstable
and decay ([[radioactivity|Spontaneous decay of unstable atomic nuclei with emission of radiation (α, β or γ).]]).

::: formel "Composition of the nucleus"
The composition of a nucleus is written $_Z^A X$ where

- *X* = the chemical symbol of the element
- *A* = the **[[mass number|The total number of protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus. Denoted *A*.]]**. Gives the number of nucleons (p and n) in the nucleus. Gives
  roughly the mass of the nucleus in u.
- *Z* = the **[[atomic number|The number of protons in an atomic nucleus. Determines which element it is. Denoted *Z*.]]**. Gives the number of protons (p) in the nucleus. Also gives
  the number of e⁻ in the atom and the positive charge of the nucleus.
:::

Atoms with more or fewer electrons than protons (and therefore with a charge)
are called **ions**. Atoms of the same element but with different numbers of neutrons
are called **[[isotopes|Atoms of the same element (same *Z*) but with different numbers of neutrons (different *A*).]]** (from Greek iso = same and topos = place; they have
the same place in the periodic table).

| Isotope | Systematic notation | Alternative | Mass number | Number of protons | Number of neutrons | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen-1 | ${_1^1} \mathrm{H}$ or $^1\mathrm{H}$ | H-1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | Protium |
| Hydrogen-2 | ${_1^2} \mathrm{H}$ or $^2\mathrm{H}$ | H-2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | Deuterium |
| Hydrogen-3 | ${_1^3} \mathrm{H}$ or $^3\mathrm{H}$ | H-3 | 3 | 1 | 2 | Tritium |

::: textlosning
**a)** $_1^1 \mathrm{H}$ **b)** $_1^2 \mathrm{H}$ **c)** ⁴He **d)** Beryllium-9

**a)** $_1^1 \mathrm{H}$. The number at the bottom left says that the nucleus has
1 proton. The number at the top left says that the number of protons AND neutrons
is 1. So there are no neutrons in the nucleus. You can find the number of
neutrons in the nucleus by subtracting the atomic number (the number "at the bottom") from
the mass number (the number "at the top"): (1 − 1) = 0 neutrons.

**Answer:** One proton (+).

**b)** $_1^2 \mathrm{H}$. The number of protons is 1. The number of neutrons is
(2 − 1) = 1.

**Answer:** One proton (+) and one neutron.

**c)** ⁴He. In this notation the atomic number has not been written out. Then
we have to check it in a table of the elements: He stands for helium with
atomic number 2. The nucleus therefore has 2 protons. The number of neutrons is
(4 − 2) = 2.

**Answer:** Two protons (+) and two neutrons.

**d)** Beryllium-9. This is yet another way of writing atomic nuclei.
We see that the element is beryllium and 9 stands for the mass number, that is the
total number of protons and neutrons. Beryllium has atomic number 4. The nucleus
therefore has 4 protons and (9 − 4) = 5 neutrons.

**Answer:** Four protons (+) and five neutrons.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Building blocks of the atom"
- The nucleus contains **protons** (p) and **neutrons** (n). Around it
  there are **electrons** (e⁻).
- All three are called **elementary particles**. The two in the nucleus are called
  **nucleons** or nuclear particles.
:::

::: sampunkt "The notation"
- A nucleus is written $_Z^A \mathrm{X}$.
- $A$ is the **mass number**: the number of nucleons, that is protons plus
  neutrons.
- $Z$ is the **atomic number**: the number of protons. It determines the element and
  the number of electrons in a neutral atom.
- The number of neutrons is therefore $A - Z$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Masses and charges"
- The atomic mass unit:
  $1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.66054 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, roughly the
  mass of one nucleon.
- Proton: $+1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ and
  $1.00728\ \mathrm{u}$.
- Neutron: charge 0 and $1.00866\ \mathrm{u}$.
- Electron: $-1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ and only
  $0.00055\ \mathrm{u}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Ion and isotope"
- **Ion**: an atom with more or fewer electrons than protons, that is with
  a charge.
- **Isotopes**: the same element, that is the same $Z$, but **different numbers of
  neutrons**.
- The isotopes of hydrogen: protium, deuterium and tritium.
:::

::: sampunkt "The strong nuclear force"
- The protons repel each other, so something stronger must hold the
  nucleus together.
- The **strong nuclear force** only acts over very short distances, about
  $10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$.
- That is why **large** nuclei become unstable and decay.
:::

::: sampunkt "Excitation"
- If energy is supplied, an electron can be **excited** to an orbit with higher
  energy.
- When it falls back it is **de-excited** and emits a **photon**
  with the corresponding energy.
:::
:::
