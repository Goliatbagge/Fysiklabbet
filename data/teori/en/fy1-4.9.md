# Work with friction

When [[work|Energy transferred when a force acts along a distance: *W* = *F* · *s* · cos *α*. SI unit joule (J).]] is done with [[friction|The force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact.]], the work done by the frictional force
becomes **frictional heat**.

::: formel "Frictional heat"
$$
E_v = F_f \cdot s
$$

where

- $E_v$ = frictional heat (J or Nm)
- $F_f$ = frictional force (N)
- *s* = [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]] (m)
:::

If a change in height takes place, the work is calculated differently depending on whether there is friction
or not.

::: formel "Gravitational potential energy and work"
If a change in height takes place

- **without friction**, the work *W* corresponds to the change in [[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]]
  Δ$E_p$:

  $$
  W = \Delta E_p
  $$

- **with friction**, the work corresponds to "the change in potential energy" + "frictional heat"
  $E_v$:

  $$
  W = \Delta E_p + E_v
  $$
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Cart up an inclined plane with friction (Advanced)"
**You pull a cart 5.0 m up an inclined plane with an angle of inclination of 30°, so
that it ends up 2.5 m above its starting height. The cart has a mass of 32 kg. How much work
do you do if the cart slides with a coefficient of friction of 0.35?**

::: handskrift
typ: vagnfriktion
:::

::: textlosning
**Think:** Into which form or forms of energy is the work converted? In this
case both potential energy $E_p$ **and** [[thermal energy|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]] $E_v$ are produced. The thermal energy
is given by the work done by the frictional force. The work therefore gives an increase in
potential energy $E_p$ and thermal energy $E_v$.

$$
W = E_p + E_v
$$

Substituting $E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h$ and $E_v = F_f \cdot s$ gives

$$
W = m \cdot g \cdot h + F_f \cdot s
$$

We have all the values except the frictional force $F_f$. With $F_f = \mu \cdot F_N$
we get

$$
W = m \cdot g \cdot h + \mu \cdot F_N \cdot s
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 32\ \mathrm{kg} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \\
h = 2.5\ \mathrm{m} \\
\mu = 0.35 \\
F_N = F_2 = m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha = 32 \cdot 9.82 \cdot \cos 30^{\circ} = 272.139\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \\
s = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
W = 32 \cdot 9.82 \cdot 2.5 + 0.35 \cdot 272.139\ldots \cdot 5.0
= 1\,261.844\ldots\ \mathrm{Nm} \approx 1\,300\ \mathrm{Nm} = 1.3\ \mathrm{kNm}
$$

**Answer:** 1.3 kNm
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Frictional heat"
- The work done by the frictional force becomes **heat**.
- $E_v = F_f \cdot s$, where $s$ is the **actual distance** along
  the surface, not the difference in height.
:::

::: sampunkt "Work with and without friction"
- **Without friction**: $W = \Delta E_p$.
- **With friction**: $W = \Delta E_p + E_v$.
- With friction, **more** work is therefore needed for the same gain in height.
:::

::: sampunkt "The whole formula"
- $W = m \cdot g \cdot h + \mu \cdot F_N \cdot s$
- On an inclined plane the normal force is the perpendicular component:
  $F_N = m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha$.
- $h$ is the gain in height, $s$ is the distance along the plane. **Do not mix
  them up.**
:::

::: sampunkt "How to solve it"
1. Calculate the potential energy from the gain in height $h$.
2. Calculate the normal force from the angle of the plane.
3. Calculate the frictional force $F_f = \mu \cdot F_N$.
4. Multiply by the distance $s$ along the plane and add.
:::
:::
