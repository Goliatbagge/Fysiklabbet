# Momentum and impulse

## Momentum

The product of mass and [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] is called **momentum**, written *p*, and
its [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] is kg · m/s (kilogram metres per second). Momentum can be seen as
a measure of how "hard to stop" an object is, and it is useful in collisions.
Momentum is a **[[vector|A quantity with both magnitude and direction, for example velocity, force or acceleration. Differs from a scalar, which only has magnitude.]]** with the same direction as the velocity.

::: härledning "Note: Momentum ≠ kinetic energy"
Do not confuse momentum with [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]! They have different units and different
definitions.
:::

::: formel "Momentum (definition)"
$$
p = m \cdot v
$$

where

- *m* = mass (kg)
- *v* = velocity (m/s)
- *p* = momentum (kg · m/s)
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Momentum and kinetic energy"
**An object with a mass of 48 kg has a velocity of 7.0 m/s to the left. We
define the positive direction as to the right. Determine its a) momentum,
b) kinetic energy.**

::: handskrift
typ: rorelsemangd
:::

::: textlosning
**a)**

$$
p = m \cdot v
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 48\ \mathrm{kg} \\
v = -7.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (\text{minus sign: the direction is to the left})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
p = 48 \cdot (-7.0) = -336\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx -340\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$

**Answer:** −340 kg · m/s

**b)**

$$
E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 48\ \mathrm{kg} \\
v = 7.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (\text{no minus sign: kinetic energy is not a vector})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E_k = \frac{48 \cdot 7.0^{2}}{2} = 1\,176\ \mathrm{J} \approx 1\,200\ \mathrm{J} = 1.2\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

**Answer:** 1.2 kJ
:::
:::

## Impulse

A [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] acting for a certain time is called an **impulse**, written *I*, and its
SI unit is Ns (newton second). An impulse **changes momentum**. Impulse is a
vector with the same direction as the force. According to [[Newton's third law|For every force (action) there is an equal and opposite force (reaction).]] about force and
reaction force, an impulse on one object will lead to an equal but
opposite impulse on another object.

::: formel "Impulse (definition)"
$$
I = F \cdot \Delta t
$$

where

- *I* = impulse (Ns)
- *F* = force (N)
- Δ*t* = time (s)
:::

The fact that an impulse gives a corresponding change in momentum is called the **impulse-momentum theorem**.

::: formel "The impulse-momentum theorem"
$$
I = \Delta p \qquad\text{or}\qquad I = m \cdot \Delta v
\qquad\text{or}\qquad I = m v_2 - m v_1
$$

where

- *I* = impulse (Ns)
- Δ*p* = change in momentum (kg · m/s)
- *m* = mass (kg)
- $v_2$ = velocity after (m/s)
- $v_1$ = velocity before (m/s)
- Δ*v* = change in velocity (m/s)

::: härledning "Derivation: The impulse-momentum theorem"
We set up the formula for impulse.

$$
I = F \cdot \Delta t
$$

According to [[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]] *F* = *m* · *a*. This gives

$$
I = m \cdot a \cdot \Delta t
$$

The definition of [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ gives

$$
I = \frac{m \cdot \Delta v \cdot \Delta t}{\Delta t}
= m \cdot \Delta v = \Delta p \qquad\text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

## Impulse and graphs

::: formel "Impulse in graphs"
In an ***F-t* graph** the impulse corresponds to **the area under the graph** (since
impulse = the product of force *F* and time *t*).

In a ***v-t* graph** the change in velocity gives the change in momentum,
which in turn corresponds to the impulse.
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: The world's hardest football kick"
**A football has a mass of 0.45 kg and is lying still. What average force is needed on
the ball for it to reach a [[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]] of 211 km/h if the contact time with the foot is 20 ms?
(The Brazilian Ronny Heberson achieved this feat.)**

::: handskrift
typ: fotbollsspark
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for impulse and solve for the force *F*.

$$
I = F \cdot \Delta t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F = \frac{I}{\Delta t}
$$

The impulse is found with the impulse-momentum theorem.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
I = m \cdot \Delta v = 0.45 \cdot 211\ \mathrm{km/h}
   = 0.45 \cdot \dfrac{211}{3.6}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 26.375\ \mathrm{Ns} \\
\Delta t = 20\ \mathrm{ms} = 0.020\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F = \frac{26.375}{0.020} = 1\,318.75\ \mathrm{N} \approx 1\,300\ \mathrm{N} = 1.3\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 1.3 kN
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Momentum"
- $p = m \cdot v$, with the unit kg · m/s.
- A measure of how **hard to stop** an object is.
- A **vector** with the same direction as the velocity, so the sign comes along.
:::

::: sampunkt "Do not confuse it with kinetic energy"
- $p = m \cdot v$ is a vector and can be negative.
- $E_k = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}$ is a scalar and is always substituted without
  a sign.
- They have completely different units: kg · m/s compared with J.
:::

::: sampunkt "Impulse"
- A force acting for a certain time: $I = F \cdot \Delta t$, with
  the unit Ns.
- A **vector** with the same direction as the force.
- According to Newton's third law, an impulse on one object gives an equal but
  opposite impulse on the other.
:::

::: sampunkt "The impulse-momentum theorem"
- **An impulse changes the momentum:**
  $I = \Delta p = m \cdot \Delta v = m v_2 - m v_1$.
- Derived from $I = F \Delta t$ with $F = ma$ and
  $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Impulse in graphs"
- In an ***F-t* graph** the impulse is **the area under the graph**.
- In a ***v-t* graph** the change in velocity gives the change in momentum,
  that is the impulse.
:::

::: sampunkt "Typical task"
- Finding the force from a short impact:
  $F = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{m \cdot \Delta v}{\Delta t}$.
- A short contact time gives a very large force. That is why a football kick
  lasting $20\ \mathrm{ms}$ gives more than $1\ \mathrm{kN}$.
:::
:::
