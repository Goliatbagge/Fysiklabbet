# Kinetic energy

The energy that an object has because of its motion is called **[[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]**
(energy of motion), written $E_k$, and like all forms of energy it has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] J (joule).
How large the kinetic energy is depends on the mass and
the [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]]. The larger the mass and the higher the velocity, the higher the kinetic energy.

::: formel "Kinetic energy"
$$
E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

where

- $E_k$ = kinetic energy (J)
- *m* = mass of the object (kg)
- *v* = velocity of the object (m/s)

::: härledning "Derivation: Kinetic energy"
The increase in kinetic energy is as large as the [[work|Energy transferred when a force acts along a distance: *W* = *F* · *s* · cos *α*. SI unit joule (J).]] that was needed to produce
the motion, so

$$
E_k = W = F_s \cdot s \qquad (1)
$$

Since we want to increase a velocity, a certain accelerating [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] is needed. According to
[[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]] this force is

$$
F_s = m \cdot a \qquad (2)
$$

The [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]] *s* at [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] we have calculated earlier as

$$
s = \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2} \quad (\text{if } v_0 = 0) \qquad (3)
$$

Substituting (2) and (3) into (1) gives

$$
E_k = F_s \cdot s = m \cdot a \cdot \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
= \frac{m \cdot a^{2} \cdot t^{2}}{2}
= \frac{m \cdot (a \cdot t)^{2}}{2} \qquad (4)
$$

At constant acceleration *v* = *a* · *t*. This means that
*v*² = (*a* · *t*)². Substituting (*a* · *t*)² = *v*² into (4) gives

$$
E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2} \qquad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: The kinetic energy of a car"
**A car has a mass of 1.2 tonnes and a velocity of 72 km/h. How large is its
kinetic energy?**

::: handskrift
typ: bilenergi
:::

::: textlosning
$$
E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 1.2\ \mathrm{tonnes} = 1\,200\ \mathrm{kg} \\
v = 72\ \mathrm{km/h} = \dfrac{72}{3.6}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 20\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E_k = \frac{1\,200 \cdot 20^{2}}{2} = 240\,000\ \mathrm{J} = 0.24\ \mathrm{MJ}
$$

**Answer:** 0.24 MJ
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: A braking cyclist"
**A cyclist with bicycle has a mass of 85 kg and brakes on level ground from 10 m/s to
7.0 m/s over 30 metres. How large was the average braking force?**

::: handskrift
typ: bromscyklist
:::

::: textlosning
The braking work *W* is equal to the decrease in kinetic energy,
that is, the kinetic energy before minus the kinetic energy after:

$$
W = E_{k\text{before}} - E_{k\text{after}}
$$

Substituting $W = F_s \cdot s$ gives

$$
F_s \cdot s = E_{k\text{before}} - E_{k\text{after}}
$$

We solve for the average braking force $F_s$.

$$
F_s = \frac{E_{k\text{before}} - E_{k\text{after}}}{s}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
E_{k\text{before}} = \dfrac{m \cdot v_1^{2}}{2} = \dfrac{85 \cdot 10^{2}}{2} = 4\,250\ \mathrm{J} \\
E_{k\text{after}} = \dfrac{m \cdot v_2^{2}}{2} = \dfrac{85 \cdot 7.0^{2}}{2} = 2\,082.5\ \mathrm{J} \\
s = 30\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_s = \frac{4\,250 - 2\,082.5}{30} = 72.25\ \mathrm{N} \approx 72\ \mathrm{N}
$$

**Answer:** 72 N
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Kinetic energy"
- The energy an object has because of its motion. Also called
  **energy of motion**. The SI unit is J.
- $E_k = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}$
- Larger mass and higher velocity give more kinetic energy.
:::

::: sampunkt "The velocity is squared"
- Double the velocity gives **four times** as much kinetic energy.
- Double the mass only gives twice as much.
- That is why the braking distance grows so quickly with speed.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the formula comes from"
- The kinetic energy is as large as the work that was needed:
  $E_k = F_s \cdot s$.
- Substitute $F_s = m \cdot a$ and
  $s = \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}$.
- Use $v = a \cdot t$, and you get
  $E_k = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Work and kinetic energy"
- $W = \Delta E_k$, so the work is equal to the **change** in
  kinetic energy.
- Braking force from a braking manoeuvre:
  $F_s = \dfrac{E_{k\text{before}} - E_{k\text{after}}}{s}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Things to remember"
- Always convert the velocity to m/s and the mass to kg before
  substituting.
- Square the velocity, not the whole product.
:::
:::
