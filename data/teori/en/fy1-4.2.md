# Gravitational potential energy

Energy that cannot be seen but is "stored" in objects, as in a compressed coil spring
or in a stretched rubber band, is called **[[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]]**. Energy that is stored
because of increased height is called **gravitational potential energy**. Gravitational potential energy is a type of
potential energy, written $E_p$, and like all forms of energy it has the
[[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] J (joule).

Gravitational potential energy therefore depends only on changes in height (the vertical direction or
the *y*-direction). The height is decided by where you put the **zero level**. The zero level is a
reference point where the potential energy is set to 0. It can be put anywhere, but
is usually put "at the bottom" of the system (for example the floor or the ground). If an
object is below the zero level, it can therefore get a negative value.
Energy in a real sense, however, can never be negative.

It is **the position of the centre of gravity** that gives the height above the zero level. For example,
a book standing on its end has more potential energy than if it is lying flat.

Lifting an object a certain [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]] requires a [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]]. We then do
[[work|Energy transferred when a force acts along a distance: *W* = *F* · *s* · cos *α*. SI unit joule (J).]], since work = force · distance. It is this work that leads to
an increase in the potential energy.

::: formel "Gravitational potential energy"
$$
E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h
$$

where

- $E_p$ = gravitational potential energy (J)
- *m* = mass of the object (kg)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg) (9.82 N/kg in Sweden)
- *h* = height above the zero level (m)

::: härledning "Derivation: Gravitational potential energy"
The potential energy is given by the work we do when we lift an object, so

$$
E_p = W = F_s \cdot s
$$

The force we must apply corresponds to the weight of the object, so $F_s$ = *m* · *g*.
The distance we lift the object gives a certain height, so *s* = *h*. Substituting gives

$$
E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h \qquad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

::: formel "Gravitational potential energy and work"
If a change in height takes place **without [[friction|The force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact.]]**, the work *W* corresponds to the change in
potential energy Δ$E_p$.

$$
W = \Delta E_p
$$
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Cart up an inclined plane"
**You pull a cart 5.0 m up an inclined plane with an angle of inclination of 30°, so
that it ends up 2.5 m above its starting height. The cart has a mass of 32 kg. How much work
do you do if the cart rolls without friction?**

::: handskrift
typ: vagnplan
:::

::: textlosning
**Think:** Into which form or forms of energy is the work converted? Answer:
Only into potential energy $E_p$ (the cart rolling without friction means that
we get no frictional heat). The work *W* therefore gives the increase in
potential energy.

$$
W = E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 32\ \mathrm{kg} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \\
h = 2.5\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
W = 32 \cdot 9.82 \cdot 2.5 = 785.6\ \mathrm{Nm}
\approx 790\ \mathrm{Nm} = 0.79\ \mathrm{kNm}
$$

**Answer:** 0.79 kJ
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Potential energy and gravitational potential energy"
- **Potential energy** is stored energy, for example in a compressed
  spring or a stretched rubber band.
- **Gravitational potential energy** $E_p$ is the potential energy stored through increased
  height. The SI unit is J.
:::

::: sampunkt "The formula"
- $E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h$
- $m$ is the mass (kg), $g$ the gravitational field strength
  ($9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg}$ in Sweden) and $h$ the height above the zero level (m).
- Derived from the work: the force is the weight $m \cdot g$ and the distance is
  the height $h$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The zero level"
- The **zero level** is the reference height where the potential energy is set to 0.
- It can be chosen freely, but is usually put at the bottom of the system.
- Below the zero level $E_p$ becomes negative in the calculation. Real energy can
  never be negative.
:::

::: sampunkt "The height is measured to the centre of gravity"
- It is the position of the **centre of gravity** above the zero level that counts.
- A book on its end therefore has more potential energy than the same book lying down.
:::

::: sampunkt "Work and potential energy"
- If the change in height takes place **without friction**,
  $W = \Delta E_p$.
- Pulling a cart without friction up an inclined plane: only
  the **difference in height** matters, not how long the plane is.
:::
:::
