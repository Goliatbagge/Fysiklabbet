# Calculating quantities with integrals

In many scientific applications we use integrals to add up
changes over time or distance. If a quantity, for example velocity
or power, varies, we cannot just use simple multiplication.
Instead we calculate the "area under the graph" using an integral.

The basic idea is that **the integral of a rate of change gives the accumulated
quantity**. The integral of the velocity gives the distance, the integral of the power gives
the energy, and so on. You can always tell which quantity the integral stands for from
the units.

::: formel "The unit of the integral"
The unit of the integral is always the product of the units of the axes:

$$
\text{unit of the } y\text{-axis} \cdot \text{unit of the } x\text{-axis} = \text{unit of the integral}
$$

Example: a velocity in m/s integrated over the time in s gives a distance in m,

$$
\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}
$$
:::

Below, four common applications are collected. In all cases the quantity we
are looking for is the integral of a rate of change: the velocity $v(t)$ is
change in position per time, the acceleration $a(t)$ is change in velocity per time,
the force $F(s)$ gives work per distance and the power $P(t)$ is energy per time.

::: formel "Four common applications where integrals are used"
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Change in position:} \qquad && \Delta s &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt\[4pt]
\text{Change in velocity:} \qquad && \Delta v &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt\[4pt]
\text{Work:} \qquad && W &= \int_{s_1}^{s_2} F(s)\,ds\[4pt]
\text{Energy consumption:} \qquad && E &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} P(t)\,dt
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Which quantity does the area stand for?"
**The graph above shows a flow where the $y$-axis has the unit litre/minute and
the $x$-axis the unit minute. Determine which quantity and unit the integral (the area)
stands for.**

::: handskrift
typ: integralenhet
:::

::: textlosning
The unit of the integral is the product of the units of the axes:

$$
\frac{\text{litre}}{\text{min}} \cdot \text{min} = \text{litre}
$$

So we get a volume expressed in litres.

**Answer:** Volume in litres.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Energy from power"
**The power in a resistor is given by the formula $P(t) = 3.31t^2$. How much energy
is converted during the first five seconds?**

::: handskrift
typ: energiureffekt
:::

::: textlosning
Energy is calculated as power multiplied by time. Since the power varies,
the converted energy is given by the integral of the power over time, between
$t = 0$ and $t = 5$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E = \int_0^5 P(t)\,dt = \int_0^5 3.31t^2\,dt &= \left[\frac{3.31t^3}{3}\right]_0^5 = \frac{3.31\cdot 5^3}{3} - \frac{3.31\cdot 0^3}{3}\[4pt]
&= 137.916\ldots \approx 138
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer:** $E \approx 138\ \mathrm{J}$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: Distance from velocity"
**A car is driving on a slip road onto a motorway. The driver keeps the speed
$15\ \mathrm{m/s}$ when she starts accelerating with constant acceleration
$a = 2.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ for 6 seconds to reach motorway speed. At
the moment the acceleration starts ($t = 0$) the car has travelled a total of
$400\ \mathrm{m}$ from its starting point.<br>a) Write the function $s(t)$ for
the car's position relative to the starting point.<br>b) How far
does the car travel during these 6 seconds of acceleration?**

::: handskrift
typ: strackaurfart
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We have the acceleration $a(t) = 2.5$ and are looking for the position $s(t)$. First we
find the velocity $v(t)$ as the integral of the acceleration with respect to time:

$$
v(t) = 2.5t + C
$$

The condition $v(0) = 15$ gives $2.5\cdot 0 + C = 15$, so $C = 15$ and

$$
v(t) = 2.5t + 15
$$

Then we find the distance $s(t)$ as the integral of the velocity:

$$
s(t) = \frac{2.5t^2}{2} + 15t + D
$$

The condition $s(0) = 400$ gives $D = 400$, so

$$
s(t) = \frac{2.5t^2}{2} + 15t + 400
$$

**b)** We substitute $t = 6$ into the function for the distance:

$$
s(6) = \frac{2.5\cdot 6^2}{2} + 15\cdot 6 + 400 = 45 + 90 + 400 = 535\ \mathrm{m}
$$

Since the car had travelled $400\ \mathrm{m}$ before the acceleration, the
distance during the acceleration is $535 - 400 = 135\ \mathrm{m}$.

Alternatively, we get the displacement directly as the integral of the velocity between
$t = 0$ and $t = 6$:

$$
\int_0^6 (2.5t + 15)\,dt = \left[\frac{2.5t^2}{2} + 15t\right]_0^6 = 45 + 90 - 0 = 135\ \mathrm{m}
$$

**Answer:** a) $s(t) = \dfrac{2.5t^2}{2} + 15t + 400$ &emsp; b) $135\ \mathrm{m}$
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The basic idea"
- The integral of a **rate of change** gives the accumulated quantity.
- If the quantity varies, you cannot just multiply. The area under the graph must be calculated.
:::

::: sampunkt "The unit reveals the quantity"
- The unit of the integral is the unit of the $y$-axis times the unit of the $x$-axis.
- $\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m}$, so velocity integrated over time gives a distance.
- Always do the unit analysis to know what the answer means.
:::

::: sampunkt "Four applications"
- Change in position: $\Delta s = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt$
- Change in velocity: $\Delta v = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt$
- Work: $W = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} F(s)\,ds$
- Energy consumption: $E = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} P(t)\,dt$
:::

::: sampunkt "Two routes to the answer"
- **With a condition:** find the antiderivative with $C$, find $C$ from the starting value and substitute the time.
- **With a definite integral:** integrate directly between the two times. Then no $C$ is needed.
- The definite integral gives the **change**, not the absolute value.
:::

:::
