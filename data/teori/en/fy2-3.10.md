# AC generator

A [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] whose size and polarity (direction) vary periodically is called an
**alternating voltage**. A source that produces an alternating voltage is called an
**AC generator** (alternating current generator).

We look at the principle of a simple AC generator. We let a loop
rotate in a [[magnetic field|Region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where other magnetic objects are acted on by a force.]] at [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]].

A loop rotates with constant angular velocity *ω* in the magnetic field *B* between the north end (red) and the south end (white) of the magnet. Slip rings and brushes carry the voltage to a voltmeter, and the voltage *u* varies sinusoidally with time *t*.
:::

Since the flux through the loop is constantly changing (as the perpendicular area
changes), a voltage is induced in it, according to the law of induction
$e = -\dfrac{d\Phi}{dt}$. Using the right-hand rule we can also see that
the [[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]] (and the voltage) will change direction during the rotation.

Values of current or voltage at a particular time in an AC circuit
are called **instantaneous values** and are written with lower-case letters: *u* for
instantaneous voltage and *i* for instantaneous current.

The maximum instantaneous values of current or voltage in an AC circuit
are called **peak values** and are written with ^ (circumflex): *û* for
peak voltage and *î* for peak current. *û* and *î* are read "u-peak" and
"i-peak".

::: formel "Instantaneous voltage and instantaneous current in an AC generator"
$$
u = \hat{u} \cdot \sin(\omega t)
$$

$$
i = \hat{\imath} \cdot \sin(\omega t)
$$

where

- *u* = instantaneous voltage (V)
- *û* = peak voltage (V)
- *i* = instantaneous current (A)
- *î* = peak current (A)
- *ω* = angular velocity (rad/s)
- *t* = time (s)

The [[magnetic flux|The amount of magnetic field passing through a surface: *Φ* = *B* · *A* · cos *α*. SI unit weber (Wb).]] *Φ* is at its maximum when the loop is horizontal and
perpendicular to the flux lines, because the area *A* facing the flux lines is then
at its maximum. The perpendicular area $A_\perp$ depends on the angle *α* through which it has been turned
from the horizontal plane according to

$$
\Phi = B \cdot A_\perp = B \cdot A \cdot \cos\alpha \qquad (1)
$$

From before we have

$$
\omega = \frac{\alpha}{t} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \alpha = \omega t
$$

*α* = *ωt* in (1) gives

$$
\Phi = B \cdot A \cdot \cos(\omega t)
$$

or with the flux written as a function of time

$$
\Phi(t) = B \cdot A \cdot \cos(\omega t)
$$

According to the law of induction the induced voltage at a particular time is

$$
e = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = -\Phi'(t)
$$

From before we have $\Phi(t) = B \cdot A \cdot \cos(\omega t)$. We
differentiate the function and get

$$
e = -\Phi'(t) = -\bigl(-B \cdot A \cdot \omega \cdot \sin(\omega t)\bigr) = B \cdot A \cdot \omega \cdot \sin(\omega t) \qquad (2)
$$

Since $-1 \le \sin(\omega t) \le 1$, the maximum voltage *ê* is obtained when
$\sin(\omega t) = 1$, so

$$
\hat{e} = B \cdot A \cdot \omega
$$

Substituting $\hat{e} = B \cdot A \cdot \omega$ into (2) gives

$$
e = \hat{e} \cdot \sin(\omega t)
$$

So the alternating voltage *u* at a particular time in a generator can be written

$$
u = \hat{u} \cdot \sin(\omega t) \quad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$

Current and voltage have the same phase angles (when the voltage is at its maximum the
current is also at its maximum) according to [[Ohm's law|The relationship between voltage, current and resistance: *U* = *R* · *I*.]] $U = R \cdot I$, so the instantaneous current *i*
can be written

$$
i = \hat{\imath} \cdot \sin(\omega t) \quad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

Instead of an angular velocity, alternating currents are often given with a [[frequency|Number of oscillations per second. SI unit hertz (Hz) = 1/s.]]
expressed in Hz, which states how many times the current changes direction each
second. Remember the relationship between angular velocity and frequency, and between angular velocity and
[[period|The time for one complete oscillation or one complete wave: *T* = 1 / *f*.]].

::: formel "Angular velocity"
$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi \cdot f
$$

where

- *ω* = angular velocity (rad/s)
- *T* = period (s)
- *f* = frequency (Hz)
:::

With alternating current the voltage and current vary. They can be given an
average value called the **effective value** (RMS value), which is written with capital letters:
*U* for voltage and *I* for current.

::: formel "Effective values for alternating current"
$$
U = \frac{\hat{u}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

$$
I = \frac{\hat{\imath}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

where

- *U* = effective voltage (V)
- *û* = peak voltage (V)
- *I* = effective current (A)
- *î* = peak current (A)
:::

::: kuriosa "The Swedish mains"
The Swedish mains supply has a mains voltage (effective voltage) of **230 V** and
a frequency of **50 Hz**.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: The American mains"
**The USA has a mains voltage of 110 V with a frequency of 60 Hz. Write an expression for
how the voltage varies with time in an American mains supply.**

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for instantaneous voltage and then need to determine
the peak voltage *û* and the angular velocity *ω*.

$$
u = \hat{u} \cdot \sin(\omega t) \qquad (1)
$$

We start by determining the peak voltage *û*.

$$
U = \frac{\hat{u}}{\sqrt{2}} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \hat{u} = \sqrt{2} \cdot U
$$

Substituting *U* = 110 V gives

$$
\hat{u} = \sqrt{2} \cdot 110 = 155.563\ldots\ \mathrm{V} \approx 156\ \mathrm{V}
$$

We then determine the angular velocity *ω*.

$$
\omega = 2\pi \cdot f
$$

Substituting *f* = 60 Hz gives

$$
\omega = 2\pi \cdot 60 = 120\pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}
$$

Substituting $\hat{u} = 156$ V and $\omega = 120\pi$ rad/s into (1) gives

$$
u = 156 \cdot \sin(120\pi \cdot t)
$$

**Answer:** $u = 156 \cdot \sin(120\pi \cdot t)$
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "AC generator"
- A loop rotates at constant speed in a magnetic field.
- The perpendicular area changes all the time, so the flux varies and a
  voltage is induced.
- The voltage and the current **change direction** every half turn. This gives an
  **alternating voltage**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Instantaneous values and peak values"
- **Instantaneous values** are written with lower-case letters: $u$ and $i$.
- **Peak values** are written with a circumflex: $\hat{u}$ and $\hat{\imath}$,
  read "u-peak" and "i-peak".
- $u = \hat{u} \sin(\omega t)$ and
  $i = \hat{\imath} \sin(\omega t)$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the sine shape comes from"
- The flux is $\Phi(t) = B A \cos(\omega t)$.
- The law of induction gives $e = -\Phi'(t)$, and the derivative of cosine is minus
  sine.
- So $e = BA\omega \sin(\omega t)$, with the peak value
  $\hat{e} = B A \omega$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Effective values"
- The average value that corresponds to a direct voltage with the same power. Written with
  **capital** letters.
- $U = \dfrac{\hat{u}}{\sqrt{2}}$ and
  $I = \dfrac{\hat{\imath}}{\sqrt{2}}$.
- It is the effective value that is meant when we say that the mains voltage is
  $230\ \mathrm{V}$. So the peak voltage is just over
  $325\ \mathrm{V}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Angular velocity and frequency"
- $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$
- Swedish mains: $230\ \mathrm{V}$ and
  $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
- American: $110\ \mathrm{V}$ and
  $60\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
:::
:::
