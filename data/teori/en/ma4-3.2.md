# Calculating areas with integrals

In Mathematics continuation level 1c you met the integral as a way of measuring the **area under a
curve**. We review that interpretation and then calculate areas using
the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Graphically, the area between the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ and the $x$-axis,
from $x = a$ to $x = b$, corresponds to the integral below.

::: formel "The integral as an area"
$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx
$$
:::

In the expression the different symbols mean:

- $\int$ — the integral sign (a summation sign for infinitely many terms),
- $a$ — the lower limit of integration (graphically the $x$-value where the area starts),
- $b$ — the upper limit of integration (graphically the $x$-value where the area ends),
- $f(x)$ — the integrand (graphically the function we take the area under),
- $dx$ — the variable of integration (graphically the infinitely narrow width of a
  rectangle).

## Areas below the *x*-axis

An integral can have a **negative** value. This happens when the graph lies below
the $x$-axis in the interval. An area, on the other hand, can never be negative. For the area
$A$ we therefore have:

::: formel "Area above and below the *x*-axis"
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_a^b f(x)\,dx && \text{if } f(x) > 0 \text{ (the graph lies above the } x\text{-axis)}\[4pt]
A &= -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx && \text{if } f(x) < 0 \text{ (the graph lies below the } x\text{-axis)}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

If the region lies partly above and partly below the $x$-axis, we have to split
the calculation into several intervals, one for each side (see Example 2).

## The fundamental theorem of calculus

To calculate an integral we use an **antiderivative** $F(x)$ of
$f(x)$ (that is, a function whose derivative is $f$). The fundamental theorem says that
the value of the integral is the difference between the values of the antiderivative at the two
limits.

::: formel "The fundamental theorem of calculus"
$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b) - F(a)
$$
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Calculate two integrals"
**Calculate<br>a)&nbsp;$\displaystyle\int_1^4 \frac{3x^2 + 5}{x}\,dx$&emsp;&emsp;b)&nbsp;$\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/4} \cos 2x\,dx$**

::: handskrift
typ: integralvarden
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We write the rational expression as a sum and simplify:

$$
\int_1^4 \frac{3x^2 + 5}{x}\,dx = \int_1^4 \left(\frac{3x^2}{x} + \frac{5}{x}\right)dx = \int_1^4 \left(3x + \frac{5}{x}\right)dx
$$

We integrate term by term and substitute the limits:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_1^4 \left(3x + \frac{5}{x}\right)dx &= \left[\frac{3x^2}{2} + 5\ln x\right]_1^4\[4pt]
&= \frac{3\cdot 4^2}{2} + 5\ln 4 - \left(\frac{3\cdot 1^2}{2} + 5\ln 1\right)\[4pt]
&= \frac{48}{2} + 5\ln 4 - \left(\frac{3}{2} + 0\right) = \frac{45}{2} + 5\ln 4
\end{aligned}
$$

**b)** Here an antiderivative of $\cos 2x$ is $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{2}$:

$$
\int_0^{\pi/4} \cos 2x\,dx = \left[\frac{\sin 2x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/4} = \frac{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}}{2} - \frac{\sin 0}{2} = \frac{1}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}
$$

**Answer:** a) $\dfrac{45}{2} + 5\ln 4$ &emsp; b) $\dfrac{1}{2}$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Area of a shaded region"
**The curve $y = x^3 - 4x$ and the $x$-axis enclose a region. Calculate the area of
the shaded region between $x = -1$ and $x = 2$.**

::: handskrift
typ: areaskuggad
:::

::: textlosning
The graph lies **above** the $x$-axis between $x = -1$ and $x = 0$, and **below**
the $x$-axis between $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. We therefore split the integral into two
intervals, where the area for $0 < x < 2$ corresponds to the negative integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - 4x)\,dx - \int_{0}^{2} (x^3 - 4x)\,dx\[4pt]
&= \left[\frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{4x^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^{0} - \left[\frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{4x^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{2}\[4pt]
&= \left[0 - \left(\frac{1}{4} - 2\right)\right] - \left[\left(4 - 8\right) - 0\right]\[4pt]
&= \left[-\left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{8}{4}\right)\right] - \left[-4\right] = \frac{7}{4} + 4 = \frac{7}{4} + \frac{16}{4} = \frac{23}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer:** $\dfrac{23}{4}$ a.u.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The integral as an area"
- $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the area between the graph and the $x$-axis from $x = a$ to $x = b$.
- $a$ and $b$ are the limits of integration, $f(x)$ is the integrand and $dx$ the variable of integration.
- The integral sign is a summation sign for infinitely many infinitely narrow rectangles.
:::

::: sampunkt "The fundamental theorem"
- $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b) - F(a)$.
- First the upper limit is substituted, then the lower one is subtracted.
- Put **brackets** around the whole expression at the lower limit, otherwise the signs go wrong.
:::

::: sampunkt "Regions below the axis"
- If the graph lies below the $x$-axis, the integral is **negative**.
- The area is then $A = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, since an area can never be negative.
- If the region lies partly above and partly below, the calculation must be **split** at the zero.
:::

::: sampunkt "Before integrating"
- Simplify the integrand first: $\dfrac{3x^2 + 5}{x} = 3x + \dfrac{5}{x}$.
- Remember $\ln 1 = 0$ and $\sin 0 = 0$, which often make the lower limit zero.
- Areas are given in area units, a.u. A plain integral value is given without a unit.
:::

:::
