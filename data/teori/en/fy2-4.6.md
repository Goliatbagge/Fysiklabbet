# Wave-particle duality and the de Broglie wavelength

As we know, light can be seen as both a particle and a wave. The Frenchman **Louis de
Broglie** (1920s) assumed that this could also apply to other particles.
**All particles**, not just [[photons|A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Carries the energy *E* = *h* · *f*, where *h* is Planck's constant.]], have wave properties. The [[wavelength|The distance between two wave crests (or other identical points) in a wave. Denoted *λ*. SI unit metre (m).]] that
a particle has is called the **de Broglie wavelength**.

::: formel "de Broglie wavelength"
$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{m \cdot v}
$$

where

- *λ* = wavelength of the particle (m)
- *h* = [[Planck's constant|The physical constant *h* ≈ 6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s. Links the energy of a photon to its frequency.]] ($6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}$ Js)
- *m* = mass of the particle (kg)
- *v* = [[velocity|Displacement per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of the particle (m/s)

::: härledning "Derivation: de Broglie wavelength"
The momentum of the photon from earlier theory is

$$
p = \frac{h \cdot f}{c} \qquad (1)
$$

From earlier theory we also have $c = f \cdot \lambda$, and putting this into (1) gives

$$
p = \frac{h \cdot f}{f \cdot \lambda} = \frac{h}{\lambda} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \qquad (2)
$$

From classical physics we have the momentum $p = m \cdot v$. Substituting
this into (2) gives

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{m \cdot v} \quad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

The formula means that **all moving particles have a certain wavelength**.
For the wave properties to be noticeable, the wavelength must be of the same
order of magnitude as the size of the particle, which is the case for small particles
such as **electrons, protons and neutrons**.

The phenomenon was confirmed by experiments with electrons ten years later!
Electrons were fired through a double slit. Behind the double slit was a screen
that recorded where the electron struck. If electrons only had
particle properties, they would go through either the left slit or
the right slit and form a pattern with two lines on the screen.

We see that an **interference pattern** forms, with lighter and darker
bands, exactly as if the electron were a wave!

If you send **one electron at a time** through the openings, the same
interference pattern still forms! So it is not the case that the electrons
"push each other aside". The electron interferes **with itself**
and seems to go through both openings at the same time, that is, like a wave.

Matter made up of elementary particles therefore shows both particle
and wave properties. This dual nature is called
**wave-particle duality**.

::: tips "Wave-particle duality"
Elementary particles and atoms behave as

- **particles**, for example when they collide in a gas.
- **waves** in double-slit experiments, just like light!
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: de Broglie wavelength of an electron"
**Determine the de Broglie wavelength of an electron that is accelerated from rest
by a [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] of 200 V.**

::: handskrift
typ: debroglie
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for the de Broglie wavelength

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{m \cdot v} \qquad (1)
$$

The speed of the electron is calculated from the conversion of electrical energy
into [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]

$$
E_\mathrm{e} = E_\mathrm{k} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Q \cdot U = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot Q \cdot U}{m}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
h \approx 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{Js} \\
m \approx 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot Q \cdot U}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C} \cdot 200\ \mathrm{V}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}}} = 8.386\ldots \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

Substituting into (1) gives

$$
\lambda = \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 8.386\ldots \cdot 10^{6}} = 8.672\ldots \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} \approx 8.7 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} = 87\ \mathrm{pm}
$$

**Answer:** 87 pm
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Wave properties of a tennis ball?"
**You hit a tennis ball so that it gets a [[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]] of 108 km/h. A tennis ball has
a diameter of 6.7 cm and weighs 58 grams. Will it show
wave properties?**

::: handskrift
typ: tennisboll
:::

::: textlosning
For noticeable wave properties, the de Broglie wavelength must be of the same
order of magnitude as (or larger than) the particle. The "particle" in this case is
the tennis ball. For the tennis ball to show wave properties, it must
therefore have a de Broglie wavelength of at least 6.7 cm.

We set up the formula for the de Broglie wavelength.

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{m \cdot v}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
h \approx 6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{Js} \\
m = 58\ \mathrm{g} = 0.058\ \mathrm{kg} \\
v = 108\ \mathrm{km/h} = \tfrac{108}{3.6}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 30\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}}{0.058 \cdot 30} = 3.808\ldots \cdot 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

This is VERY much smaller than the tennis ball's diameter of 6.7 cm.

**Answer:** No, the tennis ball will not show wave properties.
:::
:::

We see in the example above that for **macroscopic objects** (objects visible to
the naked eye) quantum effects are completely negligible. That is why classical
mechanics works so well for balls, cars and bananas, but not for photons and
electrons.

::: kuriosa "The Copenhagen interpretation"
According to **Heisenberg's uncertainty principle** in quantum mechanics, it is impossible
to know a particle's exact position and momentum (or
velocity) at the same time. This is because, to measure a particle's position,
you have to use a method that affects its momentum, and vice versa.
If, for example, you send a photon at an electron to measure its
position, the photon will affect the electron's momentum and change
its velocity.

The more precisely we know one of the two properties, the less precisely we can
know the other. To describe where an electron is, we must
instead use a mathematical **wave function** (determined by
the **Schrödinger equation**, a differential equation) that represents
the **probability** of a particle being at a certain position.

When you measure a particle's position or velocity, the wave function
"collapses" to a specific position or velocity. This means
that the particle **did not have a definite position or velocity before
the measurement**, but that the measurement itself affects the particle and its
behaviour. It also means that there is no "objective
reality" at the quantum level, only possibilities and probabilities.

All of the above is part of the **Copenhagen interpretation**, which got its name
from **Niels Bohr**, who worked in Copenhagen in the early 20th century. Both
the Schrödinger equation and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle are mathematical
constructions. However, they do not give a mental picture of what "really"
happens.
:::

::: kuriosa "The many-worlds hypothesis (the multiverse)"
The **many-worlds hypothesis** is an alternative interpretation of quantum mechanics that
stands in contrast to the Copenhagen interpretation. It proposes that instead
of the wave function collapsing to a single position or velocity
on measurement, **the universe splits** into several parallel realities,
each with a unique version of the particle.

It also means that **everything that is possible at the quantum level actually happens
in parallel universes**. If, for example, it is possible for a particle
to be in two different positions at the same time, there will be two
parallel universes, one for each position.

Very small changes at the beginning of the universe, for example a
tiny increase in the [[initial velocity|The velocity at the time *t* = 0 (when the motion starts). Denoted *v*₀.]] at the Big Bang, would make it impossible
for [[gravity|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. Gravity gives an object its weight and is directed straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] to pull together local "islands" of matter to form
stars and galaxies. Our universe with stars, planets and life is
therefore incredibly improbable. The many-worlds hypothesis could then explain
not only that such an improbable universe can exist, but also that
it **must** exist, since all combinations and quantum events exist.

There could therefore be incredibly many more universes in a
**multiverse**, where an overwhelming majority contain no stars,
planets or life at all. **Stephen Hawking** supports this hypothesis, which
however causes debate among researchers. They disagree about whether it can even be called a
scientific hypothesis, since it is **not falsifiable** and therefore cannot
be tested. Some even call it pseudoscience.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Wave-particle duality"
- Light behaves **both as a wave and as a particle**.
- **Louis de Broglie** assumed that the same applies to **all** particles, not
  just photons.
:::

::: sampunkt "de Broglie wavelength"
- $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{m v}$
- Derived from $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$ with the classical momentum
  $p = mv$.
- **All moving particles have a wavelength.**
:::

::: sampunkt "Why we do not notice it"
- The wave properties only show when the wavelength is of the same order of magnitude as
  the particle itself.
- The mass is in the **denominator**, so large objects get an insignificantly small
  wavelength.
- For **electrons, protons and neutrons**, on the other hand, it is fully
  measurable.
:::

::: sampunkt "The double-slit experiment"
- Electrons were fired one at a time at a double slit.
- If they were only particles, the screen would show **two lines**.
- Instead an **interference pattern** built up, as for a wave.
- The hypothesis was thus confirmed experimentally about ten years after it
  was put forward.
:::
:::
