# The Pythagorean identity

The sum of "the square of the sine of an angle" and "the square of the cosine of
the same angle" is always equal to 1. This relationship is called the **Pythagorean
identity** (in Swedish *trigonometriska ettan*, "the trigonometric one") and holds for every angle $v$.

Squares of trigonometric functions, for example $(\sin v)^2$, are written
$\sin^2 v$ to keep the number of brackets down. So it means the same
thing: $\sin^2 v = (\sin v)^2$.

::: formel "The Pythagorean identity"
For all angles $v$

$$
\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v = 1
$$
:::

## Proof using the unit circle

We draw a unit circle (radius 1) and an arbitrary angle $v$. The radius
to the point on the circle forms a right-angled triangle together with its
components. The point on the circle has the coordinates $(\cos v,\ \sin v)$, so
the triangle's horizontal leg is $\cos v$ and its vertical leg is $\sin v$.
The hypotenuse is the radius, which is 1.

Using Pythagoras' theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$) for the right-angled triangle we get

$$
(\sin v)^2 + (\cos v)^2 = 1^2
$$

$$
\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v = 1
$$

which was to be proved. The proof also holds for angles over 90°, where the $x$-
and/or $y$-coordinate is negative: when the coordinates are squared in
Pythagoras' theorem, the terms are positive anyway.

::: formel "Rewriting the Pythagorean identity"
From the Pythagorean identity it follows directly that

$$
\sin^2 v = 1 - \cos^2 v
$$

$$
\cos^2 v = 1 - \sin^2 v
$$
:::

If the angle lies in a known quadrant, we can use the Pythagorean identity to
find one of $\sin v$ and $\cos v$ when the other is given. When we
take the square root we get two possible values ($\pm$), and the quadrant decides which
sign is correct. Remember the division: in the **first quadrant** both
$x$ and $y$ are positive, in the **second quadrant** $x$ is negative and $y$ positive,
in the **third quadrant** both are negative and in the **fourth quadrant** $x$ is
positive and $y$ negative.

::: exempel "Example 1: Find cos v from sin v"
**The angle $v$ lies in the second quadrant. Find $\cos v$ if $\sin v = 0.6$.**

::: handskrift
typ: trigettan
:::

::: textlosning
The Pythagorean identity gives

$$
\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v = 1
$$

$$
0.6^2 + \cos^2 v = 1
$$

$$
0.36 + \cos^2 v = 1 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \cos^2 v = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64
$$

$$
\cos v = \pm\sqrt{0.64} = \pm 0.8
$$

Since the angle $v$ lies in the second quadrant, $\cos v$ (the $x$-coordinate)
must be negative. So we choose the minus sign.

**Answer:** $\cos v = -0.8$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Show a trigonometric identity"
**Show that $\dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x} - \tan^2 x = 1$.**

::: handskrift
typ: trigidentitet
:::

::: textlosning
We start from the left-hand side (LHS) and write $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$,
so that $\tan^2 x = \dfrac{\sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}$. Then both terms get the same
denominator:

$$
\text{LHS} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} - \tan^2 x = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} - \frac{\sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1 - \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}
$$

By the Pythagorean identity $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$, so the numerator can
be replaced:

$$
\text{LHS} = \frac{\cos^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = 1 = \text{RHS}
$$

which was to be shown.

**Answer:** The identity is shown, $\dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x} - \tan^2 x = 1$.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The Pythagorean identity"
- $\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v = 1$ for **all** angles $v$.
- $\sin^2 v$ means $(\sin v)^2$. The notation saves brackets.
:::

::: sampunkt "The proof"
- On the unit circle the point has the coordinates $(\cos v,\ \sin v)$.
- The legs are $\cos v$ and $\sin v$, the hypotenuse is the radius 1.
- Pythagoras' theorem directly gives $\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v = 1^2$.
- The proof also holds for obtuse angles, since the squares are always positive.
:::

::: sampunkt "Rewritings"
- $\sin^2 v = 1 - \cos^2 v$
- $\cos^2 v = 1 - \sin^2 v$
- Use them to get the whole expression in **a single** trigonometric function.
:::

::: sampunkt "Choosing the sign"
- From $\cos^2 v = 0.64$ it follows that $\cos v = \pm 0.8$. Both signs must be considered.
- **The quadrant decides**: in the second quadrant the $x$-coordinate is negative, so $\cos v = -0.8$.
- For identities: start from one side, write $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ and substitute using the identity.
:::

:::
