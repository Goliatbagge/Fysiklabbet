# Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of an atomic nucleus is called the **nuclear mass**. To calculate the nuclear mass
you take the mass of the whole atom (the nuclide mass) and then subtract
the mass of the electrons (the electron mass).

::: formel "Nuclear mass"
$$
m_\text{nucleus} = m_\text{nuclide} - Z \cdot m_\text{electron}
$$

where

- $m_\text{nucleus}$ = mass of the atomic nucleus (u)
- $m_\text{nuclide}$ = nuclide mass/mass of the atom (u)
- *Z* = [[atomic number|The number of protons in an atomic nucleus. Determines which element it is. Denoted *Z*.]]/number of electrons
- $m_\text{electron}$ = mass of the electron (u)
:::

The simplest composite atomic nucleus consists of one proton and one neutron.
It is the nucleus of hydrogen-2, which is written $_1^2 \mathrm{H}$. We compare the mass of
this nucleus with the mass of its constituents.

We start by calculating the mass of the constituents (one proton and one
neutron). From the table we find their masses (we use 5 decimal places).

$$
m_\text{particles} = m_\text{proton} + m_\text{neutron}
= (1.00728 + 1.00866)\ \mathrm{u} = 2.01594\ \mathrm{u}
$$

We then calculate the mass of the composite nucleus (the deuteron) with
the formula for nuclear mass.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m_\text{nuclide} = 2.01410\ \mathrm{u}\ (\text{table}) \\
Z = 1 \\
m_\text{electron} = 0.00055\ \mathrm{u}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
m_\text{nucleus} = (2.01410 - 1 \cdot 0.00055)\ \mathrm{u} = 2.01355\ \mathrm{u}
$$

So the mass of one proton and one neutron is 2.01594 u, but when they are put together
into a nucleus the mass is 2.01355 u.

The mass of the composite nucleus is therefore **not** the sum of the proton's
and the neutron's masses, but **lower**. This means that the mass of the nucleus is
lower than the sum of the masses of its constituents!

The decrease in mass goes into **binding energy** that holds the nucleus
together ("mass is energy" according to Einstein's well-known formula $E = m c^2$).
The binding energy is released when nuclei decay.

The difference between the mass of the atomic nucleus and the sum of the masses of its individual
constituents is called the **[[mass defect|The difference between the mass of an atomic nucleus and the sum of the masses of its building blocks. Corresponds to the binding energy via *E* = *m* · *c*².]]**.

::: formel "Mass defect"
$$
\Delta m = m_\text{particles} - m_\text{nucleus}
$$

where

- $\Delta m$ = mass defect (kg or u)
- $m_\text{particles}$ = total mass of the individual particles (kg or u)
- $m_\text{nucleus}$ = mass of the composite nucleus (kg or u)
:::

::: formel "Binding energy"
$$
E = \Delta m \cdot c^2
$$

where

- *E* = binding energy (J)
- $\Delta m$ = mass defect (kg)
- *c* = the [[speed|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of light in a vacuum (≈ 2.998 · 10⁸ m/s)
:::

The energies of individual atoms and particles are very low. For the same
reason that the atomic mass unit u is used for small masses, we introduce
the unit **eV (electronvolt)** for small energies.

1 eV is the energy an electron gains when it passes through a [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] of 1 V.
That energy is $E = Q \cdot U = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1
= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$.

::: formel "Converting between electronvolts and joules"
- 1 eV = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J (the same numerical value as the charge of the electron)
- 1 J = $\dfrac{1}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}$ eV
:::

Sometimes it is convenient to convert directly from the atomic mass unit u to MeV,
for example when you want to convert mass defects into energy in a single step.

::: formel "Converting from atomic mass units to megaelectronvolts"
$$
1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.49\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

::: härledning "Derivation: Converting between u and MeV"
$$
1\ \mathrm{u} \approx 1.66 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

With $E = m \cdot c^2$:

$$
E \approx 1.66 \cdot 10^{-27} \cdot (2.998 \cdot 10^8)^2\ \mathrm{J}
= 1.492\ldots \cdot 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}
$$

Converting to eV:

$$
E = \frac{1.492\ldots \cdot 10^{-10}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}\ \mathrm{eV}
= 9.313\ldots \cdot 10^8\ \mathrm{eV}
\approx 931\ \mathrm{MeV} \qquad \text{q.e.d.}
$$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Energy released in a decay"
**A nucleus has a mass of 128.25449 u and decays into products with a
total mass of 128.13822 u. How much energy was released in the reaction?
Answer in MeV.**

::: handskrift
typ: sonderfallsenergi
:::

::: textlosning
We start by calculating the mass defect $\Delta m$, that is the difference in mass.

$$
\Delta m = (128.25449 - 128.13822)\ \mathrm{u} = 0.11627\ \mathrm{u}
$$

We multiply the mass defect by 931.49 to get the released energy
*E* in MeV.

$$
E = 0.11627 \cdot 931.49 = 108.304\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx 108.30\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

**Answer:** 108.30 MeV
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: The nucleus of hydrogen-2"
**The nucleus of hydrogen-2 consists of one proton and one neutron.**

**a) Determine the mass defect. Answer in kg and u.**
**b) Determine the binding energy of the nucleus. Answer in J and MeV.**
**c) How much energy is released when a deuteron decays?**
**d) What is the binding energy per nucleon?**

::: handskrift
typ: bindningsenergi
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The masses of the proton, the neutron and the nuclide $_1^2 \mathrm{H}$ are found
in a table (we use them with five decimal places).

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m_\text{particles} = m_\text{proton} + m_\text{neutron}
   = (1.00728 + 1.00866)\ \mathrm{u} = 2.01594\ \mathrm{u} \\
m_\text{nucleus} = m_\text{nuclide} - Z \cdot m_\text{electron}
   = (2.01410 - 1 \cdot 0.00055)\ \mathrm{u} = 2.01355\ \mathrm{u}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\Delta m = 2.01594 - 2.01355 = 0.00239\ \mathrm{u}
$$

With $1\ \mathrm{u} \approx 1.66 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$:

$$
\Delta m = 0.00239 \cdot 1.66 \cdot 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}
= 3.9674 \cdot 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg}
\approx 3.97 \cdot 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

**Answer:** 0.00239 u or 3.97 · 10⁻³⁰ kg

**b)** The mass defect corresponds to the binding energy.

$$
E = \Delta m \cdot c^2
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\Delta m = 3.9674 \cdot 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg} \\
c \approx 2.998 \cdot 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E = 3.9674 \cdot 10^{-30} \cdot (2.998 \cdot 10^8)^2
= 3.5659\ldots \cdot 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}
$$

We convert to electronvolts by dividing by the elementary charge.

$$
E = \frac{3.5659\ldots \cdot 10^{-13}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}}
= 2.225\ldots \cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{eV} \approx 2.23\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

Alternatively, we could have obtained the binding energy directly from the mass defect
expressed in u, using the conversion factor 931.49:

$$
E = 0.00239\ \mathrm{u} \cdot 931.49 = 2.226\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV}
\approx 2.23\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

**Answer:** 3.57 · 10⁻¹³ J or 2.23 MeV

**c)** The energy released is the same as the binding energy (the answer to
part b).

**Answer:** 2.23 MeV

**d)** The nucleus has two nucleons (one proton and one neutron). The binding energy
per nucleon is therefore obtained by dividing the total binding energy by 2.

$$
\frac{2.226\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV}}{2} = 1.113\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.11\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

**Answer:** 1.11 MeV/nucleon
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Nuclear mass"
- $m_\text{nucleus} = m_\text{nuclide} - Z \cdot m_\text{electron}$
- The nuclide mass in the table applies to the **whole atom**, so the mass of the electrons
  must be subtracted.
:::

::: sampunkt "Mass defect"
- $\Delta m = m_\text{particles} - m_\text{nucleus}$
- The mass of the nucleus is **less** than the sum of the masses of its building blocks.
- Hydrogen-2: the building blocks have a mass of $2.01594\ \mathrm{u}$ but the nucleus only
  $2.01355\ \mathrm{u}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Binding energy"
- The missing mass has become **binding energy** that holds the nucleus
  together.
- $E = \Delta m \cdot c^{2}$
- The energy is **released** when the nucleus decays.
- The **binding energy per nucleon** is obtained by dividing by the mass number
  $A$. It tells you how tightly bound the nucleus is.
:::

::: sampunkt "Electronvolt"
- $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ is the energy an electron gains from a voltage of
  $1\ \mathrm{V}$.
- $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, the same
  numerical value as the elementary charge.
:::

::: sampunkt "The shortcut from u to MeV"
- $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.49\ \mathrm{MeV}$
- Multiply the mass defect in u directly by 931.49 to get the energy in
  MeV, without the detour via kilograms and joules.
:::
:::
