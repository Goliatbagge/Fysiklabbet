# Torricelli's equation

::: härledning "Note: this section is not finished yet"
This section is under development. The content below is a first draft.
:::

So far, when we have solved problems with [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] (think of a car that
brakes, or a ball we throw straight up), we have used these two
formulas:

$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t
$$

$$
s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac{1}{2} a \cdot t^{2}
$$

They work really well. But you have probably noticed that we very often have to use
*both of them* to solve a single problem.

For example: "What [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]] *s* does a car need to brake from 20 m/s
to 0 m/s with an acceleration of −5 m/s²?"

If we look at formula 2 to find the distance *s*, we see that we need to know
the time *t*. We do not have it. So we first have to go to formula 1, solve for
the time *t*, and then put that value into formula 2. It works, but it is
two steps. It takes time, and there are more places to make careless mistakes.

What if there were a shortcut? An "elegant" formula that simply *skips
the time*?

That is exactly what we are going to look at today. We will derive and start using one
of the most powerful shortcuts in kinematics: **Torricelli's equation**.

::: formel "Torricelli's equation"
$$
v^{2} - v_0^{2} = 2 a s
$$

where

- *v* = final velocity (m/s)
- *v*₀ = [[initial velocity|The velocity at the time *t* = 0 (when the motion starts). Written *v*₀.]] (m/s)
- *a* = acceleration (m/s²)
- *s* = [[displacement|The vector from the start position to the end position. Differs from distance, which is the total length of path.]] (m)

::: härledning "Derivation"
Torricelli's equation, correctly written $v^{2} - v_0^{2} = 2as$, is derived
directly from the other equations of motion ($v = v_0 + at$ and
$s = v_0 t + \dfrac{1}{2} a t^{2}$) by eliminating the time *t*. Since
those equations themselves require constant acceleration, the same applies to
Torricelli's.
:::
:::

This equation is sometimes called "the timeless equation", and that is
exactly what it is. It gives us a direct relationship between:

- *v* (the final velocity)
- *v*₀ (the initial velocity)
- *a* (the acceleration)
- *s* (the displacement)

…without us having to care at all about how long (*t*) the motion took.

If we go back to our car problem: we know *v*₀ (20), *v* (0) and *a*
(−5). We are looking for *s*. With Torricelli's equation we can solve it in a single step,
instead of two.

The only rule (and it is exactly the same as for the other formulas) is that
it only works if the acceleration *a* is **constant**.

## Important details to remember

Even though it is always *applicable* at constant acceleration, there are a
couple of common "traps" or important nuances to think about when you *use* it.

::: härledning "1. s is displacement, not distance"
- **Displacement (*s*)**: the net change in position (a [[vector|A quantity with both magnitude and direction, for example velocity, force or acceleration. Differs from a scalar, which has only magnitude.]]). If you
  throw a ball straight up and catch it at the same height, your total
  *displacement* is *s* = 0.
- **Distance**: the total path you have moved (a scalar). In the ball example the
  distance is 2 · the maximum height.
- Torricelli's equation uses **displacement**. If *s* = 0 in the ball example,
  the equation gives $v^{2} - v_0^{2} = 0$, which correctly says that the final velocity
  *v* has the same *[[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]]* as *v*₀, just in the opposite direction.
:::

::: härledning "2. One dimension at a time"
- The equation handles motion in a single dimension (for example only the *x*-direction or
  only the *y*-direction).
- For projectile motion (two dimensions) you have to split up the problem. You can
  use the equation for the vertical motion (with $a_y = -g$) and separately
  for the horizontal motion (often with $a_x = 0$).
:::

## When is it NOT applicable?

The equation fails as soon as the acceleration is **not** constant. Examples:

- **[[Air resistance|The force from the air that slows down objects moving through the air. Depends on the shape, size and speed of the object.]]**: the [[force|An influence that can change the motion or shape of an object. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] (and therefore the acceleration) depends on the [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]].
- **Spring force**: the force (and therefore the acceleration) depends on the position
  ($F = -k x$).
- **Circular motion**: even at *constant speed* in a circle, the
  acceleration vector constantly changes direction (it is not constant).

In these cases you have to use calculus (integration) instead to solve
the problem.

**In summary:** the equation is the very definition of what happens
at constant acceleration, and its big advantage is that it relates
velocity, acceleration and displacement **without you needing to know the time
(*t*)**.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Torricelli's equation"
- $v^{2} - v_0^{2} = 2as$
- Called the **timeless equation**: it links $v$, $v_0$, $a$ and
  $s$ **without** the time being needed.
- Derived from $v = v_0 + a \cdot t$ and
  $s = v_0 t + \dfrac{1}{2} a t^{2}$ by eliminating $t$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why it is useful"
- Without it, two steps are needed: first calculate the time from one formula, then
  substitute it into the other.
- With it, one step is enough, and the risk of careless mistakes is smaller.
:::

::: sampunkt "The condition"
- The acceleration $a$ must be **constant**. The same requirement as for the other
  formulas of motion.
:::

::: sampunkt "Trap: s is displacement"
- $s$ in the formula is the **displacement**, not the distance.
- A ball that is thrown up and caught at the same height has $s = 0$.
- The equation then gives $v^{2} = v_0^{2}$: the same speed back, but in the opposite
  direction.
:::

::: sampunkt "Trap: one dimension at a time"
- The equation applies to motion in **one** direction.
- For projectile motion the problem is split up: vertically with $a_y = -g$, horizontally
  usually with $a_x = 0$.
:::

::: sampunkt "When it does not apply"
- **Air resistance**: the force depends on the velocity.
- **Spring force**: the force depends on the position, $F = -kx$.
- **Circular motion**: the direction of the acceleration changes all the time, even at
  constant speed.
:::
:::
