# Complex circuits

Circuits that are not simple, for example ones that contain both series and
parallel connections, are called **complex**. Calculating currents and
voltages in such circuits often takes several steps.

Remember the relationships we have already covered:

::: formel "Overview"
**Series connection:** $R_\text{tot} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$

**Parallel connection:** $\dfrac{1}{R_\text{tot}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \ldots$

**[[Ohm's law|The relationship between voltage, current and resistance: *U* = *R* · *I*.]]:** $U = R \cdot I$

**Kirchhoff's first law:** The total [[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]] into a point is equal to the
total current out of the point.

**Current in a series connection:** The current through components in series is equally
large.

**[[Voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] in a parallel connection:** The voltage across components in parallel
is equally large.
:::

**a) the total [[resistance|Opposition to electric current. According to Ohm's law: *U* = *R* · *I*. SI unit ohm (Ω).]] in the circuit.**
**b) the current through the 18 Ω resistor.**
**c) the current through the 20 Ω and the 30 Ω resistor.**

::: textlosning
**a)** In this circuit we have two resistors in parallel ($R_2$ and
$R_3$) that are in series with the first resistor ($R_1$). We start by
calculating the equivalent resistance $R_{2,3}$ of the resistors
in parallel.

$$
\frac{1}{R_{2,3}} = \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}
= \frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{30} = 0.0833\ldots
$$

$$
R_{2,3} = \frac{1}{0.0833\ldots} = 12\ \mathrm{\Omega}
$$

These are in series with $R_1 = 18\ \mathrm{\Omega}$, so the total
resistance in the circuit $R_\text{tot}$ is given by

$$
R_\text{tot} = R_1 + R_{2,3} = 18 + 12 = 30\ \mathrm{\Omega}
$$

**Answer:** 30 Ω

**b)** The current out of the battery *I* will be the same as
the current through the 18 Ω resistor, $I_1$, since it cannot take any other
path. We calculate the current out of the battery by solving for the current *I*
from Ohm's law.

$$
U = R \cdot I \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad I = \frac{U}{R}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
U = 20\ \mathrm{V} \\
R_\text{tot} = 30\ \mathrm{\Omega}\ (\text{from part a})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
I_1 = I = \frac{20}{30} = 0.666\ldots\ \mathrm{A} \approx 0.67\ \mathrm{A}
$$

**Answer:** 0.67 A

**c)** After the current has passed the 18 Ω resistor, it divides at
the branch point. To determine the current through the 20 Ω resistor, we set
up Ohm's law for that resistor and solve for the current $I_2$.

$$
U_2 = R_2 \cdot I_2 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad I_2 = \frac{U_2}{R_2} \qquad (1)
$$

We have $R_2 = 20\ \mathrm{\Omega}$, but we do not have $U_2$. We calculate $U_2$ from
the sum of the partial voltages.

$$
U_1 + U_2 = U \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad U_2 = U - U_1
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
U = 20\ \mathrm{V} \\
U_1 = R_1 \cdot I_1 = 18\ \mathrm{\Omega} \cdot 0.666\ldots\ \mathrm{A} = 12\ \mathrm{V}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
U_2 = 20 - 12 = 8\ \mathrm{V}
$$

Substituting $U_2 = 8$ V and $R_2 = 20\ \mathrm{\Omega}$ into (1), $I_2 = \dfrac{U_2}{R_2}$, gives

$$
I_2 = \frac{8.0}{20} = 0.40\ \mathrm{A}
$$

So the current through the 20 Ω resistor is 0.40 A. Since the current before
the branch point was 0.666… A, the current through the 30 Ω resistor must, according to
Kirchhoff's first law, be

$$
I_3 = I_1 - I_2 = (0.666\ldots - 0.40)\ \mathrm{A} = 0.266\ldots\ \mathrm{A}
\approx 0.27\ \mathrm{A}
$$

**Answer:** 0.40 A through the 20 Ω resistor and 0.27 A through the 30 Ω resistor.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Complex circuit"
- A circuit that contains **both** series and parallel connections.
- Solved in several steps, from the inside out.
:::

::: sampunkt "The toolbox"
- Series: $R_\text{tot} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$
- Parallel:
  $\dfrac{1}{R_\text{tot}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \ldots$
- Ohm's law: $U = R \cdot I$
- Kirchhoff's first law: as much current into a point as out of it.
:::

::: sampunkt "The two keys"
- The **current** is equally large through components **in series**.
- The **voltage** is equally large across components **in parallel**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Method"
1. Combine the parallel branches into one equivalent resistance.
2. Add the series resistances to get $R_\text{tot}$.
3. Calculate the main current from $I = \dfrac{U}{R_\text{tot}}$.
4. Calculate the voltage across each part with $U = R \cdot I$.
5. Use the branch's own voltage to get the partial currents.
6. Check with Kirchhoff: the partial currents should add up to
   the main current.
:::
:::
