# Summary: Energy

In this chapter you have met energy in its different forms and how it is transferred
between the forms through work. You can now calculate with potential energy, kinetic energy,
power and efficiency, and you have seen how the principle of conservation of energy and
the conservation of momentum are used to solve problems with collisions, throws and
motion on slopes.

## Concepts to know

- **energy**: the ability to do work; written *E*, SI unit J (joule).
- **work**: the energy converted when a force gives a displacement in the
  direction of the force: $W = F_s \cdot s$.
- **gravitational potential energy (potential energy)**: energy stored because of
  the object's height above a chosen zero level.
- **kinetic energy**: the energy an object has because of
  its velocity.
- **the principle of conservation of energy**: energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only
  converted between different forms.
- **mechanical energy**: the sum of potential and kinetic energy, $E_m = E_p + E_k$.
- **frictional heat**: the energy that "disappears" from the mechanical energy
  with friction; it always becomes heat.
- **power**: work or energy per unit time, $P = W/\Delta t$; SI unit
  W (watt).
- **efficiency**: the fraction of the supplied energy that becomes useful,
  $\eta = E_n/E_t$; a number between 0 and 1.
- **momentum**: the product of mass and velocity, $p = m \cdot v$; a
  vector.
- **impulse**: a force acting for a certain time, $I = F \cdot \Delta t$;
  changes the momentum.
- **elastic and inelastic collision**: in an elastic collision the objects bounce
  apart, in an inelastic (or perfectly inelastic) collision they stick
  together; momentum is conserved in both cases.

## Formulas

::: formel "Work"
$$
W = F_s \cdot s
$$

where

- *W* = work (Nm or J)
- $F_s$ = the force in the direction of motion (N)
- *s* = distance (m)
:::

::: formel "Potential energy and kinetic energy"
$$
E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h
\qquad\qquad
E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

where

- $E_p$ = gravitational potential energy (J)
- $E_k$ = kinetic energy (J)
- *m* = mass (kg)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg) (9.82 N/kg in Sweden)
- *h* = height above the zero level (m)
- *v* = velocity (m/s)

If the change in height takes place without friction, the work $W$ corresponds to the change in
potential energy: $W = \Delta E_p$.
:::

::: formel "The principle of conservation of energy and mechanical energy"
$$
E_m = E_p + E_k
$$

$$
E_{p1} + E_{k1} = E_{p2} + E_{k2} \qquad \text{(without friction)}
$$

$$
E_{p1} + E_{k1} = E_{p2} + E_{k2} + E_v \qquad \text{(with frictional heat } E_v \text{)}
$$

where

- $E_m$ = mechanical energy (J)
- $E_{p1}$, $E_{k1}$ = potential and kinetic energy at moment 1
- $E_{p2}$, $E_{k2}$ = potential and kinetic energy at moment 2
- $E_v$ = frictional heat (J), $E_v = \Delta E_m$ or $E_v = F_f \cdot s$
:::

::: formel "Power and efficiency"
$$
P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}
\qquad\qquad
\eta = \frac{E_n}{E_t} = \frac{P_n}{P_t}
$$

where

- *P* = power (W or J/s)
- Δ*E*, *W* = energy converted and work respectively (J)
- Δ*t* = time (s)
- *η* = efficiency, 0 ≤ *η* ≤ 1
- $E_n$, $P_n$ = useful energy and power respectively
- $E_t$, $P_t$ = energy and power supplied respectively

$$
1\ \mathrm{W} = 1\ \mathrm{J/s}
\qquad
1\ \mathrm{J} = 1\ \mathrm{Ws}
\qquad
1\ \mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{J}
$$
:::

::: formel "Momentum and impulse"
$$
p = m \cdot v
\qquad\qquad
I = F \cdot \Delta t
\qquad\qquad
I = \Delta p = m \cdot \Delta v = m v_2 - m v_1
$$

where

- *p* = momentum (kg · m/s)
- *I* = impulse (Ns)
- *F* = force (N)
- Δ*t* = time (s)
- Δ*p* = change in momentum (kg · m/s)

In an *F*-*t* graph the impulse corresponds to the area under the graph.
:::

::: formel "Conservation of momentum"
$$
m_A \cdot v_{A0} + m_B \cdot v_{B0} = m_A \cdot v_A + m_B \cdot v_B
$$

$$
m_A \cdot v_A + m_B \cdot v_B = (m_A + m_B) \cdot v \qquad \text{(perfectly inelastic collision)}
$$

where

- $m_A$, $m_B$ = mass of object A and B respectively
- $v_{A0}$, $v_{B0}$ = initial velocity of object A and B respectively
- $v_A$, $v_B$ = final velocity of object A and B respectively
- *v* = shared final velocity in a perfectly inelastic collision
:::

::: formel "Frictional heat"
$$
E_v = F_f \cdot s
\qquad\qquad
W = \Delta E_p + E_v \qquad \text{(work for a change in height with friction)}
$$

where

- $E_v$ = frictional heat (J)
- $F_f$ = frictional force (N)
- *s* = distance (m)
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- The zero level for potential energy can be chosen freely (usually at the bottom of the system).
  Only the *change* in height matters for the calculations.
- A force only does work if it has a component in the
  direction of motion: if the object moves horizontally at constant velocity while
  the force is vertical, $F_s = 0$ and the work is 0.
- For motion at an angle (for example pulling a cart at an angle) only
  the force component in the direction of motion is used: $F_s = F \cdot \cos \alpha$.
- The principle of conservation of energy $E_{p1} + E_{k1} = E_{p2} + E_{k2}$ applies when mechanical
  energy is conserved (no friction); with friction, the frictional heat $E_v$ is added
  to the right-hand side.
- The mass often "disappears" from the energy equation (it appears in every term).
  That is why, for example, freely falling objects hit the ground at the same speed regardless of
  mass.
- Momentum is a vector and the signs (+/−) must be kept track of;
  kinetic energy is always positive since the velocity is squared.
- In an *F*-*t* graph the area under the graph gives the impulse, just as the area in a
  *v*-*t* graph gives the displacement.
- The momentum in a closed system is always conserved in a collision, whether
  the collision is elastic or inelastic.
- In a perfectly inelastic collision (the objects stick together) they get a
  shared final velocity. Then the law of conservation of momentum can be written with
  $(m_A + m_B) \cdot v$.
- The efficiency can never be larger than 1 (100 %): a device can never
  give out more energy than it receives.

## Before the test

Can you...?

- Calculate work $W = F_s \cdot s$, even when the force acts at an angle (with a
  cos component in the direction of motion)?
- Calculate potential energy $E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h$ and explain that the zero level
  can be chosen freely?
- Calculate kinetic energy $E_k = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}$?
- Use the principle of conservation of energy, both without and with friction, to solve problems
  with changes in height and changes in velocity?
- Calculate power $P = \dfrac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$ and convert between J, Ws
  and kWh?
- Calculate efficiency $\eta = \dfrac{E_n}{E_t}$ and explain why it
  always lies between 0 and 1?
- Distinguish between momentum $p = m \cdot v$ and kinetic energy $E_k$, and
  explain why momentum is a vector?
- Use the impulse-momentum theorem $I = F \cdot \Delta t = \Delta p$, also from the area
  under an *F*-*t* graph?
- Set up the law of conservation of momentum for collisions, both elastic and
  (perfectly) inelastic?
- Calculate frictional heat $E_v = F_f \cdot s$ and use it in
  the principle of conservation of energy?
