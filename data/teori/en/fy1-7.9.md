# Electric power

In a resistor, energy is converted (and becomes [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]]). The electrical energy
converted per unit time is called **electric [[power|Work or energy per unit time: *P* = *W* / *t*. SI unit watt (W).]]**.

::: formel "Electric power"
$$
P = U \cdot I
$$

$$
P = R \cdot I^2
$$

$$
P = \frac{U^2}{R}
$$

where

- *P* = electric power (W)
- *U* = [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] across the component (V)
- *I* = [[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]] through the component (A)
- *R* = [[resistance|Opposition to electric current. According to Ohm's law: *U* = *R* · *I*. SI unit ohm (Ω).]] of the component (Ω)

::: härledning "Derivation: Electric power"
For the derivation we will use some earlier relationships.

- Electrical energy: $U = E/Q \Leftrightarrow E = Q \cdot U$ (1)
- Charge: $I = Q/t \Leftrightarrow Q = I \cdot t$ (2)
- [[Ohm's law|The relationship between voltage, current and resistance: *U* = *R* · *I*.]]: $U = R \cdot I$ (3)
- Ohm's law: $I = U/R$ (4)

We already know that power is defined as the energy converted
each second.

$$
P = \frac{E}{t} \qquad (5)
$$

(1) in (5) gives

$$
P = \frac{Q \cdot U}{t} \qquad (6)
$$

(2) in (6) gives

$$
P = \frac{I \cdot t \cdot U}{t} = I \cdot U = U \cdot I \qquad \text{q.e.d.}
$$

We have now shown that $P = U \cdot I$ (7).

(3) in (7) gives

$$
P = R \cdot I \cdot I = R \cdot I^2 \qquad \text{q.e.d.}
$$

(4) in (7) gives

$$
P = U \cdot \frac{U}{R} = \frac{U^2}{R} \qquad \text{q.e.d.}
$$
:::
:::

Sometimes the power in cords/wires/filaments has to be determined. Then remember
the formula for the resistance of a wire.

::: formel "Resistance of a wire"
$$
R = \rho \cdot \frac{l}{A}
$$

where

- *R* = resistance of the wire (Ω)
- *ρ* = resistivity (Ω·m)
- *l* = length of the wire (m)
- *A* = cross-sectional area of the wire (m²)
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for electric power and solve for the current *I*.

$$
P = U \cdot I \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad I = \frac{P}{U}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
P = 5.0\ \mathrm{W} \\
U = (1.5 + 1.5)\ \mathrm{V} = 3.0\ \mathrm{V}\ (\text{batteries in series are added})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
I = \frac{5.0}{3.0} = 1.666\ldots\ \mathrm{A} \approx 1.7\ \mathrm{A}
$$

**Answer:** 1.7 A
:::

::: textlosning
We are to determine the power. In the question we have the resistance *R* and the voltage
*U*. We therefore choose to use the formula

$$
P = \frac{U^2}{R}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
U = 230\ \mathrm{V} \\
R = 50\ \mathrm{\Omega}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
P = \frac{230^2}{50} = 1\,058\ \mathrm{W} \approx 1\,100\ \mathrm{W} = 1.1\ \mathrm{kW}
$$

**Answer:** 1.1 kW or 1 100 W

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Electric power"
- The electrical energy converted **per unit time**, usually into
  heat in a resistor.
- The SI unit is W (watt).
:::

::: sampunkt "Three forms of the same formula"
- $P = U \cdot I$
- $P = R \cdot I^{2}$
- $P = \dfrac{U^{2}}{R}$
- Choose the form whose quantities you already know.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where they come from"
- Start from $P = \dfrac{E}{t}$ with $E = Q \cdot U$ and
  $Q = I \cdot t$. The time cancels and what remains is
  $P = U \cdot I$.
- Then substitute Ohm's law, $U = R I$ or $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$, for the
  other two forms.
:::

::: sampunkt "The energy"
- $E = P \cdot t$
- Use time in seconds for joules, or use kWh for
  energy consumption.
:::
:::
