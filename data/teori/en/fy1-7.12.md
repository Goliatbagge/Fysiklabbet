# Electric potential

Electric fields and gravitational fields have a lot in common. Gravitational fields
affect masses in the direction of the field, while electric fields affect charges
in the direction of the field.

When we talked about [[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]] earlier in the subject, we concluded that we
could increase an object's gravitational potential energy by lifting
the object **against** the direction of the gravitational field. If we let go of the object,
it falls and potential energy is converted into [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]].

The same applies to electric fields! We can increase an object's "electrical
potential energy" by moving a charge against the direction of the electric field's
force. If we let go of the object, it "falls" and electrical potential energy
is converted into kinetic energy.

The "electrical potential energy" is called the **electric potential**, is denoted *V*
and has, just like [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]], the unit V (volt). Unlike voltage,
potential has a sign (positive, negative or zero). [[Current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]] flows from a point
with **higher potential** to a point with **lower potential**.

Just as gravitational potential energy cannot be determined without a zero level,
electric potential cannot be determined without a zero level. The zero level in
an electric circuit is called **earth** (ground), and there the potential is 0 V. In
circuit diagrams, earth is marked with an earth symbol (three horizontal
lines below each other that get narrower downwards). If there are several earth points in a
circuit, it corresponds to connecting a conductor between the earth points.

An earth connection is a conductor connected to the ground of the Earth (hence the name).
Since the Earth can be seen as a large conductor, the voltage between different
earthed points is 0. An earthed conductor gets the same potential as the Earth. It
leads unwanted charges/current away from the circuit and down into the ground.

This is good from a safety point of view, since for example an electrical fault could
make the casing of an unearthed toaster live. If the casing is
earthed, the current in an electrical fault will instead go down into the ground.

## Determining the potential at a point (potential walk)

The potential at a point is determined by starting in an earthed part of the circuit,
where the potential is 0. Then you "walk" from the earth point to
the point whose potential is to be determined. The potential changes as we pass through
batteries and resistors as shown below.

::: formel "Changes in potential during a potential walk"
| If you go … | then … |
| --- | --- |
| from a battery's negative terminal to its positive terminal | the potential **increases** by the battery's voltage *U* |
| from a battery's positive terminal to its negative terminal | the potential **decreases** by the battery's voltage *U* |
| through a resistor **against** the current | the potential **increases** by $R \cdot I$ |
| through a resistor **with** the current | the potential **decreases** by $R \cdot I$ |
:::

Since potential corresponds to "electrical potential energy", it follows that if we
walk a whole lap around a circuit, the sum of all
changes in potential must be 0 (since the potential must be the same at
the point both before and after the walk). This principle is called **Kirchhoff's
second law**.

::: formel "Kirchhoff's second law"
The sum of all changes in potential in a closed circuit is zero.

$$
\sum \Delta V = 0
$$
:::

Voltage is a difference in potential and cannot be negative. If the difference in
potential were for example (−3.0) V, the voltage is 3.0 V. Mathematically
this can be written with the absolute value |…|. Then we write |−3.0| = 3.0. You
can therefore think of the absolute value as "removing the minus sign".

::: formel "Voltage between two points"
$$
U = |V_A - V_B|
$$

where

- *U* = voltage between point A and point B (V)
- $V_A$, $V_B$ = potential at point A and at point B respectively (V)

|…| stands for the absolute value. If $V_A - V_B$ is negative, the minus sign is
removed.
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We start by determining the potential at point A, that is
$V_A$. We start at the earthed point B, where the potential is 0, and then go
through the 3.0 Ω resistor **against** the current, which is 2.0 A.

$$
V_A = 0 + R_{3\Omega} \cdot I = 0 + 3.0 \cdot 2.0 = +6.0\ \mathrm{V}
$$

We then determine the potential at point B. It is 0 since it is earthed.

$$
V_B = 0
$$

Finally we determine the potential at point C by walking from point
B through the 9.0 Ω resistor **with** the current, which is 2.0 A.

$$
V_C = 0 - R_{9\Omega} \cdot I = 0 - 9.0 \cdot 2.0 = -18\ \mathrm{V}
$$

**Answer:** $V_A = +6.0$ V and $V_B = 0$ and $V_C = -18$ V.

**b)** The voltage is the difference in potential between the points A and C.

$$
U = |V_C - V_A| = |-18 - 6.0| = |-24| = 24\ \mathrm{V}
$$

**Answer:** 24 V
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Electric potential"
- The electrical counterpart of potential energy. Denoted $V$ and measured
  in volts.
- Unlike voltage, **the potential has a sign**: it can be
  positive, negative or zero.
- The current flows from **higher** to **lower** potential.
:::

::: sampunkt "Earth is the zero level"
- Just like potential energy, the potential needs a zero level. It is called
  **earth** and has $V = 0$.
- The Earth is a large conductor, so all earthed points have the same
  potential.
- Earthing leads away unwanted current and is therefore a safety matter: an
  electrical fault in an earthed toaster goes down into the ground instead of out into the casing.
:::

::: sampunkt "Potential walk"
- From **negative terminal to positive terminal**: the potential **increases** by $U$.
- From **positive terminal to negative terminal**: the potential **decreases** by $U$.
- Through a resistor **against** the current: **increases** by $R \cdot I$.
- Through a resistor **with** the current: **decreases** by $R \cdot I$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Kirchhoff's second law"
- $\sum \Delta V = 0$
- The sum of all changes in potential around a closed circuit is zero, since
  you come back to the same point.
- Used to calculate the current when several batteries oppose each other.
:::

::: sampunkt "Voltage as a potential difference"
- $U = |V_A - V_B|$
- The absolute value removes the minus sign: a difference of $-3.0$ V is a
  voltage of $3.0$ V.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to solve"
1. Start at the earthed point, where $V = 0$.
2. Walk towards the point you are looking for and add each change with the correct sign.
3. The voltage between two points is the absolute value of their difference.
:::
:::
