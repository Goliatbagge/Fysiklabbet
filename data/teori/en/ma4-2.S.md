# Summary: The derivative

This chapter built further on the derivative from Mathematics continuation level 1c: the definition
as the limit of a difference quotient, Leibniz notation $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ and
the interpretation of the derivative as the slope of a tangent and a rate of change.
After that the rules of differentiation were extended with the chain rule (for composite
functions), the derivatives of $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $e^x$ and $\ln x$, and
the product and quotient rules. The chapter ended with optimisation problems,
curve sketching with a sign table, and asymptotes: vertical, horizontal
and oblique. After the chapter you should be able to differentiate all these types of function,
interpret a value of the derivative in an application, find and classify
stationary points, and sketch graphs using a sign table and asymptotes.

## Concepts to know

- **Leibniz notation ($\dfrac{dy}{dx}$)**: an alternative way of writing the
  derivative that shows which variable you differentiate with respect to;
  particularly useful when several quantities depend on each other.
- **the definition of the derivative**: the derivative at a point is the limit of
  the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ as $h \to 0$, that is
  the slope of a secant that slides towards the tangent.
- **secant and tangent**: a secant intersects the curve at two points, a tangent
  touches the curve at a single point; the slope of the secant tends to the slope of the tangent
  as the two points slide together.
- **the derivative as a rate of change**: in applications the
  derivative states how fast a quantity changes; the unit is "unit on
  the *y*-axis per unit on the *x*-axis".
- **composite function**: a function of a function, $f(g(x))$, with an
  outer function *f* and an inner function *g*.
- **the chain rule**: the rule for differentiating a composite function: the outer
  derivative (with the inner function left unchanged) times the inner derivative.
- **related rates of change**: problems where the chain rule in
  Leibniz form, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{dz}\cdot \dfrac{dz}{dx}$,
  links the rates of change of two quantities.
- **the product rule**: the rule for differentiating a product of two functions.
- **the quotient rule**: the rule for differentiating a quotient of two functions.
- **D notation ($D(f(x))$)**: another way of writing $f'(x)$ where the expression
  to be differentiated is inserted directly into the operator *D*.
- **optimisation problem**: a problem about finding a
  quantity's largest or smallest value, usually by solving $f'(x) = 0$.
- **local maximum/minimum point and stationary point of inflection**: points where
  $f'(x) = 0$; a maximum/minimum point is a "peak"/"trough" where the curve
  turns, a stationary point of inflection only levels out without turning.
- **second derivative**: the derivative of the derivative; its sign decides whether the curve
  bends upwards (convex) or downwards (concave), and therefore the nature of a
  stationary point.
- **sign table**: a table that shows the sign of the derivative in each
  subinterval, used to sketch the graph of a function.
- **asymptote (vertical, horizontal, oblique)**: a line that the curve
  approaches but never reaches, as *x* tends to a certain value or to
  infinity.

## Formulas

::: formel "The chapter's formulas"
**Leibniz notation**

$$
y' = f'(x) = \frac{dy}{dx} \qquad\qquad y'' = f''(x) = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
$$

**The definition of the derivative**

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

**Rules of differentiation for the basic functions**

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) = x^n &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = nx^{n-1}\\
f(x) = kx^n &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = nkx^{n-1}\\
f(x) = e^{kx} &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = ke^{kx}\\
f(x) = a^x &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = a^x\ln a\\
f(x) = \ln x &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = \frac{1}{x} \quad (x>0)\\
f(x) = \sin x &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = \cos x\\
f(x) = \cos x &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = -\sin x\\
f(x) = \tan x &\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x) = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = 1+\tan^2 x
\end{aligned}
$$

where the base *a* in the fourth row is the base of the exponential function, and the angle
*x* in the trigonometric functions must be measured in radians.

**The chain rule**

$$
y' = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)
\qquad\text{or, in Leibniz notation,}\qquad
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dz}\cdot \frac{dz}{dx}
$$

where *f* (or *y* as a function of *z*) is the outer function and
$g(x)$ (or *z*) the inner function.

**The product rule**

$$
y' = f'(x)\cdot g(x) + f(x)\cdot g'(x)
$$

if $y = f(x)\cdot g(x)$.

**The quotient rule**

$$
y' = \frac{f'(x)\cdot g(x) - f(x)\cdot g'(x)}{\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^2}
$$

if $y = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$.

**The second derivative and the nature of a stationary point**

At a point where $f'(x) = 0$:

- $f''(x) > 0$: the curve bends upwards (convex): **minimum point**
- $f''(x) < 0$: the curve bends downwards (concave): **maximum point**
- $f''(x) = 0$: a sign study is needed; it may be a **stationary point of inflection**

**Vertical asymptote**

$$
\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty
\quad\Rightarrow\quad x = a
$$

For a rational function, *a* is found by setting the denominator equal to zero.

**Horizontal asymptote**

$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = m
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = m
\quad\Rightarrow\quad y = m
$$

**Oblique asymptote**

$$
f(x) = kx + m + R(x), \qquad \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} R(x) = 0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad y = kx + m
$$

Oblique asymptotes occur for rational functions where the degree of the numerator is
exactly one higher than that of the denominator.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- Leibniz notation $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ makes clear which variable you
  differentiate with respect to, which is crucial when several quantities depend on
  each other, as in related rates of change.
- The equation of the tangent is found in two steps: the $k$-value is the value of the derivative
  at the point, $y'(a)$; the $m$-value is then found by substituting the point's
  coordinates into the equation of a straight line $y = kx + m$.
- The chain rule: differentiate the outer function with the inner function left
  unchanged, then multiply by the inner derivative. If the inner
  function in turn has its own inner function, the chain rule is needed several
  times in a row.
- For related rates of change: identify what is wanted and what
  is given, set up the chain rule in Leibniz form
  ($\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{dy}{dr}\cdot \dfrac{dr}{dt}$ for example) and
  solve for the required quotient.
- The derivatives of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ only apply if the angle is measured in
  radians; in degrees the rule of differentiation does not hold.
- A product of two functions must **not** be differentiated factor by factor;
  the product rule is needed. A sum of terms, on the other hand, is still differentiated
  term by term.
- Digital tools (for example GeoGebra) can differentiate and solve $f'(x) = 0$
  numerically or graphically when calculation by hand is hard or impossible, for
  example for functions like $f(x) = x^{\sin x}$. Always decide first
  *which* operation the question corresponds to: a rate of change is a
  value of the derivative $f'(a)$, while "largest/smallest" or "no change"
  leads to the equation $f'(x) = 0$.
- Optimisation problems are solved in three steps: differentiate, set $f'(x) = 0$ and
  solve for *x*; substitute the *x*-values into the **original** function to
  get the *y*-coordinates; decide the nature using the sign of the second derivative.
- In a closed interval it is not enough to look at the stationary points:
  also compare the function value at the endpoints of the interval. The largest of
  all the compared values is the function's largest value, the smallest is its
  smallest value.
- Curve sketching is done with a sign table: mark the zeros of the derivative
  and the gaps in the domain, investigate the sign of the derivative in each
  subinterval and read off increasing ($\nearrow$) and decreasing
  ($\searrow$) branches.
- Vertical asymptotes are found from denominator = 0 (rational functions).
  Horizontal asymptotes are found from the limit as $x \to \pm\infty$, if
  it is finite. Oblique asymptotes instead occur when the degree of the numerator
  is exactly one higher than that of the denominator: then split the expression into terms until
  it is in the form $kx + m + R(x)$ with $R(x) \to 0$.
- A function cannot have both a horizontal and an oblique asymptote in
  the same direction at the same time: only one of them applies in each direction.

## Before the test

- Can you explain what the definition of the derivative really measures, and link
  the secant and the tangent?
- Do you know the difference between the notations $y'$, $f'(x)$ and $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$,
  and why Leibniz notation is sometimes preferable?
- Can you interpret a value of the derivative both graphically (the slope of the tangent) and in
  an application (rate of change, with the right unit)?
- Can you differentiate a composite function with the chain rule, even when the
  inner function in turn has its own inner function?
- Can you set up and solve problems with related rates of change
  using the chain rule in Leibniz form?
- Do you know the rules of differentiation for $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $e^{kx}$, $a^x$ and
  $\ln x$ by heart, and do you know that the trigonometric ones require radians?
- Do you know the product rule and the quotient rule, and do you know why you cannot
  differentiate a product "factor by factor"?
- Can you find stationary points (differentiate, set equal to zero, solve) and
  decide their nature using the second derivative?
- Can you find the largest/smallest value of a function in a closed
  interval by comparing stationary points and endpoints?
- Can you sketch the graph of a function using a sign table?
- Can you find vertical, horizontal and oblique asymptotes of a
  rational function?
- Do you know when a digital tool is necessary, and which operation
  ($f'(a)$ or $f'(x) = 0$) the question corresponds to?
