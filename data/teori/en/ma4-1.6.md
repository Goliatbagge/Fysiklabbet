# Relationships between angles on the unit circle

Two angles that together make $90^\circ$ are called **complementary angles**.
The sine of an angle is equal to the cosine of its complementary angle and vice
versa.

::: formel "Complementary angles"
$$
\sin(90^\circ - v) = \cos v
$$

$$
\cos(90^\circ - v) = \sin v
$$
:::

For example, this means that $\sin 80^\circ = \cos 10^\circ$ and that
$\cos 40^\circ = \sin 50^\circ$.

There are more simple relationships between angles on the unit circle that make it
easier to work with trigonometric functions. The relationships are based on
symmetries and reflections in the unit circle: they show how sine and cosine
are linked for different angles, for example when we add or subtract
$90^\circ$ or $180^\circ$, or use negative angles.

::: formel "Relationships between angles"
For all angles $v$:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\sin(90^\circ - v) = \cos v & \qquad \cos(90^\circ - v) = \sin v \\[2pt]
\sin(-v) = -\sin v & \qquad \cos(-v) = \cos v \\[2pt]
\sin(180^\circ - v) = \sin v & \qquad \cos(180^\circ - v) = -\cos v \\[2pt]
\sin(v + 90^\circ) = \cos v & \qquad \cos(v + 90^\circ) = -\sin v \\[2pt]
\sin(v + 180^\circ) = -\sin v & \qquad \cos(v + 180^\circ) = -\cos v
\end{array}
$$
:::

These formulas are **not** on the formula sheet for this level. So it is good
to understand *why* the relationships turn out as they do from the unit circle (or
to memorise them).

A clear example is what happens when we add $90^\circ$. A point
on the unit circle at the angle $v$ has the coordinates $(\cos v,\ \sin v)$. If we turn
the radius a quarter turn anticlockwise, to the angle $v + 90^\circ$, the
coordinates swap places and the $x$-coordinate also changes sign: the new point
gets the coordinates $(-\sin v,\ \cos v)$. Reading off the new coordinates gives
directly that $\cos(v + 90^\circ) = -\sin v$ and $\sin(v + 90^\circ) = \cos v$.

If an angle is given in **radians**, it is often easiest to first convert it
to degrees to interpret it more easily on the unit circle. If you have an angle that is
greater than $360^\circ$ (or less than $0^\circ$), you can add or subtract
whole turns, $360^\circ$, in the argument without changing the function value. In
radians you do the same, but then a full turn is $2\pi$ instead.

::: exempel "Example 1: Negative and turned angles"
**We know that $\sin 60^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. Without a calculator, find<br>a)&nbsp;$\sin(-60^\circ)$&emsp;&emsp;b)&nbsp;$\cos 30^\circ$&emsp;&emsp;c)&nbsp;$\sin 240^\circ$**

::: handskrift
typ: vridnavinklar
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** A negative angle reflects the point in the $x$-axis, so the sine changes sign:

$$
\sin(-60^\circ) = -\sin 60^\circ = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
$$

**b)** $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ are complementary angles (together they make
$90^\circ$), so the cosine of one is the sine of the other:

$$
\cos 30^\circ = \sin(90^\circ - 30^\circ) = \sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
$$

**c)** Half a turn ($180^\circ$) ends up directly opposite on the unit circle, so the sine
changes sign:

$$
\sin 240^\circ = \sin(60^\circ + 180^\circ) = -\sin 60^\circ = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
$$

**Answer:** a) $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$&emsp;&emsp;b) $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$&emsp;&emsp;c) $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Angle in radians"
**We know that $\cos \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Find $\cos \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$.**

::: handskrift
typ: cosradian
:::

::: textlosning
We convert the angles to degrees:

$$
\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{180^\circ}{4} = 45^\circ \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{5\pi}{4} = \frac{5 \cdot 180^\circ}{4} = 225^\circ
$$

So we know that $\cos 45^\circ = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and want to find
$\cos 225^\circ$. Since $225^\circ$ lies $180^\circ$ away from $45^\circ$,
the cosine changes sign:

$$
\cos \frac{5\pi}{4} = \cos 225^\circ = -\cos(225^\circ - 180^\circ) = -\cos 45^\circ = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

**Answer:** $-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: Show a relationship"
**Show that $\sin(450^\circ - v) = \cos v$.**

::: handskrift
typ: visasamband
:::

::: textlosning
The angle in the argument is greater than $360^\circ$. We start by subtracting one
full turn ($360^\circ$), since it does not affect the trigonometric value:

$$
\text{LHS} = \sin(450^\circ - v) = \sin(450^\circ - 360^\circ - v) = \sin(90^\circ - v) = \cos v = \text{RHS}
$$

where the last step uses the complementary angle relationship $\sin(90^\circ - v) = \cos v$.

**Answer:** QED
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Complementary angles"
- Two angles that together make $90^\circ$ are **complementary angles**.
- $\sin(90^\circ - v) = \cos v$ and $\cos(90^\circ - v) = \sin v$.
- So $\sin 80^\circ = \cos 10^\circ$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The relationships"
- $\sin(-v) = -\sin v$ and $\cos(-v) = \cos v$.
- $\sin(180^\circ - v) = \sin v$ and $\cos(180^\circ - v) = -\cos v$.
- $\sin(v + 90^\circ) = \cos v$ and $\cos(v + 90^\circ) = -\sin v$.
- $\sin(v + 180^\circ) = -\sin v$ and $\cos(v + 180^\circ) = -\cos v$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why they hold"
- The relationships are **reflections and rotations** in the unit circle.
- If the radius is turned a quarter turn anticlockwise, the coordinates swap places and the $x$-coordinate changes sign: $(\cos v,\ \sin v)$ becomes $(-\sin v,\ \cos v)$.
- The formulas are **not** on the formula sheet. Understand them from the circle instead of memorising them.
:::

::: sampunkt "Method"
1. Subtract whole turns if the angle is over $360^\circ$, or $2\pi$ in radians.
2. Convert radians to degrees if that makes the angle easier to place.
3. Write the angle as a known angle plus $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$ or a minus sign.
- $\sin 240^\circ = \sin(60^\circ + 180^\circ) = -\sin 60^\circ = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
:::
