# Optimisation problems (review)

Problems that are about finding the **largest** (maximum) or **smallest**
(minimum) value of a quantity are called **extreme value problems** or
**optimisation problems**. We solve them using the derivative: at a peak or a trough on
the curve the slope, and therefore the derivative, is zero, so we look for the values of $x$ where
$f'(x) = 0$ and then decide whether it is a maximum or a minimum.

## Review from Mathematics continuation level 1c

The sign of the first derivative tells us whether the function is increasing or decreasing. If
$f'(x) > 0$ the slope is positive ("uphill") and the function is **increasing**;
if $f'(x) < 0$ the slope is negative ("downhill") and the function is
**decreasing**. A point where the curve turns from increasing to decreasing is a
**local maximum point** ("peak"), and a point where it turns from decreasing
to increasing is a **local minimum point** ("trough"). The function value (the $y$-value)
at the point is called the local **maximum** or **minimum** respectively. If the curve levels
out and then continues in the same direction, we instead have a **stationary point of inflection**:
there too the derivative is zero, but the curve does not turn.

## The second derivative and the graph: a rule to remember

The second derivative tells us which way the curve bends, and so it can
determine the nature of a stationary point.

::: formel "The second derivative and the graph"
At a point where $f'(x) = 0$:

- $f''(x) > 0$: the curve bends **upwards** ("happy" curve, convex): **minimum point**
- $f''(x) < 0$: the curve bends **downwards** ("sad" curve, concave): **maximum point**
- $f''(x) = 0$: the curve's bending may change (**point of inflection**);
  a sign study is needed to decide the nature.
:::

## Finding stationary points and their nature

::: formel "Method in three steps"
1. **Differentiate** the original function, set $f'(x) = 0$ and solve
   the equation. The roots are the $x$-coordinates of the stationary points.
2. **Substitute** these $x$-values into the *original* function $f(x)$ to
   get the $y$-coordinates of the stationary points.
3. **Determine the nature** using the sign of the second derivative: $f''(a) > 0$ gives a
   minimum point, $f''(a) < 0$ gives a maximum point.
:::

If the function is a quadratic function, the nature can be decided directly from the sign
in front of the $x^2$ term: a positive $x^2$ term gives a minimum point, a negative
$x^2$ term a maximum point.

## The largest and smallest value in an interval

If we are looking for the largest or smallest value in a *closed* interval, it is not
enough to look at the stationary points: the endpoints of the interval can also be the
highest or lowest. So substitute **the endpoints of the interval** into $f(x)$,
calculate the $y$-value at the **stationary points that lie within the interval**, and
compare. The largest of all these values is the function's largest value in
the interval, and the smallest is its smallest value.

::: exempel "Example 1: Vanja's paddock"
**Vanja has bought 60 m of fencing for a rectangular horse paddock where one of the sides
is to be formed by a wall (no fencing is needed there, see the figure). What is the
maximum area the paddock can have?**

::: handskrift
typ: hagederivata
:::

::: textlosning
Let the two sides perpendicular to the wall be $x$ metres. The fencing on the third side is then
$60 - 2x$ metres, since $2x$ has been used for the two other sides. The area
is the base times the height:

$$
A(x) = x(60 - 2x) = 60x - 2x^2
$$

We differentiate, set the derivative equal to zero and solve the equation:

$$
A'(x) = 60 - 4x
$$

$$
A'(x) = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 60 - 4x = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x = \frac{60}{4} = 15
$$

We substitute $x = 15$ into the original function to get the area:

$$
A(15) = 60\cdot 15 - 2\cdot 15^2 = 900 - 450 = 450
$$

We determine the nature using the second derivative. Since $A'(x) = 60 - 4x$,
$A''(x) = -4$, so

$$
A''(15) = -4 < 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{maximum point}
$$

(We could also have seen this directly: $A(x) = 60x - 2x^2$ has a negative
$x^2$ term and therefore gives a maximum.)

**Answer:** The maximum area is $450\ \mathrm{m}^2$.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Find and classify the stationary points"
**Find the local stationary points of $f(x) = x^3 - 12x$ and use
the second derivative to decide whether they are maxima or minima.**

::: handskrift
typ: extrempunkter
:::

::: textlosning
We differentiate and solve $f'(x) = 0$:

$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12
$$

$$
3x^2 - 12 = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x^2 = 4 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x = \pm 2
$$

The $y$-coordinates are found from the original function:

$$
f(2) = 2^3 - 12\cdot 2 = 8 - 24 = -16, \qquad f(-2) = (-2)^3 - 12\cdot(-2) = -8 + 24 = 16
$$

The second derivative is $f''(x) = 6x$:

$$
f''(2) = 12 > 0 \Rightarrow \text{minimum point}, \qquad f''(-2) = -12 < 0 \Rightarrow \text{maximum point}
$$

**Answer:** Local maximum point $(-2,\ 16)$ and local minimum point $(2,\ -16)$.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: The largest and smallest value in an interval"
**Find the largest and the smallest value of $f(x) = x^3 - 12x$ in the interval
$0 \le x \le 3$.**

::: handskrift
typ: intervallvarden
:::

::: textlosning
We compare the function value at the endpoints of the interval and at the stationary points that
lie within the interval. From Example 2 we know that the stationary points are at
$x = -2$ and $x = 2$; only $x = 2$ lies in the interval.

$$
f(0) = 0, \qquad f(2) = 2^3 - 12\cdot 2 = -16, \qquad f(3) = 3^3 - 12\cdot 3 = 27 - 36 = -9
$$

The largest of the values is $0$ (at the endpoint $x = 0$) and the smallest is $-16$
(at the minimum point $x = 2$).

**Answer:** The largest value is $0$ (at $x = 0$) and the smallest value is $-16$
(at $x = 2$).
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Optimisation problems"
- Tasks about a largest or smallest value are called **extreme value** or **optimisation problems**.
- At a peak or trough the slope is zero, that is $f'(x) = 0$.
- $f'(x) > 0$ means increasing, $f'(x) < 0$ decreasing.
:::

::: sampunkt "The names of the points"
- **Local maximum point:** the curve turns from increasing to decreasing.
- **Local minimum point:** the curve turns from decreasing to increasing.
- **Stationary point of inflection:** the curve levels out but continues in the same direction. The derivative is zero there too.
:::

::: sampunkt "The second derivative decides the nature"
- $f''(x) > 0$: the curve bends upwards and is convex, so a **minimum point**.
- $f''(x) < 0$: the curve bends downwards and is concave, so a **maximum point**.
- $f''(x) = 0$: a sign study is needed. The point may be a point of inflection.
- Shortcut for quadratic functions: a positive $x^2$ term gives a minimum, a negative one a maximum.
:::

::: sampunkt "Method in three steps"
1. Differentiate, set $f'(x) = 0$ and solve the equation. That gives the $x$-coordinates.
2. Substitute the $x$-values into the **original** function for the $y$-coordinates.
3. Determine the nature using the sign of the second derivative.
- In a **closed** interval the values at the endpoints must also be compared with those at the stationary points.
:::

:::
