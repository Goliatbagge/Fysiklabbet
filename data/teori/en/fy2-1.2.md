# More on moments

In some situations with **rotational equilibrium** there is no natural pivot in
the problem. The pivot can, however, be chosen freely! If we have two unknown
[[forces|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] to calculate, we can place the pivot at the
point of application of one of the forces to find the other. A force
whose line of action passes through the pivot has a lever arm of 0 and gives no
moment.

::: förklaring "Explanation: Why the pivot can be chosen freely"
The claim may seem strange. In a seesaw the pivot is given:
the support in the middle, which the board actually turns around. But a board that
rests on two trestles does not turn at all. Why, then, may we calculate as if it
did, and around any point at all?

**The answer lies in the word rest.** A board that lies still does not rotate.
Not around the left support, not around the right one, not around its
centre of gravity, and not around a point in the air some distance outside the board either.
If the moments around any of these points did not cancel each other out, the
board would start to turn around exactly that point, and then it would not be lying still.
Rotational equilibrium therefore holds around **every** point we can think of. That is
why we may choose: every point gives the right answer, so we take the one that needs the least
calculation.

That every point gives the same answer can also be seen directly in the calculation. In the figure
below, the pivot O is chosen in the air some distance to the left of the board, so
that all three forces lie on the same side of it.

Seen from O, the support forces $F_P$ and $F_Q$ turn the board anticlockwise and
the gravitational force $F_G$ clockwise, so the principle of moments reads

$$
F_P \cdot l_P + F_Q \cdot l_Q = F_G \cdot l_G
$$

Now move the pivot the distance $d$ to the left, to O′. Since the three
forces are parallel, each lever arm becomes exactly $d$ longer, no more for one
than for another. About O′ the principle of moments reads

$$
F_P \cdot (l_P + d) + F_Q \cdot (l_Q + d) = F_G \cdot (l_G + d)
$$

If we multiply $d$ into the brackets, the left-hand side takes the form

$$
F_P \cdot l_P + F_Q \cdot l_Q + (F_P + F_Q) \cdot d
$$

and the right-hand side the form

$$
F_G \cdot l_G + F_G \cdot d
$$

The first terms on each side are the moments about O, and they are
equal; that was our starting point. What remains to compare are the additions
$(F_P + F_Q) \cdot d$ and $F_G \cdot d$. But the board is at rest, so
**force equilibrium** holds: the upward forces together are equal to the
downward force, $F_P + F_Q = F_G$. Therefore the two additions are equal.
The moments grow on both sides of the equals sign, but by the same amount, and
the balance remains.

The same thing happens however far we move the point, and in whichever direction
we like. If we move it onto the board, a force changes its direction of turning when
the point passes its line of action, but the calculation works out in the same way.
We test this with numbers in the check after Example 1.

Notice what made it work out: **force equilibrium**. It is
what ensures that a change of pivot adds the same amount of
moment on both sides. For a body at rest, both equilibria hold
at the same time, and then rotational equilibrium about a single point is enough for it to
hold about all points.
:::

A good rule of thumb: **the closer to the centre of gravity a supporting or carrying force
acts, the greater the share of the weight it carries.**

::: demo "Demonstration: The ruler's centre of gravity"
A ruler is placed horizontally, resting on two index fingers. We slowly slide
the index fingers towards each other. Since the index finger closest to the ruler's
**centre of gravity** gives a larger normal force, the frictional force there is also
larger. The index finger furthest from the centre of gravity, with lower
friction, therefore slides more easily, until it in turn comes closer to the centre of gravity and the
other index finger starts to slide instead. This repeats by itself,
alternately, until the index fingers touch each other. We have found the ruler's
centre of gravity!

The principle of moments gives

$$
M_1 = M_2
$$

$$
F_Q \cdot l_Q = F_G \cdot l_G
$$

We solve for $F_Q$ and get

$$
F_Q = \frac{F_G \cdot l_G}{l_Q}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_G = m \cdot g = 20\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} = 196.4\ \mathrm{N} \\
l_G = 1.5\ \mathrm{m} \\
l_Q = 2.5\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_Q = \frac{196.4 \cdot 1.5}{2.5} = 117.84\ \mathrm{N} \approx 120\ \mathrm{N} = 0.12\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 0.12 kN.

calculated using **force equilibrium**: the upward forces from the trestles must

estimate with rounded values (the gravitational force about 200 N and
the force from the right-hand trestle about 120 N) gives

::: härledning "Check: the same answer with any pivot"
In the example we first chose P and then Q as the pivot and got
$F_Q = 117.84\ \mathrm{N}$ and $F_P = 78.56\ \mathrm{N}$ (unrounded).
Now that all three forces are known, we can test the claim that
rotational equilibrium holds about every point: we choose some new
pivots, measure the lever arms from them and calculate the moments in
each direction.

| Pivot | Anticlockwise moment | Clockwise moment |
|---|---|---|
| The centre of gravity | $F_Q \cdot 1.0\ \mathrm{m} = 117.84\ \mathrm{Nm}$ | $F_P \cdot 1.5\ \mathrm{m} = 117.84\ \mathrm{Nm}$ |
| The left end of the board | $F_P \cdot 1.5\ \mathrm{m} + F_Q \cdot 4.0\ \mathrm{m} = 589.2\ \mathrm{Nm}$ | $F_G \cdot 3.0\ \mathrm{m} = 589.2\ \mathrm{Nm}$ |
| In the air, 1.0 m beyond the left end | $F_P \cdot 2.5\ \mathrm{m} + F_Q \cdot 5.0\ \mathrm{m} = 785.6\ \mathrm{Nm}$ | $F_G \cdot 4.0\ \mathrm{m} = 785.6\ \mathrm{Nm}$ |

About every point, the moments in the two directions are equal, just as
the reasoning above promised. This also holds about the centre of gravity, where
the support force $F_P$ has changed its direction of turning, and about a point that does not
even lie on the board.

Notice also that none of these points would have helped us solve
the task. With the centre of gravity as the pivot, only $F_G$ disappears, which we
could already calculate from $m \cdot g$, and both unknown forces remain in
the same equation. The freedom to choose therefore does not mean that the choice does not
matter. The answer is the same about every point, but the amount of work differs,
and the smart choice is the point where an unknown force gets the lever arm 0.
:::

The centre of gravity does not only decide how the load is shared between two supports. It
also decides whether an object stays standing at all.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The principle of moments"
- Moment of a force: force times lever arm, $M = F \cdot l$. The SI unit is Nm.
- The lever arm $l$ is the **perpendicular distance** from the pivot to
  the force's line of action.
- **Rotational equilibrium**: the anticlockwise moments together are equal to
  the clockwise moments, $M_1 = M_2$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Choose the pivot wisely"
- In rotational equilibrium the pivot can be **chosen freely**: a body at
  rest does not rotate about **any** point, so the moments balance about all of them.
- Place it at an unknown force's point of application: the force gets the lever arm 0 and
  disappears from the moment equation.
- Two unknown forces? Calculate one at a time with a pivot for each, or
  the second using force equilibrium.
:::

::: sampunkt "Force equilibrium"
- A body at rest satisfies **force equilibrium**: the upward forces together are
  equal to the downward forces.
- Board on two supports: $F_P + F_Q = F_G$.
- Rule of thumb: **the closer to the centre of gravity** a support is, **the greater
  the share of the weight** it carries.
:::

::: sampunkt "Typical task: board on two supports"
1. Draw the forces: $F_P$ and $F_Q$ upwards at the supports, $F_G$ downwards from
   the centre of gravity.
2. Choose the pivot at one of the supports. The principle of moments gives
   $F_Q = \dfrac{F_G \cdot l_G}{l_Q}$.
3. The other support force from force equilibrium: $F_P = F_G - F_Q$ (or
   the principle of moments again, with a new pivot).
:::

::: sampunkt "Centre of gravity"
- The point where the whole gravitational force $F_G$ can be considered to act.
- Found with the ruler experiment: the finger closest to the centre of gravity carries the most load,
  gets the greatest friction and slides last. The fingers meet under the centre of gravity.
- An object **topples over** when the vertical line through the centre of gravity ends up
  outside the pivot.
:::
:::
