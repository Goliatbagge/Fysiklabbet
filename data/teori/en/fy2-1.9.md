# Numerical simulation of projectile motion

::: härledning "Extension"
This section is an **extension**. It builds on projectile motion from the previous section and
shows how a motion can be calculated step by step when the formulas are not enough,
for example when air resistance cannot be neglected.
:::

The difference is due to [[air resistance|The force from the air that slows down objects moving through it. Depends on the object's shape, size and velocity.]]. For the shuttlecock it is
large compared with the gravitational force, and then the formulas for
projectile motion from the previous section do not hold.
:::

The formulas for projectile motion are based on the [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] being **constant**:
$a_x = 0$ and $a_y = -g$ during the whole throw. With air resistance this no longer
holds. Air resistance depends on the speed, and the speed changes all the time
during the throw. Then the acceleration also changes all the time, both in magnitude
and direction, and there are no simple formulas for the position and the velocity.

The solution is to let the calculation take **small steps in time**. During a very
short time interval the acceleration hardly has time to change, so there we can
calculate as if it were constant. Then we take the next step, and the next, all the way
until the object lands. Such a way of calculating is called a **numerical method**,
and calculating a motion step by step in this way is called
**simulating** the motion. This is how most computer simulations of motion work,
including several of the simulations on this site.

## The idea: divide time into short steps

From Physics level 1 you recognise the relationships for average velocity and
average acceleration:

$$
v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} \qquad\qquad a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
$$

Rewriting them, we get how much the position and the velocity **change** during
the time $\Delta t$:

$$
\Delta s = v \cdot \Delta t \qquad\qquad \Delta v = a \cdot \Delta t
$$

If the time step $\Delta t$ is short, the velocity and the acceleration change only
a little during the step. Then we can use the values at the **beginning** of the step to
calculate the change. The new position is the old position plus the change, and
the new velocity is the old velocity plus the change. Just as in
the previous section, we split the motion into the *x*-direction and the *y*-direction and calculate
them separately.

In the figure below, the same throw is calculated in two ways. The grey curve is the
exact parabolic trajectory. The blue path is calculated with the time step
$\Delta t = 0.2\ \mathrm{s}$: in each step the ball is moved straight ahead in the
direction the velocity had at the beginning of the step. The path therefore becomes a chain of
straight segments.

The same principle applies in every step: new value = old value + the change
during the time step. The formulas below go from step number $n$ to step number
$n + 1$.

::: formel "The step method (Euler's method)"
$$
\begin{aligned}
t_{n+1} &= t_n + \Delta t \\
x_{n+1} &= x_n + v_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t \\
y_{n+1} &= y_n + v_{y,n} \cdot \Delta t \\
v_{x,n+1} &= v_{x,n} + a_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t \\
v_{y,n+1} &= v_{y,n} + a_{y,n} \cdot \Delta t
\end{aligned}
$$

where

- *t* = time (s)
- $\Delta t$ = time step (s)
- *x*, *y* = position in the *x*-/*y*-direction (m)
- $v_x$, $v_y$ = velocity in the *x*-/*y*-direction (m/s)
- $a_x$, $a_y$ = acceleration in the *x*-/*y*-direction (m/s²)
- *n* = number of the step ($n = 0$ at the start)

::: härledning "Derivation: The step method"
The average velocity in the *x*-direction during a time step is

$$
v_x = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \Delta x = v_x \cdot \Delta t
$$

The change in position is the difference between the new and the old position,
$\Delta x = x_{n+1} - x_n$. We replace the average velocity during the step with
the velocity at the beginning of the step, $v_{x,n}$. This is an approximation, which becomes
better the shorter the time step is. Then

$$
x_{n+1} - x_n = v_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x_{n+1} = x_n + v_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t
$$

In the same way, the average acceleration in the *x*-direction gives

$$
a_x = \frac{\Delta v_x}{\Delta t} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \Delta v_x = a_x \cdot \Delta t
$$

and with the acceleration at the beginning of the step, $a_{x,n}$, we get

$$
v_{x,n+1} - v_{x,n} = a_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v_{x,n+1} = v_{x,n} + a_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t
$$

The formulas in the *y*-direction are derived in the same way.

QED.
:::
:::

The step method is also called **Euler's method**, after the Swiss
mathematician Leonhard Euler (1707–1783). It gives an **approximate value**, not an
exact value: during the whole step it uses the velocity and the acceleration
as they were at the beginning of the step. You can see that error in the figure above, where the blue
path lies a little above the grey one the whole time. The example below shows why.

::: exempel "Example 1: Two steps by hand"
**A ball is thrown obliquely upwards from the ground. The initial velocity is $8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the *x*-direction and $6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the *y*-direction. Ignore air resistance.**

**a) Use the step method with the time step $\Delta t = 0.10\ \mathrm{s}$ and determine the position of the ball after $0.20\ \mathrm{s}$.**
**b) Determine the position of the ball after $0.20\ \mathrm{s}$ with the formulas for projectile motion. How large is the error of the step method in the *y*-direction?**

::: handskrift
typ: stegtva
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We put the origin at the launch point and call the start step 0. Without
air resistance $a_x = 0$, so the velocity in the *x*-direction does not change. In the *y*-direction
$a_y = -g$.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
x_0 = 0 \\
y_0 = 0 \\
v_x = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
v_{y,0} = 6.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
a_y = -g \approx -9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \\
\Delta t = 0.10\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

**Step 1**, to $t = 0.10\ \mathrm{s}$. The new position is calculated with
the velocity at the beginning of the step:

$$
x_1 = x_0 + v_x \cdot \Delta t = 0 + 8.0 \cdot 0.10 = 0.80\ \mathrm{m}
$$

$$
y_1 = y_0 + v_{y,0} \cdot \Delta t = 0 + 6.0 \cdot 0.10 = 0.60\ \mathrm{m}
$$

During the step, the velocity in the *y*-direction has decreased because of
the acceleration due to gravity:

$$
v_{y,1} = v_{y,0} + a_y \cdot \Delta t = 6.0 + (-9.82) \cdot 0.10 = 5.018\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

**Step 2**, to $t = 0.20\ \mathrm{s}$. Now we continue from
the values after step 1:

$$
x_2 = x_1 + v_x \cdot \Delta t = 0.80 + 8.0 \cdot 0.10 = 1.60\ \mathrm{m}
$$

$$
y_2 = y_1 + v_{y,1} \cdot \Delta t = 0.60 + 5.018 \cdot 0.10 = 1.1018\ \mathrm{m} \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{m}
$$

**Answer:** $x = 1.6\ \mathrm{m}$ and $y = 1.1\ \mathrm{m}$

**b)** The components of the initial velocity are $v_0 \cdot \cos \alpha = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$
and $v_0 \cdot \sin \alpha = 6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so the formulas for the position are

$$
x = v_x \cdot t \qquad\qquad y = v_{y,0} \cdot t - \frac{g \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
v_x = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
v_{y,0} = 6.0\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \\
t = 0.20\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
x = 8.0 \cdot 0.20 = 1.6\ \mathrm{m}
$$

$$
y = 6.0 \cdot 0.20 - \frac{9.82 \cdot 0.20^{2}}{2} = 1.0036\ \mathrm{m} \approx 1.0\ \mathrm{m}
$$

The error of the step method in the *y*-direction is the difference between the two values:

$$
1.1018 - 1.0036 = 0.0982\ \mathrm{m} \approx 0.10\ \mathrm{m}
$$

In the *x*-direction the step method is exact, since the velocity in the *x*-direction is constant.
In the *y*-direction there is an error, since the ball is being braked all the time on its way up, while
the step method uses the velocity at the beginning of the step during the whole step. So it
lets the ball rise a little too fast.

**Answer:** $x = 1.6\ \mathrm{m}$ and $y = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. The step method gives *y* about $0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ too high.
:::
:::

Calculating two steps by hand works well, but a whole throw requires many steps.
Every step is exactly the same calculation, and then a spreadsheet is a perfect fit.
Each row in the sheet becomes a step, and the formulas in a row take their values
from the row above. In the spreadsheet below we continue the throw in Example 1

In the spreadsheet above, the time step 0.1 is written directly into the formulas. If
you want to try a different time step, you then have to change every formula. It is easier
to write the time step in a cell of its own, for example G2, and refer to it
with dollar signs: `=B2+D2*$G$2`. The dollar signs make the reference stay
fixed when the formula is copied downwards. The same goes for the acceleration due to gravity
9.82 and, when you add air resistance, the mass and the constant *k*.
:::

## How short should the time step be?

The shorter the time step, the less the velocity and the acceleration have time to
change during a step, and the smaller the error. The price is more steps. In
the table, the throw in Example 1 has been calculated with the step method for a few different
time steps. The landing point has been determined by drawing a straight line between
the two points closest to the ground.

| Time step $\Delta t$ | Number of steps | Range | Maximum height |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.2 s | 8 | 11.36 m | 2.44 m |
| 0.1 s | 14 | 10.57 m | 2.14 m |
| 0.05 s | 26 | 10.17 m | 1.99 m |
| 0.01 s | 124 | 9.86 m | 1.86 m |
| 0.001 s | 1 223 | 9.78 m | 1.84 m |
| Exact (the formulas) | | 9.78 m | 1.83 m |

Notice that the error in the range is roughly **halved** when the time step is
halved: 1.58 m, 0.79 m, 0.40 m. This is typical of the step method. With a
computer the number of steps does not matter, so in practice you choose a very
short time step.

::: tips "How to know that the simulation calculates correctly"
1. **Test the model on a case with a known answer.** Run the simulation without
   air resistance and compare with the formulas for projectile motion. If they do not agree,
   something is wrong in the sheet or the program.
2. **Halve the time step and run again.** If the result does not change in the
   significant figures you care about, the time step is short enough.
3. **Assess the reasonableness.** With air resistance the throw should be shorter and lower
   than without, never longer.
:::

## Air resistance

The air resistance on a ball is **directed opposite to the velocity**, that is, directed the opposite way to the motion. It depends on
the ball's shape and size and on the density of the air, but above all on
the speed. For most balls in air, the air resistance grows with the square of
the speed: twice the speed gives four times the air resistance.

::: formel "Air resistance"
$$
F_\text{luft} = k \cdot v^{2}
$$

where

- $F_\text{luft}$ = air resistance (N)
- *k* = a constant that depends on the object's shape and size and on the air (kg/m)
- *v* = speed (m/s)
:::

::: kuriosa "What determines the constant $k$?"
The constant can be written $k = \dfrac{\rho \cdot C \cdot A}{2}$, where $\rho$ is
the density of the air (about $1.2\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$), *A* is the ball's
cross-sectional area and *C* is a number without a unit that depends on the shape, about
0.5 for a ball. For a tennis ball, $k \approx 0.0012\ \mathrm{kg/m}$.
A shuttlecock has a *k* that is roughly as large, but weighs only
5 grams compared with the tennis ball's 57 grams. That is why air resistance brakes it
so much more.
:::

During the throw, two forces act on the ball: the gravitational force $F_G$ straight down
and the air resistance $F_\text{luft}$ directed opposite to the velocity. The figure shows them
to scale for the tennis ball in Example 2 below, where the air resistance at the start
is almost twice as large as the gravitational force.

To be able to use the step method we need the acceleration in the *x*-direction and
the *y*-direction. We therefore split the air resistance into components and use
Newton's second law in each direction separately.

::: formel "Acceleration with air resistance"
$$
a_x = -\frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_x}{m}
$$

$$
a_y = -g - \frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_y}{m}
$$

where

- $a_x$, $a_y$ = acceleration in the *x*-/*y*-direction (m/s²)
- *k* = the air resistance constant (kg/m)
- *v* = speed, $v = \sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}}$ (m/s)
- $v_x$, $v_y$ = velocity in the *x*-/*y*-direction (m/s)
- *m* = mass (kg)
- *g* = acceleration due to gravity (m/s²)

::: härledning "Derivation: Acceleration with air resistance"
The air resistance is directed opposite to the velocity. The velocity and
the air resistance therefore each form a right-angled triangle with their
components, and the triangles have the same angle $\alpha$. They are similar.

In similar triangles, the ratio between corresponding sides is equal:

$$
\frac{F_{\text{luft},x}}{F_\text{luft}} = \frac{v_x}{v} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_{\text{luft},x} = F_\text{luft} \cdot \frac{v_x}{v}
$$

With $F_\text{luft} = k \cdot v^{2}$ and a minus sign, since
the component is directed opposite to $v_x$, this becomes

$$
F_{\text{luft},x} = -k \cdot v^{2} \cdot \frac{v_x}{v} = -k \cdot v \cdot v_x
$$

In the same way, $F_{\text{luft},y} = -k \cdot v \cdot v_y$.

**In the *x*-direction**, the air resistance is the only force. Newton's second law gives

$$
m \cdot a_x = -k \cdot v \cdot v_x \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a_x = -\frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_x}{m}
$$

**In the *y*-direction**, both the gravitational force $-m \cdot g$ and the air resistance act:

$$
m \cdot a_y = -m \cdot g - k \cdot v \cdot v_y \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a_y = -g - \frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_y}{m}
$$

QED.
:::
:::

The minus signs automatically give the right direction. On the way up, $v_y$ is positive,
and the air resistance pulls the ball downwards together with the gravitational force. On the way
down, $v_y$ is negative, so the term $-\dfrac{k \cdot v \cdot v_y}{m}$ becomes
positive: the air resistance brakes the fall.

Each step in the simulation is now done in four parts:

1. Calculate the speed $v = \sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}}$ with the velocity at the beginning of the step.
2. Calculate the acceleration $a_x$ and $a_y$ with the formulas above.
3. Calculate the new velocity with the step method.
4. Calculate the new position with the step method.

::: exempel "Example 2: Tennis ball with air resistance"
**A tennis ball with mass $57\ \mathrm{g}$ is hit with velocity $24\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the *x*-direction and $18\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the *y*-direction. The air resistance is $F_\text{luft} = k \cdot v^{2}$ with $k = 0.0012\ \mathrm{kg/m}$.**

**a) How large is the air resistance at the moment it is hit? Compare with the gravitational force.**
**b) Determine the acceleration in the *x*-direction and the *y*-direction at the moment it is hit.**
**c) Determine the velocity in the *x*-direction and the *y*-direction after the first time step, $\Delta t = 0.050\ \mathrm{s}$.**

::: handskrift
typ: stegluft
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The air resistance depends on the speed, so we calculate that first.

$$
v = \sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}} = \sqrt{24^{2} + 18^{2}} = 30\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

$$
F_\text{luft} = k \cdot v^{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 0.0012\ \mathrm{kg/m} \\
v = 30\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_\text{luft} = 0.0012 \cdot 30^{2} = 1.08\ \mathrm{N} \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{N}
$$

The gravitational force is

$$
F_G = m \cdot g = 0.057 \cdot 9.82 = 0.55974\ \mathrm{N} \approx 0.56\ \mathrm{N}
$$

**Answer:** The air resistance is $1.1\ \mathrm{N}$, almost twice as large as the gravitational force ($0.56\ \mathrm{N}$).

**b)** We use the formulas for the acceleration with air resistance.

$$
a_x = -\frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_x}{m} \qquad\qquad a_y = -g - \frac{k \cdot v \cdot v_y}{m}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 0.0012\ \mathrm{kg/m} \\
v = 30\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
v_x = 24\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
v_y = 18\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
m = 57\ \mathrm{g} = 0.057\ \mathrm{kg} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
a_x = -\frac{0.0012 \cdot 30 \cdot 24}{0.057} = -15.157\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \approx -15\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

$$
a_y = -9.82 - \frac{0.0012 \cdot 30 \cdot 18}{0.057} = -21.188\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \approx -21\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

**Answer:** $a_x = -15\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ and $a_y = -21\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}$

**c)** The step method gives the new velocity. We use the acceleration from
b), that is, the acceleration at the beginning of the step.

$$
v_{x,1} = v_{x,0} + a_x \cdot \Delta t = 24 + (-15.157\ldots) \cdot 0.050 = 23.242\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 23\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

$$
v_{y,1} = v_{y,0} + a_y \cdot \Delta t = 18 + (-21.188\ldots) \cdot 0.050 = 16.940\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 17\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

Both components have decreased. Without air resistance, $v_x$ would have stayed at
$24\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

**Answer:** $v_x = 23\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_y = 17\ \mathrm{m/s}$
:::
:::

The next step starts with the new velocities. The speed has then decreased a little, so
the air resistance and the acceleration become a little smaller. And so it continues, step
by step, until the ball lands. That is the calculation the simulation below does.

::: kuriosa "The step method is everywhere"
Numerical methods of this kind are used wherever motion needs to be
predicted: in the physics engines of computer games, in weather forecasts and when
space probes are sent to other planets. There, refined variants are used that
give smaller errors per step, but the basic idea is the same: new value = old
value + the change during the step.

In the film *Hidden Figures* (2016), the mathematician Katherine Johnson works
out how the astronaut John Glenn's space capsule will make its way back to Earth.
When no formula is enough, she finds the solution in Euler's method, that is, the same
method as in this section. The scene is dramatised, but Johnson
really did calculate trajectories for NASA's first crewed space flights.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Why numerical?"
- The formulas for projectile motion require **constant acceleration**.
- With **air resistance**, the acceleration changes all the time.
- A **numerical method** calculates the motion in **small time steps**:
  **simulating** the motion.
:::

::: sampunkt "The step method (Euler's method)"
- New value = old value + the change.
- $x_{n+1} = x_n + v_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t$
- $v_{x,n+1} = v_{x,n} + a_{x,n} \cdot \Delta t$
- The same in the *y*-direction. The values at the **beginning of the step** are used.
:::

::: sampunkt "The time step"
- The method gives an **approximate value**.
- A halved time step gives roughly a **halved error**.
- **Check:** compare with the formulas without air resistance, and halve
  the time step until the result does not change.
:::

::: sampunkt "Air resistance"
