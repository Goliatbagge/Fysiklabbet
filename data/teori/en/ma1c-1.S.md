# Summary: Arithmetic

This chapter was about the basics of arithmetic: the number sets and
negative numbers, calculating with fractions (expanding, simplifying, addition,
subtraction, multiplication and division), numbers in decimal form (rounding
and significant figures), powers with whole, negative and rational exponents,
standard form and prefixes, and the order in which the operations are to be carried out.
After the chapter you should be able to calculate confidently by hand with fractions and powers,
round answers to the right number of significant figures or decimal places, and interpret numbers
written in standard form or with prefixes.

## Concepts to know

- **number set**: a collection of numbers with a common property: natural numbers
  ($\mathbb{N}$), integers ($\mathbb{Z}$), rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$) and
  real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$); each set lies inside the next.
- **irrational numbers**: numbers that cannot be written as a fraction of two
  integers, for example π and $\sqrt{2}$; they have an infinite decimal expansion that
  does not repeat.
- **numerator and denominator**: the number above and below the fraction bar in
  a fraction, respectively.
- **expanding and simplifying**: multiplying and dividing, respectively, the
  numerator and denominator by the same number, without changing the value of the fraction.
- **lowest common denominator (LCD)**: the smallest denominator that several fractions
  can be written with at the same time.
- **mixed form**: a fraction written as a whole number plus a fraction, for example
  $3\frac{3}{4}$, as opposed to fraction form ($\dfrac{15}{4}$).
- **approximate value and rounding digit**: a rounded value is called an
  approximate value; the digit that decides the rounding is called the rounding digit.
- **significant figures**: a measure of how precisely an approximate value
  is given; decides how much an answer should be rounded.
- **power, base and exponent**: in $a^n$, $a$ is called the base (what is
  raised to a power) and $n$ the exponent (what states how many times).
- **power form and factor form**: a number written with a base and an exponent
  ($3^5$) and written as a product of factors
  ($3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$), respectively.
- **rational exponent**: an exponent that is a fraction, for example
  $a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$.
- **power of ten and standard form**: a power of ten is a power of 10; a
  number of the form $a \cdot 10^n$ where $1 \leq a < 10$ is written in
  standard form.
- **prefix**: a letter that replaces a power of ten in front of a unit, for example
  k (kilo, $10^3$), M (mega, $10^6$) and m (milli, $10^{-3}$).
- **order of operations**: the order in which the operations are to be carried out:
  brackets, powers, multiplication/division, addition/subtraction.

## Formulas

::: formel "The chapter's formulas"
**Rules for negative numbers**

$$
a + (-b) = a - b \qquad\qquad a - (-b) = a + b
$$

$$
a \cdot (-b) = -(a \cdot b) \qquad\qquad (-a) \cdot (-b) = a \cdot b
$$

Like signs together (minus/minus or plus/plus) give plus. Different signs together
(plus/minus) give minus. The same rule applies to division.

**Addition and subtraction of fractions**

$$
\frac{a}{c} \pm \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a \pm b}{c}
$$

The fractions must have the same denominator before they are added or subtracted; otherwise
you expand them until the denominators are equal.

**Multiplication and division of fractions**

$$
\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d}
\qquad\qquad
\frac{a}{b} \Big/ \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c}
$$

When dividing, the fraction in the denominator is inverted (numerator and denominator swap
places), and then the fractions are multiplied as usual.

**The laws of exponents**

$$
a^x \cdot a^y = a^{x+y}
\qquad
\frac{a^x}{a^y} = a^{x-y}
\qquad
(a^x)^y = a^{x \cdot y}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{\!x} = \frac{a^x}{b^x}
\qquad\qquad
(ab)^x = a^x \cdot b^x
$$

**The exponent zero and negative exponents**

$$
a^0 = 1
\qquad\qquad
a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}
\qquad\qquad
\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-1} = \frac{b}{a}
$$

where $a \neq 0$ in all the expressions.

**Rational exponents**

$$
a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}
$$

**Standard form**

$$
a \cdot 10^{n}, \quad \text{where } 1 \leq a < 10 \text{ and } n \text{ is an integer}
$$

**Order of operations**

1. **Brackets**
2. **Powers**: $a^b$
3. **Multiplication and division**: $\cdot$ and /
4. **Addition and subtraction**: + and −
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- When subtracting a positive number from a negative one (for example
  $(-5) - 3$) it is easy to mix it up with "minus times minus". Think of
  the thermometer: −5 degrees that falls by another 3 degrees becomes −8 degrees, not 8.
- Comparing two fractions is most easily done by converting them to the same
  denominator. The fraction with the largest numerator is then the largest.
- To find a common denominator: first try expanding the fraction that
  has the smallest denominator (method 1); if that does not work, expand both fractions by
  each other's denominators (method 2). The LCD table method (list multiples of both
  denominators) gives the lowest common denominator.
- You may only cancel identical factors in the numerator and denominator when there is a
  **multiplication sign** between them, never when there is a plus or
  minus sign between them.
- If the numbers become large when multiplying fractions: factorise the numerator and
  denominator and simplify *before* the multiplication is carried out.
- A fraction *of* something is calculated by **multiplying** by
  the fraction, for example $\dfrac{5}{7}$ of 63 kr $= \dfrac{5}{7} \cdot 63$.
- Distinguish between $(-9)^2$ and $-9^2$: in the first case the whole of $-9$ is the base
  ($(-9)^2 = 81$), in the second only 9 is the base and the minus sign is
  outside the power ($-9^2 = -81$).
- When **multiplying and dividing** measured values, the number with the **fewest
  significant figures** decides how many significant figures the answer should be rounded to.
  When **adding and subtracting**, the number with the **fewest
  decimal places** instead decides how many decimal places the answer should have.
- To solve a power equation with the same base (for example $8^m \cdot 8^5 = 8^7$):
  rewrite both sides as a single power with the same base, then set
  the exponents equal to each other and solve for the unknown.
- When simplifying fraction expressions with powers: rewrite the numerator and denominator
  as *a single* power each, with the same base. Then the expression is easy
  to simplify with the laws of exponents.
- When changing to another prefix: if the exponent of the power of ten increases, the number
  in front of the power of ten must become smaller to the same degree (and vice versa) so that the
  size of the number does not change.
- In a division, the whole numerator and the whole denominator work as if they were
  inside "invisible brackets". On the calculator these brackets must be written
  out explicitly, otherwise the calculator calculates incorrectly.

## Before the test

- Can you describe the four number sets ($\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$,
  $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$) and how they lie inside each other?
- Do you know the rules for negative numbers ("minus minus gives plus", "plus
  minus gives minus"), and can you tell $(-5) - 3$ from $(-5) \cdot (-3)$?
- Can you expand and simplify fractions, and do you know that you may only cancel
  factors that have a multiplication sign between them?
- Can you add and subtract fractions with different denominators by finding a
  common denominator?
- Can you multiply and divide fractions, and do you know why division
  means that you invert the fraction in the denominator?
- Can you calculate a fraction of a number, for example how much $\dfrac{5}{7}$ of
  63 kr is?
- Can you round numbers according to the rounding rule, and do you know the difference
  between rounding to significant figures (multiplication/division) and
  to decimal places (addition/subtraction)?
- Do you know the laws of exponents by heart, and can you use them when simplifying and in
  power equations with the same base?
- Do you know what $a^0$ and $a^{-n}$ mean, and can you rewrite fractions with
  negative exponents?
- Can you interpret a rational exponent as a root, for example
  $a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$?
- Can you write numbers in standard form and switch between powers of ten and
  prefixes (T, G, M, k, d, c, m, µ, n, p)?
- Do you know the order of operations, and can you evaluate expressions with several operations in
  the right order, including "invisible brackets" in fractions?
