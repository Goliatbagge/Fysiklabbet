# Summary: Integrals

This chapter was about integrals: how to find antiderivatives,
calculate definite integrals with the fundamental theorem of
calculus, and use integrals to calculate areas, volumes and
probabilities. You have seen how the integral is interpreted as an area under a
curve, how it is linked to rates of change (velocity, force,
power) and how it is used to calculate the volume of solids of revolution with
the disc method. After the chapter you should be able to find antiderivatives,
calculate definite integrals and areas, also between two curves, and
set up and interpret integrals in applications such as distance, work,
energy, probability and volume.

## Concepts to know

- **antiderivative ($F$)**: a function whose derivative is the
  original function, $F'(x) = f(x)$; finding it is the reverse
  operation of differentiation.
- **the constant $C$**: added when giving *all* antiderivatives,
  $F(x) + C$, since a constant disappears when differentiated.
- **definite integral**: the integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ with given
  limits; gives a number, interpreted as an area.
- **integrand**: the function $f(x)$ that stands inside the integral sign and
  is to be integrated.
- **limits of integration ($a$, $b$)**: the lower and upper values that
  bound the integration.
- **the fundamental theorem of calculus**: the relationship between a definite
  integral and an antiderivative, $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$.
- **area unit (a.u.) and volume unit (v.u.)**: units used when an
  area or volume is calculated without a given length scale.
- **probability density function**: a function whose area under the graph (the integral) gives
  the probability for an interval of outcomes.
- **discrete and continuous sample space**: a finite number of
  possible outcomes (for example rolling a die) and infinitely many (for example
  all times or weights) respectively.
- **uniform distribution**: a probability density function that is constant (a straight,
  horizontal line) within an interval; the area is a rectangle.
- **normal distribution**: a symmetric bell curve about the mean $\mu$;
  has no simple antiderivative and is calculated with a digital tool.
- **exponential distribution**: a probability density function that decreases quickly from a
  high starting value towards zero; can often be integrated by hand.
- **solid of revolution**: a three-dimensional solid formed when a curve
  or a region is rotated one full turn around an axis.
- **the disc method**: a method of calculating the volume of a solid of revolution by
  adding up infinitely thin cylindrical discs with an integral.

## Formulas

::: formel "The formulas of the chapter"
**Antiderivatives: basic rules**

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) = x^n \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}, \quad n \neq -1\\[4pt]
f(x) = k \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = kx\\[4pt]
f(x) = e^{kx} \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = \frac{e^{kx}}{k}\\[4pt]
f(x) = a^{kx} \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = \frac{a^{kx}}{k\cdot \ln a}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Antiderivatives: trigonometric and 1/x**

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) = \sin kx \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = -\frac{\cos kx}{k}\\[4pt]
f(x) = \cos kx \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = \frac{\sin kx}{k}\\[4pt]
f(x) = \frac{1}{x} \quad&\Rightarrow\quad F(x) = \ln x, \quad (x > 0)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $k = 1$ gives the special cases $\sin x \Rightarrow -\cos x$ and
$\cos x \Rightarrow \sin x$. Angles are always given in radians.

**The fundamental theorem of calculus**

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b) - F(a)
$$

**Area between a curve and the *x*-axis**

$$
A = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \quad (f(x) > 0)
\qquad\qquad
A = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \quad (f(x) < 0)
$$

**Rules for integrals**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_a^c f(x)\,dx &= \int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_b^c f(x)\,dx\\[4pt]
\int_a^b \bigl(f(x) \pm g(x)\bigr)\,dx &= \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \pm \int_a^b g(x)\,dx\\[4pt]
\int_a^b k\cdot f(x)\,dx &= k\cdot \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\\[4pt]
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx &= -\int_b^a f(x)\,dx
\end{aligned}
$$

where $k$ in the third line is a constant, independent of $x$.

**Area between two curves**

$$
A = \int_a^b \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,dx
$$

where $f(x)$ is the upper function and $g(x)$ the lower one.

**The unit of the integral and four common applications**

$$
\text{unit of the } y\text{-axis} \cdot \text{unit of the } x\text{-axis} = \text{unit of the integral}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Change in position:} \qquad && \Delta s &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt\\[4pt]
\text{Change in velocity:} \qquad && \Delta v &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt\\[4pt]
\text{Work:} \qquad && W &= \int_{s_1}^{s_2} F(s)\,ds\\[4pt]
\text{Energy consumption:} \qquad && E &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} P(t)\,dt
\end{aligned}
$$

**Probability density function**

$$
P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx
$$

$$
f(x) \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1
$$

**Volume for rotation about the *x*-axis**

$$
V = \pi\int_a^b y^2\, dx
$$

**Volume for rotation about the *y*-axis**

$$
V = \pi\int_a^b x^2\, dy
$$

where $x$ must be expressed as a function of $y$ (the inverse of the curve) before
the integral is calculated.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- An integral can be negative if the graph lies below the *x*-axis, but an
  area can never be negative. If $f(x) < 0$ in the interval, the
  area is calculated as $-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.
- If the region lies partly above and partly below the *x*-axis (or the
  upper/lower function changes along the way), the calculation must be split into several
  integrals, one for each subinterval.
- The rules let you split an integral into subintervals
  ($a \to b \to c$), integrate sums and differences term by term, and take
  out a constant factor in front of the integral sign. The constant must not
  contain $x$.
- If you swap the limits of integration, the integral changes sign:
  $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = -\int_b^a f(x)\,dx$.
- To find the area between two curves: set the function expressions equal and
  solve the equation. The $x$-coordinates of the points of intersection become the
  limits of integration. Always check which function is the upper
  and which is the lower in the interval, and simplify the expressions before you
  integrate.
- The unit of the integral is always the product of the units of the axes (the unit of the $y$-axis
  times the unit of the $x$-axis). This reveals which quantity the integral
  (the area) actually stands for.
- Four common applications are built on the same idea: the integral of a
  rate of change gives the accumulated quantity (velocity →
  distance, acceleration → change in velocity, force → work, power →
  energy).
- In a continuous sample space (for example time or weight) the probability
  of an exact value is always 0. That is why the probability
  of an interval is calculated instead, as the area under the probability density function.
- A probability density function must be non-negative everywhere, and the total
  area under the whole curve must be exactly 1 (100 %).
- For rotation about the *x*-axis the formula $V = \pi\int_a^b y^2\,dx$ is used
  directly; for rotation about the *y*-axis the curve must first be solved for $x$
  in terms of $y$ (the inverse) before the formula $V = \pi\int_a^b x^2\,dy$
  is used.
- If the limits of integration are missing in a task about an area or a solid of revolution:
  look for the zeros of the function (intersections with the *x*-axis) or
  the points of intersection between the curves to find them.
- With a digital tool (for example GeoGebra) area and volume can often
  be calculated directly. The command `IntegralBetween(upper function, lower
  function, start x-value, end x-value)` gives the area between two curves, and
  the probability calculator is used for normal distributions that have no simple
  antiderivative.

## Before the test

- Can you explain what an antiderivative is and the notation
  $f'' \to f' \to f \to F$?
- Can you find an antiderivative of power, exponential and
  trigonometric functions, and of $\dfrac{1}{x}$?
- Do you know the difference between giving **one** antiderivative and
  **all** antiderivatives (the constant $C$)?
- Do you know the fundamental theorem of calculus and can you use it to
  calculate a definite integral?
- Do you know why an integral can be negative but an area never can,
  and how to handle a region that lies both above and below the
  *x*-axis?
- Do you know the rules for integrals: splitting by interval,
  integrating sums and differences term by term, and taking out a constant
  factor?
- Can you calculate the area between two curves, including finding the
  points of intersection and deciding which function is the upper and which
  the lower?
- Do you know how to split an area into several integrals when more than two
  curves bound the region?
- Can you determine which quantity an integral (area) stands for by
  multiplying together the units of the axes?
- Can you set up and use the formulas $\Delta s = \int v(t)\,dt$,
  $\Delta v = \int a(t)\,dt$, $W = \int F(s)\,ds$ and $E = \int P(t)\,dt$?
- Do you know what a probability density function is, which two requirements it must meet, and
  how it differs between the uniform, normal and exponential distributions?
- Can you derive and use the disc method to calculate the volume of a
  solid of revolution about both the *x*-axis and the *y*-axis, including solving
  for $x$ as a function of $y$?
