# Phases and phase changes

All substances can be in three different states: solid, liquid and gas. These
states are called **phases** or **states of matter**. The changes
between different phases have different names. Learn them!

## Melting and freezing

It takes energy to melt a substance, but no [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance are moving. SI unit kelvin (K).]] change takes place.
Instead, the energy is used to break chemical bonds. What determines how
much energy is needed to melt a substance is the mass and which substance
we have.

How much energy is needed to melt 1 kg of a substance is called the
**specific latent heat of fusion** (specific enthalpy of fusion), written $l_s$, and
has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] J/kg (joules per kilogram). The specific latent heat of fusion
varies between different substances and is looked up in a table of thermal data. The same energy that
is **absorbed** during melting is **given off** during freezing.

::: formel "Energy for melting and freezing"
$$
E = l_s \cdot m
$$

where

- *E* = melting energy (J)
- $l_s$ = specific latent heat of fusion (J/kg), looked up in a table
- *m* = mass (kg)
:::

The specific latent heat of fusion of ice is 334 kJ/kg (even though the table gives it for
ice at a temperature of −4 °C, we can assume that it applies to all ice).

## Vaporisation and condensation

It takes energy to vaporise a substance, but no temperature change takes place.
Instead, the energy is used to tear molecules loose from the liquid. Just
as for melting, it is the mass and which substance we have that determine how much
energy is needed for the vaporisation.

How much energy is needed to vaporise 1 kg of a substance is called the
**specific latent heat of vaporisation** (specific enthalpy of vaporisation),
written $l_å$, and has the SI unit J/kg (joules per kilogram). Just as
for the specific latent heat of fusion, it varies for different substances and is looked up in a
table.

::: formel "Energy for vaporisation/condensation"
$$
E = l_å \cdot m
$$

where

- *E* = vaporisation energy (J)
- $l_å$ = specific latent heat of vaporisation (J/kg), looked up in a table
- *m* = mass (kg)
:::

The same energy that is absorbed during vaporisation is given off during condensation. The specific
latent heat of vaporisation of water is 2.26 MJ/kg (notice in the table that
the latent heat of vaporisation has a different prefix from the other latent heats).

::: demo "Bursting a water balloon with a candle?"
We fill a balloon with water and place it over a candle flame. Rubber
melts at about 200 °C and the candle flame is about 1 000 °C. Despite the hot
candle flame, the balloon does not burst. This is because the temperature of
the water in the balloon will never exceed 100 °C, and the water will therefore
cool the rubber and keep it below its melting point.
:::

When we supply energy to ice below zero, the temperature rises at first. At
0 °C the curve becomes horizontal during the melting, and at 100 °C horizontal
again during the vaporisation. So the temperature does not change during a phase change.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Melting tin"
**How much energy is needed to melt 50 grams of tin at a temperature of 20 °C?**

::: handskrift
typ: smaltatenn
:::

::: textlosning
To melt the tin, energy is first needed for **heating**, $E_u$, and then
energy for **melting**, $E_s$. We write the total energy *E* as the
sum of these.

$$
E = E_u + E_s = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T + l_s \cdot m
$$

To know how much heating $\Delta T$ is needed, we have to find out
the melting point of tin. We look it up in a table and see that it is 232 °C.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
c = 0.23\ \mathrm{kJ/(kg \cdot K)}\ (\text{spec. heat cap. of tin}) \\
m = 50\ \mathrm{g} = 0.050\ \mathrm{kg} \\
\Delta T = 232 - 20 = 212\ \mathrm{K} \\
l_s = 59\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}\ (\text{spec. latent heat of fusion of tin})
\end{array} \right]
$$

Note that we give the heat capacity and latent heat in kJ, so the energy in the answer will
have the unit kJ.

$$
E = 0.23 \cdot 0.050 \cdot 212 + 59 \cdot 0.050 = 5.388\ \mathrm{kJ} \approx 5.4\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

**Answer:** 5.4 kJ
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Hot water on ice"
**You pour 1.0 litre of water at 56 degrees into a bucket with 2.0 kg of ice at a
temperature of −8 °C. How much ice will the water melt?**

::: handskrift
typ: varmvattenis
:::

::: textlosning
$$
E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp} \qquad (1)
$$

The hot water gives off the energy $E_\text{avg}$ and will cool down
($c_v \cdot m_v \cdot \Delta T_v$). The ice absorbs the energy $E_\text{upp}$ and
will be warmed up ($c_\text{ice} \cdot m_\text{ice} \cdot \Delta T_\text{ice}$)
**and** partly melt ($l_s \cdot m_\text{melted}$). So we call the mass of the ice that
melts $m_\text{melted}$, and that is what we are looking for.

Substitution into (1), $E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp}$, gives

$$
c_v \cdot m_v \cdot \Delta T_v
= c_\text{ice} \cdot m_\text{ice} \cdot \Delta T_\text{ice} + l_s \cdot m_\text{melted}
$$

We solve for $m_\text{melted}$ and get

$$
m_\text{melted} = \frac{c_v \cdot m_v \cdot \Delta T_v - c_\text{ice} \cdot m_\text{ice} \cdot \Delta T_\text{ice}}{l_s}
$$

Since part of the ice will melt, the final temperature of the mixture
(water + ice) will be 0 °C. We use this final temperature to
determine $\Delta T$ for the water and the ice.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
c_v = 4.18\ \mathrm{kJ/(kg \cdot K)} \\
m_v = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg} \\
\Delta T_v = 56 - 0 = 56\ \mathrm{K} \\
c_\text{ice} = 2.2\ \mathrm{kJ/(kg \cdot K)} \\
m_\text{ice} = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg} \\
\Delta T_\text{ice} = 0 - (-8) = 8\ \mathrm{K} \\
l_s = 334\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
m_\text{melted} = \frac{4.18 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 56 - 2.2 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 8}{334}
= 0.595\ldots\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 0.60\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

**Answer:** 0.60 kg
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Phases"
- The three **phases** or states of matter: solid, liquid and
  gas.
- The changes: **melting** and **freezing**, **vaporisation** and
  **condensation**, **sublimation** and **deposition**.
:::

::: sampunkt "The temperature stands still during a phase change"
- During a phase change the temperature does **not** change.
- The energy goes into **breaking chemical bonds** instead of increasing
  the kinetic energy.
- In a heating graph this shows up as **horizontal plateaus** at the melting and
  boiling points.
:::

::: sampunkt "Melting and freezing"
- $E = l_s \cdot m$
- $l_s$ is the **specific latent heat of fusion** (J/kg), looked up in a table.
- As much energy as is absorbed during melting is **given off** during freezing.
- For ice: $l_s = 334\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Vaporisation and condensation"
- $E = l_å \cdot m$
- $l_å$ is the **specific latent heat of vaporisation** (J/kg).
- For water: $l_å = 2.26\ \mathrm{MJ/kg}$, almost seven times
  more than for melting.
:::

::: sampunkt "Several steps in the same task"
- If a substance is to be both heated and change phase: **add** the energies,
  $E = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T + l_s \cdot m$.
- Look up the melting or boiling point to know how large $\Delta T$ is.
- The water balloon over the candle flame does not burst: the water keeps the rubber
  below $100\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, far below the melting point of rubber.
:::
:::
