# Summary: Logarithms

This chapter was about power and exponential equations and how
logarithms are used to solve them algebraically. You have met
the common logarithm as a new operation, the three laws of logarithms and how
they can be proved, as well as how logarithms are used in real applications
such as interest and change in value. After the chapter you should be able to distinguish between
power and exponential equations, solve both types algebraically with
logarithms, handle logarithmic equations and calculate with logarithms to
bases other than 10.

## Concepts to know

- **power equation**: an equation where the variable is in the base ($x^n = a$);
  solved with the laws of exponents or root expressions.
- **exponential equation**: an equation where the variable is in the exponent
  ($a^x = b$); solved graphically or algebraically with logarithms.
- **power function ($y = C \cdot x^a$)**: the general form of a
  function where the variable $x$ is in the base.
- **exponential function ($y = C \cdot a^x$)**: the general form of
  exponential change; $C$ is the initial value and $a$ is the
  change factor.
- **change factor ($a$)**: the number each step is multiplied by;
  $a > 1$ gives an increase, $0 < a < 1$ gives a decrease.
- **common logarithm ($\lg$, the logarithm to base 10)**: the number 10 must be raised to in order to get
  a certain number $a$; $\lg a = x \iff 10^x = a$.
- **general logarithm ($\log_a b$)**: the solution to the equation
  $a^x = b$ for any base $a > 0$.
- **logarithmic form and power form**: two equivalent ways of writing
  the same relationship, $\lg x = a \iff 10^a = x$.
- **logarithmic equation**: an equation where the variable is in the logarithm
  (for example $\lg x = 3$); solved by rewriting it in power form.
- **the laws of logarithms**: three rules for the sum, difference and
  power of logarithms.

## Formulas

::: formel "The formulas of the chapter"
**Exponential change: model**

$$
y = C \cdot a^x
$$

where

- $y$ = the value after $x$ changes
- $C$ = the initial value
- $a$ = the change factor
- $x$ = the number of changes (often time)

**Common logarithm: definition**

$$
a = 10^x \iff x = \lg a \qquad \text{where } a > 0
$$

**Writing a number with base 10**

$$
a = 10^{\lg a} \qquad \text{where } a > 0
$$

**Logarithmic form and power form**

$$
\lg x = a \iff 10^a = x
$$

**General logarithms**

$$
a^x = b \iff x = \log_a b
$$

**The laws of logarithms**

$$
\lg x + \lg y = \lg xy
$$

$$
\lg x - \lg y = \lg \frac{x}{y}
$$

$$
\lg x^p = p \cdot \lg x
$$

where $x > 0$ and $y > 0$.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- First decide whether the variable is in the **base** (power equation,
  $x^n = a$) or in the **exponent** (exponential equation, $a^x = b$).
  That determines which method of solution to use.
- A power equation with an **even exponent** has two solutions, one
  positive and one negative ($\pm$ in front of the root/solution); an odd
  exponent gives only one solution.
- **Method 1 for exponential equations:** rewrite both sides with base
  10 (for example $2 = 10^{\lg 2}$), simplify with the laws of exponents and set
  the exponents equal.
- **Method 2 (faster):** take the logarithm of both sides directly (put $\lg$
  in front of the whole expression) and use the third law of logarithms to
  bring the exponent down as a factor.
- A **logarithmic equation** (the variable in the logarithm) is solved by
  rewriting it from logarithmic form to power form:
  $\lg x = a \iff 10^a = x$.
- If the equation has **several logarithmic terms**, combine them into a single
  logarithm with the laws of logarithms before the logarithm is removed from
  both sides.
- All the relationships apply only for $x > 0$ and $y > 0$. You can never take
  the logarithm of a negative number or of zero.
- In applications (interest, population growth, increase in value) the
  change factor $a$ is calculated from a given percentage: an increase of
  $p\ \%$ gives $a = 1 + \dfrac{p}{100}$, a decrease gives
  $a = 1 - \dfrac{p}{100}$.
- An answer with a logarithm left in it (for example $x = \lg 53$) is an
  **exact** answer. Only round to decimals when the question asks for
  it.
- All the laws of logarithms and the relationships with base 10 apply in the same way to
  **other bases** ($\log_a b$): only the base of the power is changed.

## Before the test

- Can you distinguish between a power equation and an exponential equation by
  deciding whether the variable is in the base or in the exponent?
- Do you know why a power equation with an even exponent has two solutions,
  one positive and one negative?
- Do you know the definition of the common logarithm, $a = 10^x \iff x = \lg a$?
- Can you rewrite a number with base 10, $a = 10^{\lg a}$, and
  explain why $a$ must be greater than 0?
- Can you solve an exponential equation by rewriting both sides with
  base 10 and setting the exponents equal?
- Can you solve an exponential equation faster by taking the logarithm of
  both sides and using the third law of logarithms?
- Can you solve a logarithmic equation by rewriting it from
  logarithmic form to power form?
- Do you know all three laws of logarithms by heart, and can you use them to
  simplify or combine logarithmic expressions?
- Can you prove (derive) one of the laws of logarithms using
  the relationship $a = 10^{\lg a}$?
- Can you solve an equation with several logarithmic terms by combining them
  into a single logarithm before you remove $\lg$?
- Can you set up and solve an application with the model
  $y = C \cdot a^x$, for example interest or an increase in value?
- Can you solve equations with bases other than 10, for example $3^x = 25$, using
  general logarithms $\log_a b$?
