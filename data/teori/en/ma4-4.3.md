# Quadratic equations with complex roots

We solve a complete quadratic equation as usual with the *pq* formula.
The difference now is that we allow the expression under the root sign to become negative.
Then the roots become **complex** instead of the equation having no solution.

::: formel "The *pq* formula"
$$
x^2 + px + q = 0
$$

has the solutions

$$
x = -\frac{p}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q}
$$
:::

The number of solutions depends on the sign of the **discriminant**, that is the number
$\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q$ under the root sign:

- if the discriminant is **positive**, the equation has two real solutions,
- if it is **zero**, the equation has one real solution (a double root),
- if it is **negative**, the equation has two non-real (complex) solutions.

The solutions are also called *roots*. If the discriminant is negative, the two
roots are each other's complex conjugates, that is $a + bi$ and $a - bi$.

::: exempel "Example 1: Solve quadratic equations with complex roots"
**Solve the equations<br>a)&nbsp;$z^2 - 4z + 13 = 0$&emsp;&emsp;b)&nbsp;$z^2 - 4iz - 5 = 0$**

::: handskrift
typ: komplexarotter
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** Here $p = -4$ and $q = 13$. The *pq* formula gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= 2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 13}\\
z &= 2 \pm \sqrt{-9}\\
z &= 2 \pm 3i
\end{aligned}
$$

The discriminant was negative, so we get two complex conjugate roots.

**Answer:** $z = 2 \pm 3i$

**b)** The *pq* formula also works when the coefficients themselves are complex. Here
$p = -4i$ and $q = -5$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= 2i \pm \sqrt{(2i)^2 + 5}\\
z &= 2i \pm \sqrt{4i^2 + 5}\\
z &= 2i \pm \sqrt{-4 + 5}\\
z &= 2i \pm \sqrt{1}\\
z &= 2i \pm 1
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer:** $z = 2i \pm 1$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Find an unknown coefficient from a root"
**Find the number $a$ so that the equation $x^2 - 6x + a = 0$ has the root
$x_1 = 3 - 4i$. Also find the other root of the equation.**

::: handskrift
typ: okandkoefficient
:::

::: textlosning
Since $x_1 = 3 - 4i$ is a solution, we can substitute it into the equation to
find $a$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(3 - 4i)^2 - 6(3 - 4i) + a &= 0\\
9 - 24i + 16i^2 - 18 + 24i + a &= 0\\
9 - 24i - 16 - 18 + 24i + a &= 0\\
-25 + a &= 0\\
a &= 25
\end{aligned}
$$

We now substitute $a = 25$ into the original equation and solve it with
the *pq* formula to get the other root:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2 - 6x + 25 &= 0\\
x &= 3 \pm \sqrt{3^2 - 25}\\
x &= 3 \pm \sqrt{-16}\\
x &= 3 \pm 4i
\end{aligned}
$$

So the roots are $x_1 = 3 - 4i$ and $x_2 = 3 + 4i$: they are each other's
complex conjugates.

**Answer:** $a = 25$ and the other root is $x_2 = 3 + 4i$
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: Condition for two non-real roots"
**For which values of the number $a$ does the equation $x^2 + ax + 9 = 0$ have two
non-real roots?**

::: handskrift
typ: ickereella
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the *pq* formula to obtain the discriminant:

$$
x = -\frac{a}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 - 9}
$$

The equation has two non-real roots exactly when the discriminant (the number under
the root sign) is less than $0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 - 9 &< 0\\
\frac{a^2}{4} &< 9\\
a^2 &< 36
\end{aligned}
$$

Which gives $-6 < a < 6$.

**Answer:** $-6 < a < 6$
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The *pq* formula still applies"
- $x^2 + px + q = 0$ is solved with $x = -\dfrac{p}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q}$.
- The difference is that the expression under the root sign is now **allowed** to be negative.
- The equation then does not lack a solution. The roots become **complex**.
:::

::: sampunkt "The discriminant decides"
- The discriminant is the number under the root sign.
- Positive: two real roots. Zero: a double root. **Negative: two non-real roots.**
- Condition tasks are solved by setting the discriminant $> 0$ or $< 0$ and solving the inequality.
:::

::: sampunkt "The roots come in pairs"
- If a quadratic equation with **real** coefficients has a complex root, the conjugate is also a root.
- So $3 - 4i$ and $3 + 4i$ belong together.
:::

::: sampunkt "Unknown coefficient"
1. Substitute the known root into the equation.
2. Expand, use $i^2 = -1$ and solve for the coefficient.
3. Substitute the value into the equation and solve it to get the other root.
- All the imaginary terms should cancel out, otherwise something has gone wrong.
:::

:::
