# Summary: Complex numbers

This chapter was about complex numbers: how the imaginary unit $i$ makes
it possible to solve equations that have no real solutions, how complex numbers
are calculated with and illustrated as points or arrows in the complex
plane, and how the polar and exponential forms simplify
multiplication, powers and taking roots. After the chapter you should be able to calculate
with complex numbers using all four operations, solve quadratic and higher-degree equations with
complex roots, switch between the three forms of a number and use de Moivre's
formula and Euler's formula.

## Concepts to know

- **the imaginary unit ($i$)**: the number with the property $i^2 = -1$; makes it
  possible to take the square root of negative numbers.
- **imaginary number / purely imaginary number**: a number of the form $a + bi$ with
  $b \neq 0$ is imaginary; if also $a = 0$, it is called purely imaginary.
- **complex number ($z$)**: a number of the form $z = a + bi$; the real numbers are a
  subset of the complex numbers ($\mathbb{C}$).
- **real part ($\operatorname{Re} z$) and imaginary part ($\operatorname{Im} z$)**:
  for $z = a + bi$, $\operatorname{Re} z = a$ and
  $\operatorname{Im} z = b$.
- **conjugate ($\bar z$)**: the number $a - bi$; obtained by changing the sign of
  the imaginary part, corresponds to a reflection in the real axis.
- **the complex plane**: a coordinate system with a real axis (Re) and an
  imaginary axis (Im), where $z = a + bi$ corresponds to the point $(a,\ b)$.
- **arrow**: an arrow from the origin to the point $z$ in the complex plane.
- **modulus ($|z|$, $r$)**: the length of the arrow, $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$;
  $|z - w|$ is the distance between the points $z$ and $w$.
- **argument ($\arg z$, $v$)**: the angle the arrow makes with the positive
  real axis.
- **polar form**: the way of writing a complex number as
  $z = r(\cos v + i\sin v)$.
- **exponential form**: the way of writing a complex number as
  $z = r\,e^{iv}$, via Euler's formula.
- **de Moivre's formula**: a shortcut for calculating powers of complex numbers in
  polar form without multiplying them out.
- **the factor theorem**: if $x = a$ is a zero of the polynomial $p(x)$, then
  $(x - a)$ is a factor of the polynomial.
- **polynomial division and the zero product property**: polynomial division finds the
  remaining factor(s) of a polynomial equation when one root is known; the zero product
  property then solves the factorised equation factor by factor.

## Formulas

::: formel "The formulas of the chapter"
**The imaginary unit and square roots of negative numbers**

$$
i^2 = -1 \qquad\qquad \sqrt{-a} = \sqrt{a}\cdot i
$$

**The pq formula: complex roots**

$$
x^2 + px + q = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = -\frac{p}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q}
$$

where

- discriminant $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q$ **positive** ⟹ two real
  solutions
- discriminant **zero** ⟹ one real solution (double root)
- discriminant **negative** ⟹ two complex solutions, each other's conjugates
  $a + bi$ and $a - bi$

**Real part, imaginary part and conjugate**

$$
\operatorname{Re} z = a \qquad \operatorname{Im} z = b \qquad
\bar z = a - bi
$$

where $z = a + bi$.

**Addition and subtraction**

$$
(a + bi) \pm (c + di) = (a \pm c) + (b \pm d)i
$$

**The factor theorem**

$$
p(x) = (x - a)\cdot q(x)
$$

where $x = a$ is a zero of the polynomial $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ is a
polynomial of lower degree.

**Modulus and argument**

$$
|z| = r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \qquad\qquad \tan v = \frac{b}{a},\quad v = \arg z
$$

**Polar and exponential form**

$$
z = r(\cos v + i\sin v) = r\,e^{iv}
$$

**Multiplication and division in polar form**

$$
z\cdot w = |z|\cdot |w|\big(\cos(u + v) + i\sin(u + v)\big)
$$

$$
\frac{z}{w} = \frac{|z|}{|w|}\big(\cos(u - v) + i\sin(u - v)\big)
$$

where $z = |z|(\cos u + i\sin u)$ and $w = |w|(\cos v + i\sin v)$.

**Multiplication and division by the number i**

$$
\text{multiplication by } i \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \text{rotation } 90^\circ \text{ anticlockwise}
$$

$$
\text{division by } i \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \text{rotation } 90^\circ \text{ clockwise}
$$

**de Moivre's formula**

$$
z^n = \bigl(r(\cos v + i\sin v)\bigr)^n = r^n\bigl(\cos nv + i\sin nv\bigr)
$$

where $n$ is a positive integer.

**The equation $z^n = w$**

$$
v = \frac{\arg w + k\cdot 360^\circ}{n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n - 1
$$

where the equation $z^n = w$ has exactly $n$ roots, evenly spaced on a circle
with radius $r = \sqrt[n]{|w|}$, as the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

**Euler's formula and Euler's identity**

$$
e^{iv} = \cos v + i\sin v \qquad\qquad e^{i\pi} = -1
$$
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- The discriminant $\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q$ in the *pq* formula determines whether a
  quadratic equation has real or complex roots. If it is negative, the
  roots are always each other's conjugates, $a + bi$ and $a - bi$.
- Calculate with $i$ like any other number throughout the calculation and use
  $i^2 = -1$ only at the end to simplify the answer.
- When dividing complex numbers: always multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the
  denominator. The product $(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2$ is real, so the denominator
  becomes an ordinary number you can divide by.
- If you know one root of an equation of higher degree, the factor theorem gives
  the corresponding factor $(x - a)$; polynomial division finds the remaining
  factor of lower degree, which is then solved further (for example with the *pq* formula).
- Addition and subtraction of complex numbers work geometrically like
  vector addition: the arrows are added together. Multiplication and division work
  differently: as rotation (the arguments are added/subtracted) and scaling
  (the moduli are multiplied/divided).
- The conjugate $\bar z$ corresponds to a reflection of the arrow in the real axis
  (the Re-axis): the same real part, opposite imaginary part.
- $|z - w|$ is the distance between the points $z$ and $w$ in the complex
  plane. It is used, for example, to interpret a modulus equation as a
  circle.
- When calculating the argument: if the number lies in the first or fourth quadrant
  ($a > 0$), $\tan^{-1}$ gives the angle directly; if it lies in the second or third
  quadrant ($a < 0$), $180^\circ$ (or $\pi$) must be added to the
  calculator's answer.
- Polar form makes multiplication, powers and taking roots considerably
  simpler than rectangular form: the moduli are multiplied/raised to powers and
  the arguments added/multiplied instead of a laborious expansion of
  brackets.
- Multiplication by $i$ rotates the arrow $90^\circ$ anticlockwise; division by $i$
  rotates it $90^\circ$ clockwise.
- The equation $z^n = w$ always has exactly $n$ roots, which are evenly
  spaced on a circle with radius $\sqrt[n]{|w|}$ and form the vertices of a
  regular $n$-gon around the origin.
- A complex number can be written in three equivalent forms: rectangular
  ($a + bi$), polar ($r(\cos v + i\sin v)$) and exponential ($r\,e^{iv}$),
  with the same modulus $r$ and argument $v$ in the last two.

## Before the test

- Can you explain what the imaginary unit $i$ is and why it was introduced
  ($i^2 = -1$)?
- Can you distinguish between real part, imaginary part, complex number, imaginary number and
  purely imaginary number?
- Can you add, subtract, multiply and divide complex numbers, and
  do you know that division requires multiplying by the conjugate of the denominator?
- Can you solve a quadratic equation with the *pq* formula even when the discriminant
  is negative, and do you know that the roots are then each other's conjugates?
- Can you use the factor theorem and polynomial division to solve a
  cubic equation when you know one of the roots?
- Can you draw a complex number as a point or an arrow in the
  complex plane, and interpret the conjugate as a reflection in the Re-axis?
- Can you calculate the modulus and argument of a complex number, and determine which
  quadrant the number lies in?
- Can you switch between the rectangular, polar and
  exponential forms of a complex number?
- Do you know how multiplication and division work in polar form (moduli
  multiplied/divided, arguments added/subtracted), and what
  multiplication by $i$ does geometrically?
- Can you use de Moivre's formula to calculate a power of a
  complex number in polar form?
- Can you solve the equation $z^n = w$ and explain why it has exactly $n$
  roots, evenly spaced on a circle?
- Do you know Euler's formula and Euler's identity, and can you explain why the
  latter is called "the most beautiful equation in the world"?
