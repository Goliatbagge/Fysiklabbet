# Summary: Electromagnetism

The chapter is about the relationship between magnetism and electricity. You have seen
how currents and magnets create magnetic fields, how a magnetic field in turn
exerts a force on moving charges and current-carrying conductors, and how a
change in the magnetic field induces a voltage (induction). You should be able to
apply the right-hand rules and Lenz's law, and know how the principles
are used in generators, transformers, velocity selectors, mass spectrometers
and magnetometers.

## Concepts to know

- **magnetic field and magnetic flux density *B***: the region around a magnet
  or current-carrying conductor where other magnetic objects experience a
  force; the flux density gives the strength of the field, SI unit tesla (T).
- **poles and field lines**: magnetic field lines go from the north pole to the
  south pole outside the magnet; like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
- **the right-hand rule**: comes in three variants: thumb = current, fingers =
  field (straight conductor); fingers = current, thumb = field (coil); thumb/index finger/
  middle finger = current/field/force (magnetic force). Only the right hand may
  be used.
- **magnetic force**: the force a magnetic field exerts on a moving
  charge ($F = q \cdot v \cdot B$) or a current-carrying conductor
  ($F = B \cdot I \cdot l$).
- **magnetic flux *Φ***: the number of field lines passing through an area,
  SI unit weber (Wb); not to be confused with the flux density *B*.
- **induction and emf**: a voltage (electromotive force, emf)
  is induced when a conductor moves in a magnetic field, or when the flux through
  a circuit changes.
- **the law of induction**: the induced voltage is given by how fast the
  magnetic flux changes, $e = -\dfrac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$.
- **Lenz's law**: an induced current gets a direction such that the cause of
  its origin is opposed.
- **eddy currents**: circulating induced currents that, according to Lenz's law,
  oppose their own cause; they brake motion or give unwanted heat.
- **declination and inclination**: the deviation of the Earth's magnetic field
  sideways from geographic north (declination) and its tilt relative to
  ground level (inclination).
- **peak value and RMS value**: instantaneous values (*u*, *i*) vary with
  time; peak values (*û*, *î*) are the maximum values; RMS values (*U*, *I*) are
  the values a voltmeter/ammeter shows.
- **transformer**: two coils (primary and secondary coil) on a
  common iron core; voltage and current are rescaled according to the numbers of turns.
- **velocity selector, mass spectrometer and Hall effect**: applications where
  a magnetic force is balanced against an electric force or a
  centripetal force in order to filter speed, weigh charged particles or measure
  a magnetic field.

## Formulas

::: formel "Magnetic field around a straight conductor and in a coil"
$$
B = k \cdot \frac{I}{d}
$$

$$
B = \mu_0 \cdot \frac{n \cdot I}{l}
$$

where

- *B* = magnetic flux density (T)
- *k* = $2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ T·m/A (straight conductor, applies to vacuum and air)
- $\mu_0$ = the permeability of vacuum ($4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}$ T·m/A)
- *I* = the current through the conductor/coil (A)
- *d* = the distance from the straight conductor (m)
- *n* = number of turns (in the coil)
- *l* = length of the coil (m)
:::

::: formel "Magnetic force"
$$
F = q \cdot v \cdot B
$$

$$
F = B \cdot I \cdot l
$$

$$
F = k \cdot \frac{I_1 \cdot I_2 \cdot l}{d}
$$

where

- *F* = magnetic force (N)
- *q* = size of the charge (C)
- *v* = velocity of the charge (m/s)
- *B* = magnetic flux density perpendicular to the motion/conductor (T)
- *I*, $I_1$, $I_2$ = current through the conductor(s) (A)
- *l* = length of the conductor, or the parallel length between two conductors (m)
- *d* = distance between two parallel conductors (m)
- *k* = $2.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$ T·m/A
:::

::: formel "Induction and magnetic flux"
$$
e = l \cdot v \cdot B
$$

$$
\Phi = B \cdot A_\perp
$$

$$
e = -\frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}
$$

$$
e = -N \cdot \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}
$$

where

- *e* = induced voltage/emf (V)
- *l* = length of the conductor (m)
- *v* = velocity of the conductor (m/s)
- *B* = magnetic flux density (T)
- *Φ* = magnetic flux (Wb)
- $A_\perp$ = area perpendicular to the flux lines (m²)
- Δ*Φ* = change in magnetic flux (Wb)
- Δ*t* = time taken for the change (s)
- *N* = number of turns (coil; for a single conductor/circuit *N* = 1)

The minus sign comes from Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the
change that causes it.
:::

::: formel "Alternating current"
$$
u = \hat{u} \cdot \sin(\omega t)
$$

$$
i = \hat{\imath} \cdot \sin(\omega t)
$$

$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi \cdot f
$$

$$
U = \frac{\hat{u}}{\sqrt{2}} \qquad I = \frac{\hat{\imath}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

where

- *u*, *i* = instantaneous voltage and instantaneous current respectively (V, A)
- *û*, *î* = peak voltage and peak current respectively (V, A)
- *U*, *I* = RMS values of voltage and current respectively (V, A)
- *ω* = angular velocity (rad/s)
- *T* = period (s)
- *f* = frequency (Hz)
:::

::: formel "Transformer"
$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{U_1}{U_2} = \frac{I_2}{I_1}
$$

where

- $N_1$, $N_2$ = number of turns in the primary and secondary coil respectively
- $U_1$, $U_2$ = voltage in the primary and secondary coil respectively (V)
- $I_1$, $I_2$ = current in the primary and secondary coil respectively (A)
:::

::: formel "Velocity selector, mass spectrometer and Hall effect"
$$
v = \frac{\mathbb{E}}{B}
$$

$$
m = \frac{q \cdot B \cdot r}{v}
$$

$$
U = v \cdot d \cdot B
$$

where

- *v* = velocity of the particle (m/s)
- $\mathbb{E}$ = electric field (V/m)
- *B* = magnetic flux density (T)
- *m* = mass of the particle (kg)
- *q* = charge of the particle (C)
- *r* = radius of curvature in the magnetic field of the mass spectrometer (m)
- *U* = Hall voltage (V)
- *d* = width of the magnetometer plate (m)
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- **Notation in figures:** a dot in a circle means a vector straight out of
  the plane (towards you); a cross in a circle means straight into the plane (away from
  you).
- **Only the right hand** may be used in all three variants of
  the right-hand rule. With the left hand the directions come out wrong.
- According to Newton's third law, the force between two parallel
  current-carrying conductors is **attractive** if the currents are in the same direction and
  **repulsive** if they are in opposite directions.
- An emf is induced only if there is a component of the motion that is
  perpendicular to both the conductor and the field. If the conductor moves along its own
  length, **no** voltage is induced.
- In a *Φ*-*t* graph the slope corresponds to the induced voltage
  (the instantaneous value), just as the slope in an *s*-*t* graph corresponds to
  the velocity.
- The induced current in a closed circuit is found from the emf and the resistance with
  Ohm's law, $e = R \cdot I$.
- Distinguish between the **instantaneous value** (lower-case letter: *u*, *i*), the **peak value**
  (circumflex: *û*, *î*) and the **RMS value** (capital letter: *U*, *I*) for
  alternating current. It is the RMS value that an ordinary voltmeter/ammeter
  shows.
- In a transformer the power is conserved ($P_1 = P_2$) if losses are ignored:
  if the voltage increases by a certain factor, the current decreases by the same factor.
- The magnetic force $F = q \cdot v \cdot B$ (or $F = B \cdot I \cdot l$)
  is always perpendicular to the motion/current. It can therefore act as a
  centripetal force in a circular path (mass spectrometer), but it never does
  work.
- In a velocity selector the electric and the magnetic
  force balance each other ($F_e = F_m$). Only particles with the speed $v = \mathbb{E}/B$
  pass straight through.

## Before the test

- Can you explain why a current-carrying conductor or coil creates a
  magnetic field, and determine the direction of the field with the right rule?
- Can you calculate the magnetic flux density around a straight conductor and in
  a coil (solenoid)?
- Can you use the three-finger rule to determine the direction of the
  magnetic force on a charged particle or a current-carrying conductor?
- Can you calculate the force on a charged particle ($F = q \cdot v \cdot B$)
  and on a current-carrying conductor ($F = B \cdot I \cdot l$), as well as the force
  between two parallel conductors, and decide whether it is attractive or
  repulsive?
- Can you explain what declination and inclination are for the
  Earth's magnetic field?
- Can you calculate the induced voltage (emf) for a conductor moving in a
  magnetic field, and decide when no emf is induced?
- Can you use Lenz's law to determine the direction of an induced
  current or force?
- Can you calculate with magnetic flux *Φ* and the law of induction, also from
  a *Φ*-*t* graph?
- Can you distinguish between instantaneous value, peak value and RMS value for
  alternating current, and convert between them?
- Can you calculate with the voltage and current ratios of the transformer
  ($N_1/N_2 = U_1/U_2 = I_2/I_1$)?
- Can you explain how a velocity selector and a mass spectrometer
  work, and calculate velocity and mass respectively?
- Can you explain what eddy currents are, and why they brake motion
  or generate heat?
