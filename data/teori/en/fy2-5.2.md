# Measuring distances in space

Knowing how **far away** a celestial body is is one of astronomy's
hardest and most important tasks. The distances cannot be measured with a
tape measure; instead, geometry and the properties of light are used. One of
the oldest and most fundamental methods is the **parallax method**. Before
we calculate with it, we need to get to know the units of distance
used in space.

## Parallax: hold up a finger

**Parallax** is the apparent shift of an object when the
observer moves. Try it yourself: hold up a finger in front of your
face, close one eye and then look with the other. The finger
seems to **jump** back and forth against the background, even though neither
the finger nor the background has moved. The **closer** the finger is to your eyes,
the **larger** the jump.

Astronomers use exactly the same idea to measure distances to stars.
The "eyes" are Earth's two positions on either side of the Sun, half a year apart.

## Units of distance in space

On Earth, metres and kilometres are enough, but in space the numbers become unmanageably
large. That is why some special units are used:

::: tips "Three important units of distance"
| Unit | Size | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Astronomical unit (AU) | ≈ 150 million km | Distances within the solar system |
| Light year (ly) | ≈ 9.5 trillion km | Distances between stars |
| Parsec (pc) | ≈ 3.26 light years | Parallax measurements |
:::

The **astronomical unit (AU)** is Earth's mean distance from the Sun:

::: formel "Astronomical unit"
$$
1\ \mathrm{AU} \approx 1.50 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{km} = 1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}
$$
:::

A **light year** is the distance light travels in one year: almost **10 trillion
kilometres** (see section 5.1). Since light takes time to travel, we see
distant stars as they **looked in the past**: the light from a star 1 000
light years away has travelled for 1 000 years, so in a sense we are looking 1 000 years
back in time.

## The parallax method step by step

You photograph the same star **twice, half a year apart**,
for example in January and in July. By then Earth has moved to the opposite
side of its orbit, a distance of **2 AU**. Against the very distant
background stars, the nearby star then appears to have moved
slightly. Half of the apparent shift is called the **parallax angle** *p*.

The star, the Sun and Earth form a **right-angled triangle** in which the base is
**1 AU** (Earth's distance from the Sun) and the acute angle at
the star is the parallax *p*. If we know *p*, we can calculate the distance with
trigonometry.

## Small angles: arcsecond and parsec

Since the stars are so far away, the parallax angles become **very
small**, far too small to be expressed in whole degrees. That is why the degree
is divided into smaller parts:

::: formel "Arcminutes and arcseconds"
$$
1^{\circ} = 60' = 60 \cdot 60'' = 3\,600''
$$

where an **arcminute** is written $'$ and an **arcsecond** is written $''$.
:::

The distance that gives a parallax of **exactly 1 arcsecond**, with 1 AU as
the base, is called a **parsec** (pc): the name is short for **par**allax **sec**ond.

::: formel "The parallax method"
$$
r = \frac{1}{p}
$$

where

- *r* = distance to the star (pc)
- *p* = parallax angle (arcseconds, $''$)
:::

With the formula, the distance to a star is therefore simply **one divided by
the parallax**, as long as the distance is given in parsecs and the parallax in
arcseconds. If you want the answer in light years, you multiply by 3.26:

::: formel "Parsecs to light years"
$$
1\ \mathrm{pc} \approx 3.26\ \text{light years}
$$
:::

::: tips "Why is the formula so simple?"
For small angles, the parallax (in radians) is approximately the base divided
by the distance: $p \approx \dfrac{1\ \mathrm{AU}}{r}$. A parsec is *by
definition* the distance at which 1 AU is seen at an angle of 1 arcsecond. So if you
measure *r* in parsecs and *p* in arcseconds, all the
conversion factors disappear, and what remains is simply $r = \dfrac{1}{p}$. The
smaller the parallax, the greater the distance.
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** In the right-angled triangle, 1 AU is the side opposite the angle
*p*, and the distance *r* is the adjacent side:

$$
\tan p = \frac{1\ \mathrm{AU}}{r} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad r = \frac{1\ \mathrm{AU}}{\tan p}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m} \\
p = 0.40'' = \dfrac{0.40}{3\,600}^{\circ}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
r = \frac{1.50 \cdot 10^{11}}{\tan\!\left(\frac{0.40}{3\,600}^{\circ}\right)} \approx 7.7 \cdot 10^{16}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

Converted to light years (divide by $9.5 \cdot 10^{15}\ \mathrm{m}$):

$$
r \approx \frac{7.7 \cdot 10^{16}}{9.5 \cdot 10^{15}} \approx 8.2\ \text{light years}
$$

**b)** With the parallax formula we get the answer directly in parsecs:

$$
r = \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{0.40} = 2.5\ \mathrm{pc}
$$

$$
r = 2.5 \cdot 3.26 \approx 8.2\ \text{light years}
$$

**Answer:** The distance is about 2.5 pc ≈ 8.2 light years. Both methods give
the same result.
:::
:::

## How far does the method reach?

From the Earth's surface the measurements are disturbed by the atmosphere, and the parallax method
then only works for stars that, by astronomical standards, are
**relatively close**, out to about **100 light years**. Further away the
angles become too small to measure reliably.

With **satellites** above the atmosphere it works much better. ESA's
space telescope **Gaia** has measured the parallax of almost **two billion**
stars and reaches distances of **tens of thousands of light years**, and in this way has
drawn the most detailed map of the Milky Way ever made. For even
greater distances, beyond our galaxy, other methods are needed (for example
*standard candles* such as certain pulsating stars and supernovae).

::: kuriosa "A parsec is a distance, not a time"
In the film *Star Wars*, Han Solo boasts that his ship made
"the Kessel Run in less than 12 parsecs". But a parsec is a **distance**,
not a time; it is a bit like saying "I ran the race in less than
12 kilometres". Fans have afterwards tried to rescue the line by
claiming that Han took a shorter *route*, but originally it was simply
a physics mistake in the script.
:::

::: kuriosa "Our nearest neighbour"
The nearest star apart from the Sun, **Proxima Centauri**, has a parallax
of about **0.77 arcseconds**, the largest measured for any
star. This gives the distance $r = 1/0.77 \approx 1.3\ \mathrm{pc}$,
that is, about **4.2 light years**. Even though it is our very nearest neighbour,
it would take tens of thousands of years to travel there with today's fastest space probes.
:::
