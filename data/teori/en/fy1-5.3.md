# Liquid pressure

::: demo "Introduction: Liquid pressure"
When we swim towards the bottom of a swimming pool we feel the [[pressure|Force per unit area: *p* = *F* / *A*. SI unit pascal (Pa) = N/m².]] on our ears
increase. The compressive force from the weight of the liquid above becomes larger the deeper we
dive. And the larger the compressive force becomes, the larger the pressure on our ears,
which we can feel as pain.
:::

The pressure caused by a liquid is called **liquid pressure** or
**hydrostatic pressure**. The compressive force increases with depth because of a greater
weight of liquid from above.

::: formel "Liquid pressure"
$$
p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h
$$

where

- *p* = liquid pressure (Pa)
- *ρ* = the [[density|Mass per unit volume: *ρ* = *m* / *V*. SI unit kg/m³. Decides whether an object floats in a medium.]] of the liquid (kg/m³) (998 kg/m³ ≈ 1 000 kg/m³ for water)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg) (9.82 N/kg in Sweden)
- *h* = depth of liquid (m)

::: härledning "Derivation: Liquid pressure"
We set up the formula for pressure.

$$
p = \frac{F}{A}
$$

The compressive force *F* is made up of the weight of the liquid *m* · *g*. Substituting gives

$$
p = \frac{m \cdot g}{A}
$$

From the formula for density $\rho = m/V$ we can solve for the mass of the liquid
*m* = *ρ* · *V*. The volume *V* can be written as *A* · *h*, where *A* is the base area
and *h* the depth of liquid, so *m* = *ρ* · *A* · *h*. Substituting gives

$$
p = \frac{\rho \cdot A \cdot h \cdot g}{A} = \rho \cdot g \cdot h \qquad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::
:::

::: demo "Liquid pressure and depth of liquid"
We fill three different vessels (containers) with water up to the same level. Regardless of
the amount of water or the shape of the vessel, the pressure is the same. The liquid pressure
therefore does not depend on the shape of the vessel or the amount of water, but only on
the **depth of liquid**.
:::

If we have a given liquid (and are on Earth), only the depth of liquid decides
which liquid pressure acts. The amount of liquid or the shape of the vessel does not
matter. You are subjected to the same liquid pressure whether you dive two metres
down in a sea or two metres down in a swimming pool.

::: demo "The direction of the compressive force"
We close off the bottom of an air-filled tube with a loose lid and lower it into
water. We see that the lid stays in place against the bottom of the tube. This means that
the compressive force from the liquid acts **upwards** on the lid and is larger than the
[[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and points straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] downwards on the lid.
:::

At a certain depth the liquid pressure is the same regardless of which way the surface faces.
The liquid pressure always acts **perpendicular** to the surface.

::: exempel "Example 1: Herbert Nitsch at a depth of 253 metres"
**In June 2012 Herbert Nitsch dived, without air tanks, to a depth of 253.2 metres
off the coast of Greece. What liquid pressure acted on him then? Assume that
the density of the salt water is 1 028 kg/m³.**

::: handskrift
typ: fridyk
:::

::: textlosning
$$
p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\rho_\text{water} = 1\,028\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \\
g = 9.8\ \mathrm{N/kg} \quad (\text{he was not diving in Sweden}) \\
h = 253.2\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
p = 1\,028 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 253.2 = 2\,550\,838.08\ \mathrm{Pa}
\approx 2.6 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa} = 2.6\ \mathrm{MPa}
$$

**Answer:** 2.6 MPa (which corresponds to a pressure 25 times greater than normal
[[air pressure|The pressure from the Earth's atmosphere. At sea level about 101 325 Pa (1 atm).]])
:::
:::

::: kuriosa "Physics in everyday life: Water towers"
At the top of a water tower there is a water tank. Water is pumped up into the tank
with a pump. Once the water is up in the tank, the pump is no longer needed:
the water is pushed out into the water pipes by its own weight. Such a
water tower can be clearly seen, for example, in Stenstorp. In Falköping we instead have
water reservoirs that work in the same way, although they are underground (but
still higher up than the taps in our houses).

**Think about it:** where in a block of flats is the pressure in the
taps greatest?
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Liquid pressure"
- The pressure caused by the liquid's own weight. Also called
  **hydrostatic pressure**.
- $p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h$
- $\rho$ is the density of the liquid, $g$ the gravitational field strength and $h$ the **depth** below
  the surface.
:::

::: sampunkt "Only the depth matters"
- The liquid pressure does **not** depend on the shape of the vessel or on how much liquid
  there is.
- Two metres down in a swimming pool gives the same pressure as two metres down in
  the sea.
:::

::: sampunkt "The direction"
- At a certain depth the pressure is **equally large in all directions**.
- The compressive force always acts **perpendicular to the surface**, even from below and
  upwards. That is why the lid stays on under the submerged tube.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the formula comes from"
- Start from $p = \dfrac{F}{A}$ with the compressive force
  $F = m \cdot g$.
- Substitute $m = \rho \cdot V$ and $V = A \cdot h$.
- The area $A$ cancels out and what is left is
  $p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Values to use"
- Water: $\rho \approx 1\,000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Salt water is heavier,
  about $1\,028\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.
- Ten metres of water corresponds to roughly one whole atmosphere of pressure.
:::
:::
