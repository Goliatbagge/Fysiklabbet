# Summary: Nuclear physics

This chapter was about the structure and stability of the atomic nucleus: how
protons and neutrons are held together by the strong nuclear force, how mass
is converted into binding energy, and how unstable nuclei decay through
alpha, beta and gamma radiation. After the chapter you should be able to calculate
mass defect and binding energy, write balanced decay equations,
calculate with activity and half-life, and assess radiation doses and
the harmful effects of radiation.

## Concepts to know

- **Nucleon**: collective name for protons and neutrons in the atomic nucleus.
- **Strong nuclear force**: the force that holds the nucleons together in the nucleus;
  acts only over very short distances (about $10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$).
- **Mass number ($A$)**: the total number of nucleons (p and n) in the nucleus.
- **Atomic number ($Z$)**: the number of protons in the nucleus; determines the element.
- **Isotope**: atoms of the same element (same *Z*) but with different numbers of
  neutrons (different *A*).
- **Mass defect ($\Delta m$)**: the difference between the mass of the particles
  separately and the mass of the composite nucleus.
- **Binding energy**: the energy that corresponds to the mass defect via
  $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$; holds the nucleus together.
- **Radioactive decay**: when an unstable nucleus spontaneously transforms
  while emitting ionising radiation.
- **Positron ($e^+$) and neutrino ($\nu$)**: the positron is the electron's
  antiparticle (same mass, positive charge); the neutrino is almost
  massless, uncharged and hardly interacts with matter. Both are formed in
  β decay.
- **Activity ($A$) and decay constant ($\lambda$)**: the activity is
  the number of decays per second (Bq); the decay constant is
  the constant of proportionality between activity and number of nuclei.
- **Half-life ($T_{1/2}$)**: the time it takes for half of the nuclei in
  a radioactive sample to decay.
- **Absorbed dose ($D$) and equivalent dose ($H$)**: absorbed dose is
  radiation energy per kg of tissue (Gy); equivalent dose weights this by
  the harmfulness of the type of radiation (Sv).

## Formulas

::: formel "The chapter's formulas"
**Composition of the nucleus**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X}
$$

where

- *X* = chemical symbol of the element
- *A* = mass number (number of nucleons, p + n)
- *Z* = atomic number (number of protons)

**Nuclear mass**

$$
m_\text{nucleus} = m_\text{nuclide} - Z \cdot m_\text{electron}
$$

**Mass defect**

$$
\Delta m = m_\text{particles} - m_\text{nucleus}
$$

**Binding energy**

$$
E = \Delta m \cdot c^2
$$

where

- *E* = binding energy (J)
- $\Delta m$ = mass defect (kg)
- *c* = the speed of light in a vacuum (≈ 2.998 · 10⁸ m/s)

**Converting between units**

$$
1\ \mathrm{eV} \approx 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}
\qquad\qquad
1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.49\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

**α decay**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-2}^{A-4}} \mathrm{Y} + {_2^4} \mathrm{He}
$$

**β decay**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z+1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} + \nu
\qquad \text{(β⁻)}
$$

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_1^0} \mathrm{e} + \nu
\qquad \text{(β⁺)}
$$

**Electron capture**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} + {_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} \rightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + \nu
$$

**γ decay**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X}^* \rightarrow {_Z^A} \mathrm{X} + \gamma
$$

**Activity**

$$
A = \lambda \cdot N
$$

where

- *A* = activity (Bq)
- *λ* = decay constant (1/s)
- *N* = number of atomic nuclei

**The decay constant**

$$
\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}
$$

**The law of activity and the law of radioactive decay**

$$
A = A_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
\qquad\qquad
N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

**Absorbed dose**

$$
D = \frac{E}{m}
$$

where

- *D* = absorbed dose (Gy)
- *E* = energy of the radiation (J)
- *m* = irradiated mass (kg)

**Equivalent dose**

$$
H = D \cdot Q
$$

where

- *H* = equivalent radiation dose (Sv)
- *D* = absorbed dose (Gy)
- *Q* = quality factor (Q = 20 for α, Q = 1 for β and γ)
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- The mass of a composite nucleus is always **lower** than the sum of the masses
  of its constituents. The difference (the mass defect) corresponds to
  the binding energy that holds the nucleus together.
- In **β decay** the number of electrons in the nuclide changes, so
  the electron masses must be subtracted from the nuclide masses on both sides of
  the arrow. This is **not** needed in α or γ decay.
- Decay equations are balanced like equations: the mass numbers (top) and
  the atomic numbers (bottom) must add up to the same number on both sides of the arrow.
- The quality factor *Q* distinguishes how harmful the types of radiation are: the same absorbed
  dose *D* gives a 20 times higher equivalent dose *H* for alpha radiation compared
  with beta or gamma radiation.
- The penetrating power increases α < β < γ, but the local harmful effect (if
  the radiation reaches sensitive tissue) is greatest for α and smallest for γ.
  Alpha is therefore most dangerous inside the body, gamma most dangerous outside.
- The activity and the number of nuclei decrease according to exactly the same exponential
  relationship (the same base $0.5^{t/T_{1/2}}$): if one falls to half,
  so does the other.
- After 10 half-lives about one per mille of the original amount
  remains ($0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$). This gives carbon-14 dating
  an upper limit of about fifty thousand years.

## Before the test

- Can you state the composition of an atomic nucleus (number of protons and neutrons)
  from the notation $_Z^A\mathrm{X}$?
- Do you know the difference between mass number, atomic number and isotope?
- Can you calculate mass defect and binding energy with $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$,
  and switch between u, kg, J, eV and MeV?
- Can you write and balance decay equations for α, β⁻, β⁺ and
  γ decay?
- Do you know what a positron and a neutrino are, and why they occur in
  β decay?
- Do you know the formula for activity ($A = \lambda \cdot N$) and the relationship between
  the decay constant and the half-life?
- Can you use the laws of activity and decay to calculate how
  much remains after a given time (for example in carbon-14 dating)?
- Do you know the penetrating power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation
  and why they are dangerous in different ways?
- Can you calculate with absorbed dose ($D = E/m$) and equivalent dose
  ($H = D \cdot Q$)?
