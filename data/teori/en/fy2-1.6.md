# Energy and gravitation in circular paths

In the previous section we met the [[centripetal force|The force needed to keep an object in circular motion, directed towards the centre of the circle.]]. The most rewarding tasks on
circular motion do not stop there, however, but combine it with something more:
**[[the principle of conservation of energy|Energy can be neither created nor destroyed, only converted between different forms.]]** when the speed changes along the path, or **Newton's
law of gravitation** when it is a satellite in orbit. You will recognise both tools
from Physics level 1, so we start with a short review.

## Review from Physics level 1

Two objects with mass act on each other with a mutual attractive
[[gravitational force|The force acting between all masses. At the Earth's surface it is called weight (the gravitational force).]]. The larger the masses and the shorter the distance, the greater the
[[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]].

::: formel "Newton's law of gravitation"
$$
F_G = G \cdot \frac{m_1 \cdot m_2}{r^{2}}
$$

where

- $F_G$ = gravitational force (N)
- *G* = the gravitational constant ($6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{Nm^2/kg^2}$)
- *m*₁ = mass of object 1 (kg)
- *m*₂ = mass of object 2 (kg)
- *r* = the distance between the centres of mass of the objects (m)
:::

An object at a height has [[gravitational potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]], and an
object that moves has [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]].

::: formel "Potential and kinetic energy"
$$
E_p = m \cdot g \cdot h \qquad\qquad E_k = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

where

- $E_p$ = gravitational potential energy (J)
- $E_k$ = kinetic energy (J)
- *m* = the mass of the object (kg)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg) ($9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg}$ in Sweden)
- *h* = height above the zero level (m)
- *v* = the speed of the object (m/s)
:::

::: formel "The principle of conservation of energy"
Energy can be neither created nor destroyed, only converted from one form
to another. Without energy losses, therefore,

$$
E_{p1} + E_{k1} = E_{p2} + E_{k2}
$$

where

- $E_{p1}$, $E_{k1}$ = potential and kinetic energy at time 1
- $E_{p2}$, $E_{k2}$ = potential and kinetic energy at time 2
:::

For a swing or a roller coaster car, it is the principle of conservation of energy that
links the positions in the path together: the kinetic energy lost on the way
up is stored as potential energy, ready to become speed again on the way down.

The following classic worked examples combine **circular motion**
(centripetal force, tension) with **[[the principle of conservation of energy|Energy can be neither created nor destroyed, only converted between different forms.]]** (conversion between
[[gravitational potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]] and [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]).

::: textlosning

**a)** The swing is part of a circular motion, which means that at
the bottom of the path there is a centripetal force **upwards**, in towards the centre. At
the lowest point of the path, two forces act on the seat: a gravitational force $F_G$
downwards and a tension in the rope $F_S$ upwards.

The centripetal force $F_C$ is the resultant of these two forces. Since
the centripetal force is directed in towards the centre, $F_S > F_G$.

We set up the relationship between the forces and solve for $F_S$.

$$
F_C = F_S - F_G \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_S = F_C + F_G
$$

Substituting $F_C = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}$ and $F_G = m \cdot g$ gives

$$
F_S = \frac{m v^{2}}{r} + m \cdot g \qquad (1)
$$

We need to calculate the speed *v* at the lowest part of the path. We do this
using **the principle of conservation of energy**: the potential energy $E_p$ is converted into kinetic energy
$E_k$.

$$
E_p = E_k \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m \cdot g \cdot h = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{2}
$$

We divide both sides by *m* and solve for the speed *v*.

$$
g \cdot h = \frac{v^{2}}{2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v^{2} = 2 \cdot g \cdot h \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{2 \cdot g \cdot h}
$$

Substituting $g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ and $h = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$
(the centre of gravity "falls" 1.0 m) gives

$$
v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.82 \cdot 1.0} = 4.431\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

Now we can calculate the tension $F_S$.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 80\ \mathrm{kg} \\
v = 4.431\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
r = 2.5\ \mathrm{m} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
\end{array} \right]
$$

in (1) gives

$$
F_S = \frac{80 \cdot 4.431\ldots^{2}}{2.5} + 80 \cdot 9.82 = 1414.08\ \mathrm{N} \approx 1\,400\ \mathrm{N} = 1.4\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 1.4 kN

**b)** We draw the forces at the top. In the limiting case when the rope only just
goes slack, the tension is $F_S = 0$ and only the gravitational force $F_G$ acts.

The centripetal force is therefore made up only of the gravitational force. This gives

$$
F_C = F_G \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r} = m \cdot g
$$

We divide both sides by *m* and solve for the speed *v*.

$$
\frac{v^{2}}{r} = g \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v^{2} = g \cdot r \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{g \cdot r}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \\
r = 2.5\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
v = \sqrt{9.82 \cdot 2.5} = 4.954\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 5.0\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

**Answer:** 5.0 m/s
:::

When a satellite orbits the Earth, it is the [[gravitational force|The force acting between all masses. At the Earth's surface it is called weight (the gravitational force).]] that provides
the centripetal force. If we combine the formula for centripetal force with
**Newton's law of gravitation**, we can therefore calculate where a satellite
is, directly from its speed.

::: textlosning
Hubble moves in a circular orbit, so there must be a centripetal force
that is always directed in towards the centre of the Earth. The only force
acting on the telescope is the gravitational force from the Earth. So it is
the gravitational force that provides the centripetal force.

$$
F_C = F_G
$$

Substituting $F_C = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r}$ and Newton's law of gravitation
$F_G = G \cdot \dfrac{m \cdot m_j}{r^{2}}$ gives

$$
\frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r} = G \cdot \frac{m \cdot m_j}{r^{2}}
$$

where *m* is the mass of the telescope and *r* is the distance from the centre of the Earth
to the telescope. We divide both sides by *m*. The telescope's own mass
therefore plays no part in the orbit!

$$
\frac{v^{2}}{r} = G \cdot \frac{m_j}{r^{2}}
$$

Then we multiply both sides by *r* and solve for the radius of the orbit *r*.

$$
v^{2} = G \cdot \frac{m_j}{r}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
r = G \cdot \frac{m_j}{v^{2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
G = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{Nm^2/kg^2} \\
m_j = 5.972 \cdot 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg} \quad (\text{the mass of the Earth, from a table}) \\
v = 7\,560\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
r = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{5.972 \cdot 10^{24}}{7\,560^{2}}
= 6.9695\ldots \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

This is the distance from the **centre** of the Earth. We get the height *h* above the Earth's
surface by subtracting the Earth's radius $r_j$.

$$
h = r - r_j
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
r = 6.9695\ldots \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m} = 6\,969.5\ldots\ \mathrm{km} \\
r_j = 6\,378\ \mathrm{km} \quad (\text{the radius of the Earth, from a table})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
h = 6\,969.5\ldots - 6\,378 = 591.5\ldots\ \mathrm{km} \approx 590\ \mathrm{km}
$$

**Answer:** 590 km

::: kuriosa "Newton's moon test: the Moon falls towards the Earth"
How could Newton know that the gravitational force decreases with the **square** of
the distance? He tested his law on the Moon. The Moon goes around the Earth in an orbit
with the radius $384\,400\ \mathrm{km}$, about 60 Earth radii, and the orbital period
is $T = 27.3\ \text{days} = 2.36 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{s}$. From the orbit
we can calculate the Moon's centripetal acceleration:

$$
a_C = \frac{4\pi^{2} \cdot r}{T^{2}}
= \frac{4\pi^{2} \cdot 3.844 \cdot 10^{8}}{(2.36 \cdot 10^{6})^{2}}
\approx 0.0027\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

If gravity decreases with the square of the distance, the acceleration at
the Moon, 60 Earth radii away, should be one $3\,600$th ($60^{2} = 3\,600$) of
the acceleration at the Earth's surface:

$$
\frac{g}{3\,600} = \frac{9.82}{3\,600} \approx 0.0027\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

The numbers agree. The same force that makes the apple fall from the tree therefore
keeps the Moon in its orbit. The Moon "falls" towards the Earth all the time, just
like the apple, but at the same time it moves sideways so fast that it constantly
misses: the orbit curves exactly as much as the Earth's surface curves away.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The law of gravitation (review)"
- Two masses attract each other:
  $F_G = G \cdot \dfrac{m_1 \cdot m_2}{r^{2}}$
- *G* is the gravitational constant,
  $6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{Nm^2/kg^2}$.
- *r* is measured between the centres of mass.
:::

::: sampunkt "Conservation of energy (review)"
- Potential energy $E_p = m g h$, kinetic energy
  $E_k = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{2}$.
- Without losses: $E_{p1} + E_{k1} = E_{p2} + E_{k2}$.
- A fall from rest gives the speed $v = \sqrt{2 g h}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The swing: force plus energy"
- Conservation of energy gives the **speed** at the lowest point, the force equation gives
  the **tension**: $F_S = F_C + F_G$.
- Lowest speed at the top (rope only just taut, $F_S = 0$):
  $v = \sqrt{g r}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The satellite: gravitation as centripetal force"
- The gravitational force provides the centripetal force: $F_C = F_G$.
- The satellite's own mass cancels, so the orbit depends only on the speed:
  $r = G \cdot \dfrac{m_j}{v^{2}}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Newton's moon test"
- The Moon is 60 Earth radii away, and its centripetal acceleration is
  $\dfrac{g}{3\,600}$.
- $60^{2} = 3\,600$: gravity decreases with the **square** of the distance.
:::

- The centripetal force is the resultant, directed in towards the centre.
- If the speed is needed at another position: get it from conservation of energy.
