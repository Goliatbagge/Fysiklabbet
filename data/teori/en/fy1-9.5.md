# Radiation doses

With strong radiation, atoms in our bodies will be ionised. This
damages the genetic material of the cells, the DNA molecule. If the damage is
large enough, enzymes cannot repair the molecule, and the cell may then
die or stop dividing. But the radiation can also change the DNA molecule so
that it gets new properties when it divides, and then we get a mutation.

Most of the time mutations are bad, but sometimes they give new properties that
are beneficial and are then passed on to the next generation. In some cases
the DNA damage also causes the cell to divide uncontrollably, and we
then get a cancer cell. If you are exposed to strong radiation, the symptoms are
nausea, vomiting and dizziness.

The amount of radiation is called the **radiation dose**, and it is used for example to know
how great the risk of damage is during irradiation. How much radiation energy
is absorbed per kilogram of body tissue is called the **absorbed dose**,
is denoted *D* and has the unit Gy (gray).

::: formel "Absorbed dose"
$$
D = \frac{E}{m}
$$

where

- *D* = absorbed dose (Gy or J/kg)
- *E* = energy of the radiation (J)
- *m* = irradiated mass (kg)
:::

The limit for acute radiation sickness is 1 Gy.

::: demo "Penetrating power"
Different radioactive samples have different penetrating power. We investigate how
cardboard, plastic, thin metal and lead stop the different types of radiation.
α radiation is stopped by a thin sheet of cardboard. β radiation is not stopped by a thin
sheet of cardboard, but is stopped by thin metal. γ radiation is not stopped by thin metal,
but by a thick layer of lead.
:::

Different types of radiation have different penetrating power. The penetrating power and how
much the radiation interacts with matter make them dangerous in different ways.

**Alpha radiation:** With its relatively large helium nuclei, it does a lot of
damage if it is close to the skin or inside the body, since it gives off
all its energy over a small area and badly damages the tissue there. If
inhaled, for example as radon, it does great damage to internal organs. On the other hand it has
a low penetrating power and is easily stopped by other materials, for example clothing,
so outside the body it is relatively harmless.

**Beta radiation:** The emitted electrons/positrons are small particles
and do not do the same damage to the body's molecules as alpha radiation.
On the other hand, they have greater penetrating power than the alpha particle, get further into
the body and are therefore harder to protect yourself against. Clothing does not stop them,
but thin metal does.

**Gamma radiation:** The photons are extremely small particles with even higher
penetrating power than beta radiation. It is very hard to stop, and
thick metals with a high [[density|Mass per unit volume: *ρ* = *m* / *V*. SI unit kg/m³. Determines whether an object floats in a medium.]], for example lead, are needed to stop it.
However, it spreads its energy over a larger area and does not do as
much local damage. In large doses gamma radiation can be lethal, but
normally we are not exposed to strong gamma sources.

Radiation doses where we take the type of radiation into account are called the **equivalent dose**,
are denoted *H* and have the unit Sv (sievert), after the Swedish physicist Rolf
Sievert, who was active in the 1920s.

The harmfulness of a type of radiation is set in relation to gamma radiation and
is called the **quality factor**. The quality factor is denoted *Q* and
has no unit. For gamma radiation, therefore, *Q* = 1. For alpha radiation,
which is more harmful, *Q* = 20, and for beta radiation *Q* = 1.

::: formel "Equivalent dose"
$$
H = D \cdot Q
$$

where

- *H* = equivalent radiation dose (Sv)
- *D* = absorbed dose (Gy)
- *Q* = quality factor
:::

The limit for radiation to the skin is 50 mSv/year.

## Sources of radiation

Where the radiation comes from is called the **source of radiation**. We are exposed to many
types of radiation sources, for example

- **terrestrial radiation** from naturally occurring uranium and potassium in
  the bedrock
- **cosmic background radiation** from for example supernovae, neutron stars
  and black holes. It becomes stronger the higher up in the atmosphere you go.
- radiation from **medical examinations**, for example X-rays.
- radiation from inside **our own bodies** from potassium-40 (from food) and
  carbon-14 (from the air).
- radon in indoor air from **building materials**.
- spread from **nuclear waste** and **nuclear weapons**.

::: exempel "Example 1: Person exposed to α radiation"
**A person with a mass of 75 kg is exposed to α radiation and receives an
absorbed dose of 0.50 mGy. Determine**

**a) the energy contained in the absorbed radiation.**
**b) the equivalent radiation dose.**

::: handskrift
typ: straldos
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We solve for the energy *E* from the formula for absorbed dose.

$$
D = \frac{E}{m} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad E = D \cdot m
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
D = 0.50\ \mathrm{mGy} = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Gy} \\
m = 75\ \mathrm{kg}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 75 = 0.0375\ \mathrm{J}
\approx 0.038\ \mathrm{J} = 38\ \mathrm{mJ}
$$

**Answer:** 38 mJ

**b)** We set up the formula for equivalent dose.

$$
H = D \cdot Q
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
D = 0.50\ \mathrm{mGy} = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Gy} \\
Q = 20\ (\text{looked up in a table})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
H = 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 20 = 0.010\ \mathrm{Sv} = 10\ \mathrm{mSv}
$$

**Answer:** 10 mSv
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Why radiation is dangerous"
- Ionising radiation damages the **DNA** of the cells.
- If the damage is too great, the cell may **die** or stop dividing.
- If the DNA is changed instead, we get a **mutation**, and sometimes a cancer cell.
- Acute symptoms from strong radiation: nausea, vomiting, dizziness.
:::

::: sampunkt "Absorbed dose"
- Radiation energy per kilogram of irradiated tissue.
- $D = \dfrac{E}{m}$, with the unit Gy (gray), which is the same as J/kg.
- The limit for **acute radiation sickness** is $1\ \mathrm{Gy}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Equivalent dose"
- Takes into account **which type of radiation** it is.
- $H = D \cdot Q$, with the unit Sv (sievert), after the Swede Rolf Sievert.
- The **quality factor** $Q$ has no unit: gamma and beta have $Q = 1$,
  alpha has $Q = 20$.
- Limit for the skin: $50\ \mathrm{mSv}$ per year.
:::

::: sampunkt "The three types of radiation"
- **Alpha**: large helium nuclei. Stopped by paper and clothing, but does
  great local damage **inside** the body, for example when radon is inhaled.
- **Beta**: electrons or positrons. Goes through clothing but is stopped by
  thin metal.
- **Gamma**: photons with very high penetrating power. Requires thick lead.
  Spreads its energy over a large area and does less local damage.
:::

::: sampunkt "Radiation sources around us"
- **Terrestrial radiation** from uranium and potassium in the bedrock.
- **Cosmic background radiation**, stronger higher up in the atmosphere.
- **Medical examinations**, for example X-rays.
- **Our own bodies**, through potassium-40 and carbon-14.
- **Radon** from building materials, as well as nuclear waste and nuclear weapons.
:::
:::
