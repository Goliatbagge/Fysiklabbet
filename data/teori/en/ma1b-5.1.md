# Interpreting and examining tables and charts

We meet statistics every day: in the news, in advertising and on social media,
data is presented as tables and charts. This section is about
two skills: **interpreting** (reading off and calculating with what is shown) and
**examining** (deciding whether the picture of the data is honest or misleading).

## Frequency tables

A survey often starts as a **frequency table**: for each answer
we count how many times it occurs. The number is called the **frequency**,
and the frequency's proportion of the total number is called the **relative frequency**.

::: formel "Relative frequency"
$$
\text{relative frequency} = \frac{\text{the frequency}}{\text{the total number}}
$$
:::

In a class of 20 pupils, the pupils answered the question "How do you get
to school?" like this:

| Way of travelling | Frequency | Relative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Bike | 8 | 0.40 = 40 % |
| Bus | 6 | 0.30 = 30 % |
| Walking | 4 | 0.20 = 20 % |
| Car | 2 | 0.10 = 10 % |
| **Total** | **20** | **1 = 100 %** |

The relative frequencies always add up to 1, that is, 100 %. That is a good
check that nothing has been miscounted.

is a category, and the height of the bar shows the frequency. The bars can be arranged
in any order: the categories have no order among themselves.

::: textlosning
**a)** The sector for popcorn is 45 % of the whole. The proportion times the whole
gives the number:

$$
0.45 \cdot 240 = 108
$$

**b)** Sweets were chosen by 25 % and nothing by 10 %. The difference is
$25 - 10 = 15$ percentage points, and 15 % of the cinema-goers is

$$
0.15 \cdot 240 = 36
$$

**Answer:** a) 108 cinema-goers&emsp;&emsp;b) 36 more chose sweets.
:::

## Examining charts: do not be fooled

A chart can be correctly drawn and still give a distorted picture. The
most common tricks to recognise:

- **Truncated axis**: the *y*-axis does not start at zero, so small differences
  look enormous. The most common trick of all.
- **Chosen time period**: a line graph that starts in a temporary
  dip makes a normal recovery look like a surge.
- **Different scales**: two curves in the same picture can be drawn against different
  *y*-axes, and then the comparison says nothing.
- **Areas instead of heights**: if both the width and the height of a
  picture are doubled, the area becomes four times as large, even though the value is only doubled.

Always ask: *where do the axes start, what do the scales show and which period
has been chosen?*

::: textlosning
**a)** In the truncated chart, the heights of the bars above
the break point 9 400 kr are compared:

$$
\frac{1\,000}{200} = 5
$$

So the bar looks five times as tall, like an enormous increase.

**b)** The real change factor is the ratio between the rents:

$$
\frac{10\,400}{9\,600} = 1.0833\ldots
$$

The factor 1.0833… corresponds to an increase of
$1.0833\ldots - 1 = 0.0833\ldots \approx 8.3\ \%$.

**Answer:** a) 5 times as tall&emsp;&emsp;b) The rent was increased by about 8.3 %.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Frequency table"
- **Frequency**: how many times an answer occurs.
- **Relative frequency**:
  $\dfrac{\text{the frequency}}{\text{the total number}}$.
- The relative frequencies always add up to 1, that is, 100 %. That is a
  good check.
:::

::: sampunkt "Choose the right chart"
- **Bar chart**: data in **categories** with no order among themselves.
- **Line graph**: something followed **over time**. The slope shows how
  fast the value changes.
- **Pie chart**: **parts of a whole**. The whole circle is 100 %.
:::

::: sampunkt "From proportion to number"
- $\text{the number} = \text{the proportion} \cdot \text{the total number}$
- 45 % of 240 cinema-goers is 108 people.
:::

::: sampunkt "Four common tricks"
- **Truncated axis**: the $y$-axis does not start at zero, so small differences look
  enormous. The most common trick of all.
- **Chosen time period**: a start in a temporary dip makes a normal
  recovery look like a surge.
- **Different scales**: two curves against separate $y$-axes cannot be compared.
- **Areas instead of heights**: double the width and double the height give **four
  times** as large an area, even though the value has only doubled.
:::

::: sampunkt "Examine like this"
- Where do the axes start?
- What do the scales show?
- Which time period has been chosen?
- Always calculate the **real** percentage change and compare
  with how the picture looks.
:::
:::
