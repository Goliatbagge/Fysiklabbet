# Solving systems of equations in GeoGebra

Some higher-level tasks give us two unknown [[quantities|A property that can be measured with a number and a unit, for example distance, time, force.]] where neither can be
calculated directly. We then have to set up two equations and form a
**system of equations**. If the system of equations becomes complicated, we can solve it in
**GeoGebra** instead of algebraically (for example with the substitution or
elimination method).

::: exempel "Example 1: A car that accelerates over 400 metres"
**A car drives at a [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of 15 m/s and then increases its velocity with
constant [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]]. During the acceleration the car moves 400 metres and
reaches a velocity of 28 m/s. What was the car's acceleration?**

::: handskrift
typ: accel400
:::

::: textlosning
We write down the formula for [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]] at constant acceleration and solve for
the acceleration *a*.

$$
s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

We multiply both sides by 2 and solve for the term *a* · *t*².

$$
2s = 2 v_0 \cdot t + a \cdot t^{2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
a \cdot t^{2} = 2s - 2 v_0 \cdot t
$$

We solve for *a* by dividing both sides by *t*².

$$
a = \frac{2s}{t^{2}} - \frac{2 v_0}{t}
$$

Substituting *s* = 400 m and *v*₀ = 15 m/s gives

$$
a = \frac{2 \cdot 400}{t^{2}} - \frac{2 \cdot 15}{t}
$$

$$
a = \frac{800}{t^{2}} - \frac{30}{t} \qquad (1)
$$

We now have one equation with two unknown variables, *a* and *t*. We cannot
find the acceleration with the formula $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ because
we do not have the time of the acceleration Δ*t*. **We need one more equation.**

We write down the formula for velocity at constant acceleration,

$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t
$$

Substituting *v* = 28 m/s, *v*₀ = 15 m/s gives

$$
28 = 15 + a \cdot t \qquad (2)
$$

Once again we get an equation with the two unknown variables, *a* and *t*.

Since we have two equations with two unknowns, we can now solve the equations
by setting up a system of equations from equations (1) and (2).

$$
\begin{cases}
a = \dfrac{800}{t^{2}} - \dfrac{30}{t} \\
28 = 15 + a \cdot t
\end{cases}
$$

This system of equations is rather laborious to solve algebraically, so we
use GeoGebra. We rewrite the system of equations with the variables *x* and
*y* to make it easier to enter in GeoGebra. We set *a* = *x* and
*t* = *y*, which gives the system of equations

$$
\begin{cases}
x = \dfrac{800}{y^{2}} - \dfrac{30}{y} \\
28 = 15 + x \cdot y
\end{cases}
$$

We enter the equations in GeoGebra, then click the "three dots" menu
to the right of equation 2 and choose **Solve**.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "When is a system of equations needed?"
- When the task has **two unknown quantities** and neither of them can be
  calculated directly.
- The typical case: both the acceleration $a$ and the time $t$ are unknown.
- Then **two equations** are needed, one for each unknown.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to set it up"
1. Write one equation from the distance formula
   $s = v_0 \cdot t + \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}$.
2. Write another from the velocity formula $v = v_0 + a \cdot t$.
3. Substitute the known values into both.
4. Solve the system.
:::

::: sampunkt "Solving in GeoGebra"
- Rename the variables to $x$ and $y$, for example $a = x$ and
  $t = y$, so that they are easier to enter.
- Enter both equations, click the three-dots menu next to the
  second one and choose **Solve**.
- Interpret the answer back: $x$ is the acceleration, $y$ is the time.
:::

::: sampunkt "Showing your work"
- Always write out what you do: "I solve the system of equations with CAS in
  GeoGebra and get …".
- Answer with the correct number of significant figures and the correct unit.
:::
:::
