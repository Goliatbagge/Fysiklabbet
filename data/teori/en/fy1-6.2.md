# Heating and cooling

When an object is heated, the [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance are moving. SI unit kelvin (K).]] increase depends on the energy supplied,
the mass of the object and the material of the object. The greater the mass, the more
energy is needed.

How much energy is needed to increase the temperature of a particular substance
is called the **[[specific heat capacity|How much energy is needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K. Unit: J/(kg·K).]]**, written
*c*, and has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] J/(kg · K) (joules per kilogram and kelvin).

The specific heat capacity gives the energy needed to raise 1 kg
of a substance by 1 °C and is looked up in a table of thermal data. It varies for different
substances and also for different states of the same substance. The specific
heat capacity of liquid water is, for example, not the same as that of ice,
even though it is the same substance.

During heating, energy is **absorbed**, and during cooling, energy is **given off**. The
energy absorbed or given off is calculated in the same way.

::: formel "Heating and cooling"
$$
E = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T
$$

(written $Q = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T$ in the formula booklet)

where

- *E*, *Q* = energy absorbed/given off (J)
- *c* = specific heat capacity (J/(kg · K)), different for different substances and states
- *m* = mass (kg)
- $\Delta T$ = temperature change (K or °C)
:::

When energy is transferred from one substance to another without energy losses,
the energy given off is equal to the energy absorbed.

::: formel "Energy given off and energy absorbed (without energy losses)"
$$
E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp}
$$

where

- $E_\text{avg}$ = energy given off
- $E_\text{upp}$ = energy absorbed
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Water heater"
**A water heater with a [[power|Work or energy per unit time: *P* = *W* / *t*. SI unit watt (W).]] of 5.0 kW is to heat 25 litres of water at 10 degrees
to 40 °C. How**

**a) much energy is needed for the heating?**
**b) long does it take to heat the water?**

::: handskrift
typ: varmvatten
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We set up the formula for heating.

$$
E = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
c = 4.18\ \mathrm{kJ/(kg \cdot K)} = 4\,180\ \mathrm{J/(kg \cdot K)} \\
m = 25\ \mathrm{kg}\ (\text{1 litre of water has a mass of about 1 kg}) \\
\Delta T = T_2 - T_1 = 40 - 10 = 30\ \mathrm{K}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E = 4\,180 \cdot 25 \cdot 30 = 3.135 \cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{J} \approx 3.1 \cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{J} = 3.1\ \mathrm{MJ}
$$

**Answer:** 3.1 MJ

**b)** The energy given off by the water heater is absorbed by the water.

$$
E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp} \qquad (1)
$$

We get the energy given off by the heater by solving for the energy *E* from
$P = E/t$, so $E_\text{avg} = P \cdot t$.

Substituting $E_\text{avg} = P \cdot t$ into (1), $E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp}$, gives

$$
P \cdot t = E_\text{upp}
$$

The time is asked for, so we solve for *t*.

$$
t = \frac{E_\text{upp}}{P}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
E_\text{upp} = 3.135 \cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{J}\ (\text{from part a}) \\
P = 5.0\ \mathrm{kW} = 5\,000\ \mathrm{W}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
t = \frac{3.135 \cdot 10^6}{5\,000} = 627\ \mathrm{s} = 10\ \text{minutes and } 27\ \text{seconds}
$$

**Answer:** About 10 minutes
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Mixing hot and cold water"
**You mix 5.0 dl of water at 80 degrees with 3.0 dl of water at 20 degrees. What is
the final temperature of the mixture? Ignore energy losses to the surroundings.**

::: handskrift
typ: blandvatten
:::

::: textlosning
Since [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]] flows from hot to cold, it is the hot water that gives off
energy and the cold water that absorbs the same energy.

$$
E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp} \qquad (1)
$$

The hot water cools down when it gives off energy, so
$E_\text{avg} = c \cdot m_v \cdot \Delta T_v$, where $m_v$ and $\Delta T_v$ stand
for the mass and the temperature change of the hot water.

The cold water is heated when it absorbs energy, so
$E_\text{upp} = c \cdot m_k \cdot \Delta T_k$, where $m_k$ and $\Delta T_k$ stand
for the mass and the temperature change of the cold water.

Substitution into (1), $E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp}$, gives

$$
c \cdot m_v \cdot \Delta T_v = c \cdot m_k \cdot \Delta T_k
$$

Dividing both sides by *c*:

$$
m_v \cdot \Delta T_v = m_k \cdot \Delta T_k \qquad (2)
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m_v = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}\ (\text{5 dl of water has a mass of about 0.50 kg}) \\
m_k = 0.30\ \mathrm{kg}\ (\text{3 dl of water has a mass of about 0.30 kg}) \\
\Delta T_v = 80 - T \\
\Delta T_k = T - 20
\end{array} \right]
$$

If we call the final temperature *T*, then

$$
0.50 \cdot (80 - T) = 0.30 \cdot (T - 20)
$$

$$
40 - 0.50 T = 0.30 T - 6
$$

$$
46 = 0.80 T \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad T = \frac{46}{0.80} = 57.5\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \approx 58\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
$$

**Answer:** 58 °C

::: tips "Alternative solution: weighted mean"
When it is the same liquid being mixed and no kind of phase change (for
example melting or vaporisation) takes place, the task is quicker to solve
with a **weighted mean**. The formula for the weighted mean is not in the
formula sheet, so you will have to remember it yourself. Nor do you need
to use SI units then.

$$
T_\text{final} = \frac{V_1 \cdot T_1 + V_2 \cdot T_2}{V_1 + V_2}
= \frac{5.0\ \mathrm{dl} \cdot 80\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 3.0\ \mathrm{dl} \cdot 20\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}{(5.0 + 3.0)\ \mathrm{dl}}
= 57.5\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
$$

**Answer:** 58 °C
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Specific heat capacity"
- How much energy is needed to raise **$1\ \mathrm{kg}$** of a substance by **$1\ \mathrm{K}$**.
- Written $c$ and has the unit J/(kg · K).
- Looked up in a table of thermal data. Different for different substances **and for different phases**:
  ice and liquid water do not have the same $c$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The formula"
- $E = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T$
- Written $Q = c \cdot m \cdot \Delta T$ in the formula booklet.
- The same formula applies to **heating** (energy is absorbed) and
  **cooling** (energy is given off).
:::

::: sampunkt "Energy balance"
- Without energy losses: $E_\text{avg} = E_\text{upp}$.
- The energy given off by the hot object is equal to the energy absorbed by the
  cold one.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to solve"
1. Find $c$ in the table.
2. Convert the mass to kg. One litre of water has a mass of about one kilogram.
3. $\Delta T = T_2 - T_1$. A temperature **difference** is the same in
   kelvin as in Celsius, so here you do not need to convert.
4. Combine with $E = P \cdot t$ when the time is asked for.
:::

::: sampunkt "The heat capacity of water"
- $c_\text{water} = 4.18\ \mathrm{kJ/(kg \cdot K)}$
- Unusually high, which is why water is so good at storing heat.
:::
:::
