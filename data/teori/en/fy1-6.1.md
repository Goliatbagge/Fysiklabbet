# Heat and temperature

::: demo "The feeling of warmth"
Touch an aluminium plate and a plastic plate. Which one has the higher [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance are moving. SI unit kelvin (K).]]?
The aluminium plate feels colder, but both have the same temperature! A check
with an infrared thermometer shows that both are at room temperature, about 20 °C. The reason
aluminium feels colder than plastic is that metals conduct [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]] better than
plastic. The aluminium plate therefore conducts heat away from our hand, at about 25 degrees,
faster than the plastic does. When our hand loses energy we experience it
as cold.
:::

::: demo "Melting race"
Put an ice cube on each of the aluminium and plastic plates. On which plate
will the ice cube melt fastest? On the plastic plate, since it feels
warmest? Equally fast, since both plates have the same temperature? No, on
the aluminium plate! How is that possible? The ice cube is colder than both
the aluminium plate and the plastic plate. Heat flows from hot to cold.
Since aluminium conducts heat better than plastic, the aluminium will transfer
heat to the ice cube faster, and the ice cube will then also melt faster.
:::

## Internal energy and heat

The atoms in a material have both [[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]] and [[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]. The
potential energy is stored in the chemical bonds. The kinetic energy
is in the motion of the atoms (and is what makes up the thermal energy). The sum
of potential energy and kinetic energy in a material forms the **internal energy**.

Energy that, by itself, flows from a warmer system to a colder one
is called **heat**. You could say that heat is "energy in transit". Heat
that flows _to_ us, for example from a hot fire, we experience as warm.
Heat that flows _from_ us, for example to the air outdoors in cold
weather, we experience as cold. So physically there is no such thing as cold!
It is the direction of the heat transfer that gives the sensation of warm or cold.

Heat transfer can take place in three different ways.

- **Conduction:** The transfer takes place through materials that are in contact, for example
  when heat is transferred from a hotplate to the saucepan or when heat is conducted
  away from your hand when you hold an ice cube.
- **Radiation:** Does not need a medium to be transferred. The heat of the Sun is
  transferred by radiation through space (vacuum). In fact, heat is
  given off by radiation from all objects with a temperature above absolute
  zero. Hold your hand close to your cheek and you will feel that you yourself radiate
  heat.
- **Flow/convection:** Takes place when a gas or liquid "swirls around", for
  example when we heat a room with a radiator. The cold air at the radiator
  is warmed up and rises. Cold air flows in where the warmed air
  was before and is warmed up. This creates a "stirring" of the air being heated.
  It is also convection that gives us who live in the north of the globe
  warm air (that has been heated by the Sun at more southerly latitudes).

## Temperature

The average kinetic energy of the atoms of an object is called
**temperature**. The faster the atoms move, the more collisions there are and the
higher the temperature of the object.

If the atoms in an object are completely still, there is no kinetic energy and the object
has then reached **[[absolute zero|The lowest possible temperature, 0 K = −273.15 °C. All thermal motion stops there.]]**, which is −273 °C (−273.15 °C). That
is the theoretically lowest temperature that can be reached.

The temperature is _not_ affected by the potential energy of the atoms. In a glass of
ice cubes and juice at zero degrees, potential energy is stored in chemical
bonds inside the ice cube. When the surrounding air melts the ice cube, the
internal energy increases as chemical bonds are broken, but the temperature does not (and
the juice stays cold).

::: kuriosa "High temperatures"
There is no natural highest possible temperature in the same way that
there is a lowest possible temperature. Some examples of high
temperatures:

| Source | Temperature |
| --- | --- |
| The surface of the Sun | about 5 500 °C |
| Lightning bolt | about 50 000 °C |
| Inside the Sun and hydrogen bombs | about 10⁷ °C |
| Supernovae | about 10¹² °C |
| The Hagedorn temperature (theoretical guess, when matter stops being stable) | about 10¹⁵ °C |
| Tomato on a toasted sandwich* | ∞ |

*WARNING! Physics joke.
:::

The Celsius scale, which is the temperature scale we use in everyday life,
was created by the Swede Anders Celsius (1700s) and is used in almost the whole
world. It is built on two fixed points, namely the freezing point of water, 0 °C,
and the boiling point of water, 100 °C.

For the relationship between the measured values to agree with physics'
definition of temperature (double the temperature should give double the kinetic energy
of the atoms), we need the **kelvin scale**, which starts from absolute zero
with the same scale steps as the Celsius scale.

::: formel "The kelvin scale"
$$
T_K = T_C + 273
$$

where

- $T_K$ = temperature (K)
- $T_C$ = temperature (°C)
:::

| Celsius | Kelvin |
| --- | --- |
| −273 °C | 0 K |
| 0 °C | 273 K |
| 20 °C | 293 K |

::: kuriosa "The Fahrenheit scale"
The Fahrenheit scale was created in 1727 by the German physicist Daniel Gabriel
Fahrenheit. It is used in the USA, but hardly anywhere else in the world.
The fixed points were set from the highest and lowest temperatures in Fahrenheit's
home country. 0 °F = −18 °C and 100 °F = 38 °C. One scale step on the Fahrenheit scale
corresponds to 5/9 of a scale step on the Celsius scale. The Fahrenheit scale is not used at all
in physics.
:::

::: tips "Going further: the physics of blowing"
**Why does it feel cold if you blow hard on your arm and warm if you blow
gently?**

Think about the temperatures of different things and about heat flowing from
hot to cold. Your skin is about 30 °C. Since heat radiates, the
air closest to your body will also be about 30 °C. The surrounding air is
about 20 °C (that is, colder).

When you blow hard on your arm, you blow away the 30-degree air from your
arm and drag along colder air from the surroundings. Your 30-degree arm now comes
into contact with 20-degree air and heat flows away from the arm, which you
perceive as cold. In addition, moisture **evaporates** from your arm, which is also
a cooling (endothermic) process.

When you blow gently on your arm, you do not drag along colder air from the surroundings;
instead it is rather the moist, almost 37-degree air from your
lungs that hits the arm. Your 30-degree arm now comes into contact with 37-degree
air and heat flows from the warm air to the arm, which you
perceive as warm. In addition, moisture from your exhaled air **condenses**,
which is also a warming (exothermic) process.

Finally: when you blow hard on your arm in a sauna where the air is about 75 °C,
it does NOT feel cold, but extremely hot! Then you instead replace
the 30-degree air near your arm with 75-degree air. A lot of heat will then
flow from the air to your arm instead, which burns!
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Internal energy and heat"
- **Internal energy**: the sum of the potential energy of the atoms (in chemical
  bonds) and their kinetic energy.
- **Heat**: energy that **by itself** flows from warmer to
  colder. In other words, energy in transit.
- **Cold does not exist physically.** It is the direction of the heat flow that gives
  the sensation of warm or cold.
:::

::: sampunkt "Three ways of transferring heat"
- **Conduction**: through materials that are in contact, for example a hotplate
  against a saucepan.
- **Radiation**: needs no medium, passes through vacuum. Everything warmer than
  absolute zero radiates.
- **Flow (convection)**: a gas or liquid swirls around, for example
  the air around a radiator.
:::

::: sampunkt "Temperature"
- The **average kinetic energy** of the atoms.
- Faster atoms mean a higher temperature.
- The temperature is **not** affected by the potential energy. That is why
  the temperature does not rise while ice melts, even though energy is supplied.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why metal feels colder"
- An aluminium plate and a plastic plate in the same room have **the same
  temperature**.
- Metal **conducts heat better** and therefore draws heat out of the hand
  faster, which is experienced as cold.
- For the same reason an ice cube melts **faster** on the aluminium plate.
:::

::: sampunkt "Temperature scales"
- **Celsius**: the fixed points are the freezing point and boiling point of water.
- **Kelvin**: starts from **absolute zero**,
  $-273\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, with the same scale steps as Celsius.
- $T_K = T_C + 273$
- Kelvin is needed when double the temperature should mean double the kinetic energy.
:::
:::
