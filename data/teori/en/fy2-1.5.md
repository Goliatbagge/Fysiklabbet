# Circular motion

If a body moves in a circular path, it performs a **circular motion**.

::: tips "Why is a force needed in circular motion?"
[[Newton's first law|A body remains at rest or in uniform motion if the resultant force is zero.]] (the law of inertia) says that a body continues straight ahead
with [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] if there is no [[resultant force|The sum of all the forces acting on an object (a vector sum). Gives the object its acceleration.]]. But in
a circular motion the path curves. So there must be a deflecting
resultant [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]]!
:::

In circular motion with <u>constant [[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]]</u>, the body is acted on by a
resultant force that is **always directed in towards the centre of the
circular path**. This force is called the **[[centripetal force|The force needed to keep an object in circular motion, directed towards the centre of the circle.]]** $F_C$ and
gives rise to a **[[centripetal acceleration|The acceleration of an object in circular motion. It is directed in towards the centre of the circular path and has the magnitude *v*² / *r*.]]** $a_C$.

::: tips "Note: the centripetal force is not a separate force"
The centripetal force is **not** a separate force, but is the resultant of the
force or forces that together act on an object in a circular path.
The centripetal force can be made up of [[tension|The force acting along a taut rope, line or spring.]] in a string, [[frictional force|The force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact.]],
[[normal force|The force a surface exerts perpendicular to an object resting on it.]], [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and is directed straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] and so on.
:::

::: formel "Centripetal acceleration"
$$
a_C = \frac{v^{2}}{r} = \omega^{2} \cdot r
$$

where

- $a_C$ = centripetal acceleration (m/s²)
- *v* = speed in the direction of motion (m/s)
- *r* = radius of the path (m)
- *ω* = angular velocity (rad/s)

During the time $\Delta t$ the body covers the distance
$\Delta s = v \cdot \Delta t$ along the path, and the radius then turns through the angle
$\Delta \alpha$. If the time is short, the angle is small, and then the chord between
the two positions is as long as the arc.

The velocity is always tangential, that is, perpendicular to the radius. When
the radius turns through the angle $\Delta \alpha$, the velocity therefore turns
by the same amount. The two triangles in the figure, the one with the sides *r*, *r* and
$\Delta s$ and the one with the sides *v*, *v* and $\Delta v$, therefore have the same
apex angle and two equal sides. They are similar, which gives

$$
\frac{\Delta v}{v} = \frac{\Delta s}{r}
$$

Substituting $\Delta s = v \cdot \Delta t$ gives

$$
\frac{\Delta v}{v} = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{r}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v^{2}}{r}
$$

The quotient $\dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ is exactly the acceleration. The shorter the time
$\Delta t$ we choose, the more $\Delta v$ points straight in towards the centre,
and that is the direction of the centripetal acceleration. So

$$
a_C = \frac{v^{2}}{r}
$$

With the speed in the path $v = \omega \cdot r$ we also get the second form

$$
a_C = \frac{(\omega \cdot r)^{2}}{r} = \frac{\omega^{2} \cdot r^{2}}{r}
= \omega^{2} \cdot r
$$

Q.E.D.
:::
:::

::: formel "Centripetal force"
$$
F_C = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r} = \frac{4\pi^{2} \cdot m \cdot r}{T^{2}}
$$

where

- $F_C$ = centripetal force (N)
- *m* = the mass of the object performing the motion (kg)
- *v* = speed in the direction of motion (m/s)
- *r* = radius of the path (m)
- *T* = [[period|The time for one complete oscillation or one complete wave: *T* = 1 / *f*.]] (s)

::: härledning "Derivation #1: Centripetal force"
When there is acceleration, there is a resultant force. According to [[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]]

$$
F_R = m \cdot a \qquad (1)
$$

Substituting $F_R = F_C$ and the centripetal acceleration
$a = a_C = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r}$ into (1) gives

$$
F_C = m \cdot \frac{v^{2}}{r} = \frac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r}
$$

Q.E.D.
:::

::: härledning "Derivation #2: Centripetal force with the period"
We know from before that the speed in the path is $v = \dfrac{2\pi \cdot r}{T}$.
Putting this into $F_C = \dfrac{m \cdot v^{2}}{r}$ gives

$$
F_C = \frac{m \cdot \left(\tfrac{2\pi \cdot r}{T}\right)^{2}}{r}
= \frac{m \cdot \tfrac{4\pi^{2} \cdot r^{2}}{T^{2}}}{r}
= m \cdot \frac{4\pi^{2} \cdot r}{T^{2}}
= \frac{4\pi^{2} \cdot m \cdot r}{T^{2}}
$$

Q.E.D.
:::
:::

Investigate the relationship yourself in the simulation below. A car drives at constant
speed in a circular path, seen from directly above, and the frictional force from
the road provides the centripetal force. Change the speed and watch the force arrow
grow. With the switch at the top right you change the situation. On **Race track**
the car drives lap after lap on a closed track with one wide and one tight bend:
on the straights no sideways force is needed, in the bends the frictional force points
in towards the centre, and if the grip is not enough, the car continues straight ahead. In
**Loop** the car drives on the inside of a vertical circular track, and the normal force
and the gravitational force together provide the centripetal force. In **Crest and dip**
you see the car from the side driving over two crests with a dip in between:
the normal force decreases on the crest and increases in the dip, and above the limiting speed
the car leaves the road.

::: kuriosa "Centrifugal force"
Centrifugal force is an **imaginary force** (a force that does not exist) that
describes the experience of someone in a rotating system. If, for example, we
are sitting in a car that turns to the left, it feels as if we are acted on by a
force to the right, outwards from the path. This is a consequence of the fact that we actually
want to continue straight ahead in the path because of our [[inertia|An object's resistance to a change in its motion. Greater mass → greater inertia.]] (Newton's first
law). When the car then curves to the left, we experience it as if we, relative to
the car, "want to move" to the right. Seen from a frame of reference outside the car,
we "want" only to continue straight ahead, tangentially to the direction of the path.
:::

::: demo "Demonstration: Centrifuge"

move **out** from the path, but **tangentially** in the direction of motion,

::: kuriosa "Lighter at the equator"
Stand on a pair of scales at the North Pole. There you are standing still: the normal force from
the scales balances the gravitational force exactly, $F_N = F_G$, and the scales show your whole
weight.

At the equator, on the other hand, you travel along with the Earth's rotation, one revolution per day in
a circular path with the Earth's radius $r = 6\,378\ \mathrm{km}$. You are therefore performing
a circular motion, and then there must be a centripetal force directed in
towards the centre of the Earth. The centripetal force is not a separate force, but must
be made up of the forces already acting on you. This only works out if
the gravitational force is slightly larger than the normal force, as the figure below shows.

At the pole, the normal force balances the gravitational force exactly, and the centripetal force
is zero. At the equator, the gravitational force must also provide a centripetal force, and
then the normal force becomes smaller. The dashed arrow is the resultant, not a
separate force. The difference between the arrows is greatly exaggerated in the figure.
:::

The difference between the forces is exactly the centripetal force:

$$
F_G - F_N = F_C
$$

A pair of scales shows the normal force $F_N$, so it shows less at the equator. How
much less? The centripetal acceleration at the equator is

$$
a_C = \omega^{2} \cdot r
= \left( \frac{2\pi}{86\,400\ \mathrm{s}} \right)^{2} \cdot 6.378 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}
\approx 0.034\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

which is only about 0.3 % of the acceleration due to gravity $g$. A person with
a mass of 80 kg therefore presses about 2.7 N less on the scales at the equator
than at the pole, and scales graduated in kilograms show about 0.3 kg less.
The mass is of course the same everywhere: it is the normal force that decreases,
and that is what scales measure.

In reality the difference is even larger, about 0.5 %. The rotation has
also flattened the Earth, so that a person standing at the equator
is also further from the centre of the Earth, where
the gravitational force is weaker. But even on a perfectly round Earth,
the rotation alone would make you lighter at the equator.
:::

$$

We divide both sides by *m* and solve for the speed *v*.

and Newton's law of gravitation, in two classic worked examples about a swing and

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Why a force is needed"
- Newton's first law: without a resultant force, the body continues **straight
  ahead**.
- In a circular path the path curves, so there must be a deflecting
  resultant force.
:::

::: sampunkt "Centripetal force"
- The part of the resultant force that is **directed in towards
  the centre** and bends the path. At **constant speed** the whole
  resultant is centripetal force. If the speed changes, the resultant also has a
  component **along the path**.
- $F_C = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}
  = \dfrac{4\pi^{2} m r}{T^{2}}$
- **Not a separate force**, but made up of tension, friction,
  normal force or gravitational force.
:::

::: sampunkt "Centripetal acceleration"
- $a_C = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r} = \omega^{2} r$
- Also directed in towards the centre.
:::

::: sampunkt "Centrifugal force does not exist"
- The outward force you feel in a bend is **imaginary**.
- In fact the body wants to continue **straight ahead** because of its
  inertia.
- The centrifuge and the sparkler show this: the droplets and the sparks fly off
  **tangentially**, not radially outwards.
:::

::: sampunkt "Vertical circle"
- **At the top**: the gravitational force helps, so
  $F_C = F_S + F_G$ gives $F_S = F_C - F_G$.
- **At the bottom**: the gravitational force works against it, so
  $F_S = F_C + F_G$.
- The rope is therefore under the **most** tension at the lowest point.
:::

::: sampunkt "The lowest speed at the top"
- When the rope is slack, $F_S = 0$, so the gravitational force alone makes up
  the centripetal force.
- $mg = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}$ gives
  $v = \sqrt{g r}$.
- Often combined with the **principle of conservation of energy** to find the speed at another
  position.
:::
:::
