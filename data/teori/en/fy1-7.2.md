# Forces between charges and Coulomb's law

The electric [[force|An influence that can change the motion or shape of an object. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] with which two charged objects affect each other
is calculated with **[[Coulomb's law|The force between two point charges: *F* = *k* · *Q*₁ · *Q*₂ / *r*². Decreases with the square of the distance.]]**. The larger the charges and the shorter the distance
between them, the greater the force.

**NOTE!** If two charged objects affect each other, the electric
force is **equally large** on both objects, but in opposite directions, according to
[[Newton's third law|For every force (action) there is an equal and opposite force (reaction).]]. This applies even if one charge is
larger than the other. (Compare with Newton's law of gravitation: the Earth pulls
you down with a certain force and you pull the Earth up with the same force, even though
you have different masses.)

::: formel "Coulomb's law"
$$
F = k \cdot \frac{|Q_1| \cdot |Q_2|}{r^2}
$$

where

- *F* = electric force (N)
- *k* = Coulomb's [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the gravitational acceleration *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] ≈ 8.99 · 10⁹ N·m²/C²
- $|Q_1|$, $|Q_2|$ = charge 1 and charge 2 respectively (C), ignoring the sign
- *r* = distance between charge 1 and charge 2 (m)

Remember: the elementary charge = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C.
:::

::: kuriosa "Beautiful similarities"
There are beautiful similarities between Newton's law of gravitation and Coulomb's law.
Newton's law of gravitation has the form $F = G \cdot m_1 m_2 / d^2$ and Coulomb's
law has the form $F = k \cdot q_1 q_2 / r^2$: the same structure, but with masses
and charges respectively.
:::

::: tips "Coach's tip #1"
When calculating the electric force *F*, do not bother to insert the
signs of the charges. If you need to determine the direction of the force, reason
from the signs of the charges. **Like signs repel** (push each other
away) and **opposite signs attract** (pull towards each other).
:::

::: tips "Coach's tip #2"
We often calculate with the charge of the electron, 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. To avoid
typing it into the calculator every time, we can store the value in a variable on
the calculator. If we store the value in, for example, the variable *E* (as in **e**lectron),
we only need to press "E" on the calculator to use the value.
:::

::: handskrift
typ: coulombkulor

::: textlosning
**a)** We set up Coulomb's law.

$$
F = k \cdot \frac{Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{r^2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
Q_1 = 5.0\ \mathrm{nC} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C} \\
Q_2 = 3.0\ \mathrm{nC} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C} \\
r = 30\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.30\ \mathrm{m} \\
k \approx 8.99 \cdot 10^9\ \mathrm{N \cdot m^2 / C^2}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F = 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.30^2}
= 1.498\ldots \cdot 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{N} \approx 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu N}
$$

Since the charges have opposite signs, the negative charge will
be pulled towards the positive charge.

**Answer:** 1.5 μN towards the positive charge.

**b)** According to Newton's third law, the force on the positive charge
will be the same, but directed towards the negative charge.

**Answer:** 1.5 μN towards the negative charge.
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: How does the force change?"
**Two charged spheres affect each other with a certain electric force. How
does the force change if the distance between the spheres is halved and the charge of
one of the spheres is doubled?**

::: handskrift
typ: andradkraft
:::

::: textlosning
We have no measured values, so we solve the task in general terms. Assume that the charges
are initially $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ and the distance is *r*. Then the initial
electric force $F_1$ is

$$
F_1 = k \cdot \frac{Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{r^2}
$$

If the charge of one of the spheres is doubled, $Q_1$ is doubled and
becomes $(2 Q_1)$. If the distance is halved, *r* is halved and becomes
$r/2$. We calculate the new force $F_2$ by inserting these expressions
into Coulomb's law and simplifying.

$$
F_2 = k \cdot \frac{(2 Q_1) \cdot Q_2}{\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^2}
= k \cdot \frac{2 \cdot Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{\frac{r^2}{4}}
= k \cdot \frac{4 \cdot 2 \cdot Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{r^2}
= k \cdot \frac{8 \cdot Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{r^2}
$$

So $F_1 = k \cdot Q_1 Q_2 / r^2$ and $F_2 = k \cdot 8 \cdot Q_1 Q_2 / r^2$.
We see that the force afterwards, $F_2$, is 8 times as large.

**Answer:** It becomes 8 times larger.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Coulomb's law"
- $F = k \cdot \dfrac{|Q_1| \cdot |Q_2|}{r^2}$
- $k$ is Coulomb's constant,
  $8.99 \cdot 10^{9}\ \mathrm{N \cdot m^2/C^2}$.
- $r$ is the distance between the charges, in metres.
- Larger charges give a larger force. Double the distance gives only a
  **quarter** of the force.
:::

::: sampunkt "Calculate without signs"
- Insert the **magnitudes** of the charges, that is ignore the signs.
- Determine the direction by **reasoning**: like signs repel, opposite
  signs attract.
:::

::: sampunkt "The force is equally large on both"
- According to Newton's third law, the electric force is **equally large** on
  both charges, but in opposite directions.
- This applies even when one charge is much larger than the other.
:::

::: sampunkt "The same form as the law of gravitation"
- Coulomb: $F = k \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$.
- Newton: $F = G \dfrac{m_1 m_2}{d^2}$.
- The same structure, with charges instead of masses. The difference is that
  gravity always pulls, while electric forces both pull and
  push.
:::

::: sampunkt "In practice"
- Convert the distance to **metres** and the charges to **coulombs**
  before substituting. nC is $10^{-9}$ C.
- Store the elementary charge in a variable on the calculator, so you do not have to
  type it in every time.
:::
:::
