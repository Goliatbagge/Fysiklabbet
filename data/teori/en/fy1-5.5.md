# Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle

::: demo "What floats"
We put different balls into a beaker of water. The table tennis ball floats high
above the surface, the rubber ball floats low in the surface and the metal ball does not float
at all. The [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and is directed straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] on the balls that float acts downwards. Since they do not
sink, they must be acted on by an equally large **[[buoyant force|The upward force from a liquid or gas on an object that is immersed in it.]]** from the water. We
also put in an aluminium tray, which floats.
:::

Objects that are completely or partly immersed in a liquid are acted on by an
upward [[force|An influence that can change the motion or shape of an object. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] from the liquid called the **buoyant force** (upthrust), which is given by
**[[Archimedes' principle|An object immersed in a liquid is acted on by a buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced liquid.]]**. The reason we get an upward buoyant force from the liquid is
that the upward pressure force on the bottom is greater than the downward pressure force on the top,
because the liquid pressure is greater further down.

Even when an object is completely immersed in water, it is acted on by a buoyant force.
An object under water feels "lighter". However, there is **no**
loss of mass; the reason it feels lighter is a smaller
downward **[[resultant force|The sum of all forces acting on an object (vector sum). Gives the object its acceleration.]]**.

When something floats, $F_\text{lyft} = F_\text{tyngd}$, and when something
sinks, $F_\text{lyft} < F_\text{tyngd}$.

Archimedes' principle is named after the Greek physicist Archimedes who,
according to an anecdote, came to this insight when he stepped into his bathtub and
the water overflowed. Overjoyed, he ran naked through the streets shouting
"eureka!" (I have found it!).

::: formel "Buoyant force (Archimedes' principle) in words"
A body that is completely or partly immersed in a liquid is acted on by a
buoyant force that is **equal to the weight of the displaced liquid**.
:::

::: formel "Buoyant force (Archimedes' principle)"
$$
F_L = \rho \cdot g \cdot V
$$

where

- $F_L$ = buoyant force (N)
- *ρ* = [[density|Mass per unit volume: *ρ* = *m* / *V*. SI unit kg/m³. Determines whether an object floats in a medium.]] of the displaced liquid (kg/m³)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg)
- *V* = volume of the displaced liquid / volume of the object below the surface (m³)

::: härledning "Derivation: Buoyant force"
The buoyant force $F_L$ is equal to the weight of the displaced liquid. If the
displaced liquid has the mass $m_v$, then

$$
F_L = m_v \cdot g
$$

According to the formula for density, for a liquid with volume *V* and density
*ρ*,

$$
\rho = \frac{m_v}{V} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m_v = \rho \cdot V
$$

Substitution gives

$$
F_L = \rho \cdot V \cdot g = \rho \cdot g \cdot V \qquad\text{QED}
$$
:::
:::

For floating objects, $F_L = F_G$. For sinking objects,
$F_L < F_G$.

::: kuriosa "Buoyancy"
Another way of looking at buoyancy is that things float only if the
body as a whole has a lower density than the medium it is in. The
overall density of an iron ship is less than the density of
water (the ship contains a lot of air). That is why it floats. For the same reason
it is impossible to drown in quicksand, since quicksand has a higher density
(2 000 kg/m³) than the human body (1 000 kg/m³).
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Boat of 450 kg"
**A boat has a mass of 450 kg. How large is a) the buoyant force the water exerts on the boat,
b) the volume of water the boat displaces?**

::: handskrift
typ: batlyft
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The boat floats, so $F_L = F_G$.

$$
F_L = F_G = m \cdot g
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 450\ \mathrm{kg} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_L = 450 \cdot 9.82 = 4\,419\ \mathrm{N} \approx 4.4\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 4.4 kN

**b)** We solve for the volume from Archimedes' principle.

$$
F_L = \rho \cdot V \cdot g \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad V = \frac{F_L}{\rho \cdot g}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_L = 4\,419\ \mathrm{N} \\
\rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
V = \frac{4\,419}{998 \cdot 9.82} = 0.450\ldots\ \mathrm{m^3} \approx 0.45\ \mathrm{m^3}
$$

**Answer:** 0.45 m³ (450 litres)
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Iron chest on the seabed"
**An iron chest measuring 70 × 90 × 40 cm with a mass of 260 kg stands on the
seabed. Would you be able to lift the chest?**

::: handskrift
typ: jarnkista
:::

::: textlosning
Since the chest stands on the bottom, the downward gravitational force $F_G$ must be greater than
the upward buoyant force $F_L$. The force *F* we must apply to lift the chest
is the difference between the gravitational force and the buoyant force, that is

$$
F = F_G - F_L
$$

**Note** that when we calculate the buoyant force with Archimedes' principle, it is
the density of the **water** we must use, not that of the iron!

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_G = m \cdot g = 260 \cdot 9.82 = 2\,553.2\ \mathrm{N} \\
F_L = \rho \cdot g \cdot V
     = 998 \cdot 9.82 \cdot (0.70 \cdot 0.90 \cdot 0.40)
     = 2\,469.690\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F = F_G - F_L = 2\,553.2 - 2\,469.690\ldots = 83.509\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Are you strong enough to lift the chest? We work out which mass gives the corresponding weight.

$$
m = \frac{F}{g} = \frac{83.509\ldots}{9.82} = 8.504\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 8.5\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

**Answer:** Yes, you would be able to lift the chest. It only feels like 8.5 kg.

::: tips "Alternative solution"
To make the calculations simpler, we can convert all the measurements to dm³ and
use the fact that 1 litre of water has a mass of about 1 kg. Remember that 1 dm³ = 1 litre.

The volume of the displaced water is

$$
V = 7\ \mathrm{dm} \cdot 9\ \mathrm{dm} \cdot 4\ \mathrm{dm}
= 252\ \mathrm{dm^3} = 252\ \mathrm{litres}
$$

Since 1 litre of water has a mass of about 1 kg, 252 litres of water must have a mass of about
252 kg. The buoyant force therefore corresponds to the weight of 252 kg, while the gravitational force
corresponds to the weight of 260 kg. This means that the force you must apply
to lift the chest corresponds to

$$
260 - 252 = 8\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

**Answer:** Yes, you would be able to lift the chest. It only feels like about 8 kg.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Buoyant force"
- An object that is completely or partly immersed in a liquid is acted on by an
  **upward buoyant force** $F_L$.
- The cause: the liquid pressure is greater further down, so the pressure force **from below
  is greater** than the one from above.
:::

::: sampunkt "Archimedes' principle"
- The buoyant force is **equal to the weight of the displaced liquid**.
- $F_L = \rho \cdot g \cdot V$
- $\rho$ is the density of the **liquid**, never of the object. $V$ is the volume that
  is below the surface.
:::

::: sampunkt "Float or sink"
- **Floats**: $F_L = F_G$.
- **Sinks**: $F_L < F_G$.
- Alternative view: something floats if its **overall density** is
  lower than that of the medium. An iron ship floats because it mostly contains
  air.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why something feels lighter in water"
- No mass disappears. The downward **resultant force** just becomes
  smaller.
- The force needed to lift: $F = F_G - F_L$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The quick method"
- Calculate the volume in **cubic decimetres**, that is litres.
- One litre of water has a mass of about one kilogram, so the volume in litres is directly
  the buoyant force expressed as a mass in kilograms.
- A chest of $260\ \mathrm{kg}$ that displaces 252 litres feels like
  $8\ \mathrm{kg}$.
:::
:::
