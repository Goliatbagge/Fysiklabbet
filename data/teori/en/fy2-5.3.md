# Phases of the Moon and eclipses

The Moon orbits the Earth with a [[period|The time for one complete oscillation or one complete wave cycle: *T* = 1 / *f*.]] of about **29.5 days** (a
**synodic month**). Since the Moon has no light of its own, we only see the
part that is lit by the Sun. Depending on where in its orbit the Moon is,
seen from the Earth, we see different parts of the Moon's lit side.
This gives rise to the **phases of the Moon**.

## Waxing and waning

During the **waxing** phase, from new moon to full moon, the lit part
grows from the **right**. During the **waning** phase, from full moon to
new moon, the light shrinks from the right so that the Moon shines more
towards the left. This applies as seen from the **northern hemisphere**;
in the southern hemisphere the direction is reversed.

::: tips "Memory rule for waxing/waning"
In the northern hemisphere:

- If the Moon looks like a **D** (bright right side), it is **waxing**
  towards full moon.
- If the Moon looks like a **C** (bright left side), it is **waning**
  towards new moon.

In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round: a C means waxing, a D
waning.
:::

## The difference between a synodic and a sidereal month

The Moon takes **27.3 days** to go once around the Earth measured against
the fixed stars (a **sidereal month**). But since the Earth has moved some
distance along its orbit around the Sun in the same time, the Moon has to
go a little **more** than one full revolution to return to the same
position relative to the Sun and the Earth. That is why the **synodic
month** (from new moon to new moon) is longer:
**29.5 days**.

## Eclipses: when all three line up

Most of the time the Moon passes slightly **above** or **below** the line
between the Sun and the Earth. But a couple of times a year the three
bodies stand almost exactly on **a straight line**, and then an
**eclipse** occurs: one of the bodies ends up in the shadow of another.

- A **solar eclipse** happens at **new moon**: the Moon lies between the
  Sun and the Earth and casts its shadow on the Earth's surface.
- A **lunar eclipse** happens at **full moon**: the Earth lies between the
  Sun and the Moon, and the Moon glides into the Earth's shadow.

The shadow behind an illuminated body always has two parts. The Sun is not
a point but an **extended** light source: innermost there is a region that
no direct sunlight reaches at all, the [[umbra|The part of a shadow that no direct light from the light source reaches.]], and around it a
region reached by light from *part* of the solar disc, the [[penumbra|The part of a shadow reached by light from only part of the light source.]].

A solar eclipse, seen from the side (the figure is not to scale). The rays from the upper and lower edges of the Sun are tangent to the Moon and build up the shadow. The Moon is small, and its umbra narrows to a point at roughly the
Earth's distance. That is why the umbra only hits a small patch of the
Earth's surface, usually **100–300 km** wide. The patch sweeps across the
Earth at well over 1 000 km/h, and at any given place totality lasts at
most **7.5 minutes**, usually only two or three. In the penumbra, which is
several thousand kilometres wide, you instead see that a **part** of the
solar disc is hidden.

That the Moon **can** cover the Sun at all is a coincidence. The Sun is
about 400 times larger in diameter than the Moon, but it is also about
400 times further away. That is why both appear equally large in the
sky, about half a degree across. This measure is called the body's
**angular diameter**.

::: formel "Angular diameter"
$$
\alpha \approx \frac{2R}{d}
$$

where

- *α* = angular diameter in radians (valid for small angles)
- *R* = radius of the body
- *d* = distance to the body
:::

::: exempel "Example: The angular diameter of the Moon and the Sun (Intermediate)"
**The Moon's radius is $1.74 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ and its mean distance from
the Earth is $3.84 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}$. The Sun's radius is
$6.96 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}$ and the distance to the Sun is
$1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$. Calculate the angular diameter of<br>a) the Moon&emsp;&emsp;b) the Sun.**

::: handskrift
typ: vinkeldiameter
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** Substitute the Moon's values into the formula:

$$
\alpha_\text{Moon} \approx \frac{2R}{d} = \frac{2 \cdot 1.74 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}}{3.84 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}} = 9.0625 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}
$$

Convert to degrees by multiplying by $\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi}$:

$$
9.0625 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \cdot \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} = 0.5192\ldots^\circ \approx 0.519^\circ
$$

**b)** The same calculation for the Sun:

$$
\alpha_\text{Sun} \approx \frac{2 \cdot 6.96 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}}{1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}} = 9.28 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}
$$

$$
9.28 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \cdot \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} = 0.5317\ldots^\circ \approx 0.532^\circ
$$

**Answer:** The Moon subtends an angle of 0.519° and the Sun 0.532°: almost
exactly the same size. It is precisely this coincidence that allows the
Moon to cover the solar disc.
:::
:::

### Annular eclipse

The Moon's orbit is an **ellipse**, so its distance from the Earth varies by
about 10 %. When the Moon is at its furthest, its angular diameter shrinks to
about 0.49°, smaller than the Sun's 0.53°. Then the tip of the umbra does
not reach all the way to the Earth's surface, and the Moon cannot cover the
whole solar disc. Around the black lunar disc a bright **ring** of the
Sun's surface remains: an **annular** solar eclipse.

::: tips "Three kinds of solar eclipse"
| Type | What happens | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total | The umbra hits the Earth's surface | The solar disc fully covered, the corona visible |
| Annular | The tip of the umbra does not reach | A bright ring around the lunar disc |
| Partial | Only the penumbra hits the place | Part of the solar disc hidden |
:::

### During totality

Only during the few minutes when the solar disc is **fully** covered can
you see what is otherwise drowned in sunlight:

- **The corona**: the Sun's outer atmosphere, a pearly shimmering halo
  stretching several solar radii out.
- **Prominences**: pinkish-red arcs of gas at the Sun's edge.
- **Baily's beads**: the last rays of sunlight, filtering through the
  valleys along the Moon's edge like a string of glittering beads.
- **The diamond ring**: the very last (and the very first) bead, flashing
  against the ring of the corona.

At the same time the temperature drops noticeably, the light falls to
twilight level and the brightest stars and planets appear.

::: kuriosa "Never look straight at the Sun"
Looking at the Sun, even when 99 % of the disc is covered, can cause
permanent damage to the retina, entirely without pain. The retina has no
pain nerves, so you notice nothing until the damage is already done.
Ordinary sunglasses, exposed film, smoked glass and CDs do **not**
protect you.

Use approved **eclipse glasses** (ISO 12312-2), or project the Sun's
image through a small hole onto a sheet of paper. Only during the total
phase, when the solar disc is fully covered, is it safe to look with the
naked eye. The very second the first ray of sunlight returns you must
look away.
:::

## Lunar eclipse

At full moon the Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon. If the three are
nearly on a straight line, the Moon glides into the Earth's shadow and
darkens strongly: a **lunar eclipse**.

A lunar eclipse, seen from the side (the figure is not to scale). The Earth is much larger than the Moon, so the umbra is still wide where the Moon passes, and the Moon fits entirely inside it. Red sunlight refracted in the Earth's atmosphere is bent into the umbra and colours the Moon copper red.
:::

Here the roles are reversed compared with the solar eclipse: the body
casting the shadow, the Earth, is now the **large** one. The Earth's umbra
is therefore about 2.5 times as wide as the Moon where the Moon passes,
and the Moon can lie entirely inside it for up to **1 hour and 40
minutes**. Three cases are distinguished:

::: tips "Three kinds of lunar eclipse"
| Type | What happens | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total | The whole Moon inside the umbra | The Moon darkens and turns copper red |
| Partial | Part of the Moon in the umbra | A dark, curved bite out of the lunar disc |
| Penumbral | The Moon only in the penumbra | Faint shading, easy to miss |
:::

### Why does the Moon turn red: the blood moon

The Moon does not go completely dark during totality but glows copper red.
The cause is the Earth's atmosphere, which does two things to the sunlight
that grazes past the Earth's edge:

1. **Refracts it**: the light is bent as it passes through the air and is
   steered into the umbra instead of continuing straight ahead.
2. **Scatters away the blue**: short-wavelength (blue) light is scattered
   in all directions by the molecules of the air, while long-wavelength
   (red) light gets through. It is the same **Rayleigh scattering** that
   makes the sky blue and the sunset red (see section 5.4).

What is left to light up the Moon is therefore the red light: in practice
the light from **all the Earth's sunrises and sunsets at once**. How dark
and how red the Moon becomes depends on how much dust and cloud the
atmosphere contains at the time; after large volcanic eruptions the Moon
can turn almost pitch black during totality.

::: tips "Solar eclipse or lunar eclipse?"
| | Solar eclipse | Lunar eclipse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phase of the Moon | New moon | Full moon |
| In the shadow lies | A patch of the Earth | The Moon |
| Visible from | A narrow band | The whole night side of the Earth |
| Total phase lasts | At most about 7.5 min | Up to about 1 h 40 min |
| Dangerous to look at | Yes, except during totality | No |
:::

So the reason lunar eclipses feel more common is not that they happen more
often, but that the **whole** night side of the Earth sees the same lunar
eclipse, while a solar eclipse is only visible from the narrow band where
the umbra happens to fall.

## Why eclipses do not happen every month

If the Moon's orbit lay in exactly the same plane as the Earth's orbit
around the Sun, there would be a solar eclipse at every new moon and a
lunar eclipse at every full moon: two a month. That is not the case,
because the Moon's orbit is **tilted about 5.1°** to the plane of the
Earth's orbit around the Sun (the [[ecliptic|The plane in which the Earth's orbit around the Sun lies. The Sun's apparent yearly path across the starry sky lies in the same plane.]]).

The Moon's orbit is tilted 5.1° to the ecliptic and crosses it at two points, the nodes. At an ordinary new moon the Moon lies above (or below) the ecliptic, and the shadow passes by the Earth. Only when the new moon occurs near a node do all three bodies line up.
:::

The two points where the Moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic are called
**nodes**. Only when the Moon is near a node *at the same time* as it is
new moon or full moon do the three bodies lie close enough to a straight
line. This happens during two **eclipse seasons** each year, and that is
why there are 2–5 solar eclipses per year, of which only a few are
total.

Since the band of the umbra is also so narrow, any one place on the Earth
sees a total solar eclipse on average only about once every **375 years**.
Travelling to the shadow is therefore the most common way to experience
one.

::: kuriosa "Saros: the rhythm of eclipses"
After **18 years, 11 days and 8 hours**, a period called the *saros*,
almost exactly the same relative position of the Sun, Earth and Moon
returns, and a very similar eclipse occurs again. The Babylonian
astronomers already knew the period and could predict eclipses with it,
long before anyone knew why it worked.

Those eight extra hours mean that the Earth has had time to turn a third
of a revolution, so the next eclipse in the same saros series is seen
about 120° further west.

Nor is the rhythm eternal: the Moon is moving away from the Earth by about
3.8 cm per year. In a few hundred million years the lunar disc will always
be too small to cover the Sun, and then every single solar eclipse will be
annular.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Phases of the Moon"
- The Moon has **no light of its own**. We only see the part that the Sun lights up.
- The phase depends on **where in its orbit** the Moon is, seen from the Earth.
- The eight phases: new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter,
  waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter,
  waning crescent.
:::

::: sampunkt "Waxing or waning"
- In the **northern hemisphere**: if the Moon looks like a **D** it is **waxing**,
  if it looks like a **C** it is **waning**.
- In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round.
:::

::: sampunkt "Two kinds of month"
- **Sidereal month**, 27.3 days: one revolution measured against the fixed stars.
- **Synodic month**, 29.5 days: from new moon to new moon.
- The difference arises because **the Earth moves** along its own orbit
  in the meantime, so the Moon has to go a little more than one revolution.
:::

::: sampunkt "Eclipses"
- A **solar eclipse** happens at **new moon**: the Moon between the Sun and the Earth.
- A **lunar eclipse** happens at **full moon**: the Earth between the Sun and
  the Moon.
- They do not happen every month, because the Moon's orbit usually lies slightly above
  or below the line.
:::

::: sampunkt "Umbra and penumbra"
- The Sun is an **extended** light source, not a point.
- **Umbra**: no direct sunlight at all. Here the eclipse is
  **total**.
- **Penumbra**: light from part of the solar disc gets through. Here it is
  **partial**.
- The umbra only hits a band of 100 to $300\ \mathrm{km}$, and totality
  lasts at most 7.5 minutes.
:::

::: sampunkt "Angular diameter"
- $\alpha \approx \dfrac{2R}{d}$, in radians and for small angles.
- The Sun is about 400 times larger than the Moon but also lies 400 times
  further away. That is why they look **almost equally large**, about half a
  degree.
- The Moon's orbit is an **ellipse**. When the Moon is far away the
  umbra does not reach, and a bright ring remains: an **annular**
  eclipse.
:::
:::
