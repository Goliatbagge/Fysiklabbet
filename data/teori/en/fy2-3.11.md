# Transformer

An electrical component that changes (transforms) [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] is called a
**transformer**. It is what sits in the "lump" on, for example, the
charger for your computer or mobile phone.

::: demo "Demonstration: Transformer"
We connect a 300-turn coil to a 1500-turn coil with an iron core
running through both. We apply a voltage across the first coil and see
that the voltage across the second coil becomes almost 5 times as large.
:::

From the wall socket we get an alternating voltage of 230 V. If we build a computer
that is to run on, for example, a voltage of 20 V, we need to **step down**
the voltage.

Inside the transformer there are two coils. The coil connected to the input
voltage is called the **primary coil**, and the coil that induces the new
voltage and is connected to the device to be powered is called the
**secondary coil**. They are linked by an iron core to increase
the magnetic flux density.

The primary coil is the one connected to the wall socket, which then induces a certain
voltage $e_1$. The alternating current through the primary coil generates a varying
[[magnetic field|Region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where other magnetic objects experience a force.]] through the iron core. This means that a [[magnetic flux|The amount of magnetic field passing through a surface: *Φ* = *B* · *A* · cos *α*. SI unit weber (Wb).]] *Φ*
will vary through the secondary coil, which thereby induces a new voltage
$e_2$. So in this way we can adjust an output voltage!

::: formel "Voltage and current in a transformer"
$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{U_1}{U_2}
$$

$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{I_2}{I_1}
$$

where

- $N_1$, $N_2$ = number of turns in the primary and secondary coil respectively
- $U_1$, $U_2$ = voltage in the primary and secondary coil respectively (V)
- $I_1$, $I_2$ = current in the primary and secondary coil respectively (A)

::: härledning "Derivation: Voltage in a transformer"
We already know that the induced voltage in a coil, when a
magnetic flux varies, is given by $e = -N \cdot \dfrac{d\Phi}{dt}$. The
induced voltage in the primary coil $e_1$ and the secondary coil $e_2$
can then be written

$$
e_1 = -N_1 \cdot \frac{d\Phi}{dt} \quad \text{and} \quad e_2 = -N_2 \cdot \frac{d\Phi}{dt}
$$

We set up the ratio between the induced voltages of the coils.
The minus signs and the flux change $\dfrac{d\Phi}{dt}$, which is the same in
both coils, cancel.

$$
\frac{e_1}{e_2} = \frac{-N_1 \cdot \frac{d\Phi}{dt}}{-N_2 \cdot \frac{d\Phi}{dt}} = \frac{N_1}{N_2}
$$

Since the internal [[resistance|Opposition to electric current. By Ohm's law: *U* = *R* · *I*. SI unit ohm (Ω).]] of the transformer is small, the
RMS values satisfy $U_1 \approx e_1$ and $U_2 \approx e_2$, so

$$
\frac{U_1}{U_2} = \frac{N_1}{N_2} \quad \text{QED}
$$
:::

::: härledning "Derivation: Current in a transformer"
[[The principle of conservation of energy|Energy can be neither created nor destroyed, only converted between different forms.]] applies. If we ignore energy losses, this means
that the [[power|Work or energy per unit time: *P* = *W* / *t*. SI unit watt (W).]] (the energy converted each second) in the primary coil $P_1$ must
be the same as the power in the secondary coil $P_2$. This gives

$$
P_1 = P_2
$$

We already know $P = U \cdot I$, which substituted above gives

$$
U_1 \cdot I_1 = U_2 \cdot I_2
$$

We solve for the ratio $\dfrac{U_1}{U_2}$ and get

$$
\frac{U_1}{U_2} = \frac{I_2}{I_1}
$$

From the derivation of the voltage in a transformer we have
$\dfrac{U_1}{U_2} = \dfrac{N_1}{N_2}$. Putting this into the equation above gives

$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{I_2}{I_1} \quad \text{QED}
$$
:::
:::

The power *P* through the primary and secondary coil is, if we ignore
energy losses, the same, so we cannot "create" energy with
transformers. Since the power $P = U \cdot I$, the current
decreases by the same factor as the voltage increases. So if we step up
the voltage so that it becomes 10 times as large, the current at the same time becomes
a tenth as large.

::: tips "High voltage vs. low voltage"
A **high voltage with low current** is good from an energy point of view, since
the energy losses in the power lines are smaller when the electricity is transported, but
bad from a safety point of view, as the risk of unwanted discharges increases.

A **low voltage with high current** is instead good from a safety point of view,
but bad from an energy point of view.
:::

::: demo "Demonstration: Jacob's ladder"
We connect two vertical electrodes ("metal rods") to a transformer
that gives a high voltage (dangerous!). An electric arc is struck at the bottom, where
the rods are closest together. Since the warm air rises, the
electric spark is forced upwards along the rods. It seems to climb step by
step until it reaches the top, where the gap becomes too large. Then it breaks
with a final snap, only to be born again at the bottom a microsecond later.

**Explanation:** When the voltage between the electrodes becomes high enough,
the air stops being an insulator and instead becomes a conductor, as
the atoms are ionised and form a **plasma**.

The arc is extremely hot (several thousand degrees). This heat warms the
surrounding air, which lowers its [[density|Mass per unit volume: *ρ* = *m* / *V*. SI unit kg/m³. Decides whether an object floats in a medium.]]. According to the principle of
convection, the warm air rises. Since the plasma in the arc is
lighter than the surrounding cool air, it is carried along by the rising
air current and travels upwards along the electrodes.

As the arc rises, it is forced to stretch out, since
the distance between the V-shaped electrodes increases. The longer the arc becomes,
the higher the voltage needed to maintain the current through the
ionised plasma. In the end the voltage of the transformer is not enough
to overcome the increased resistance and the heat losses. The arc
breaks (goes out).

When the upper arc goes out, the load it placed on the
power source disappears. The voltage across the electrodes then immediately rises to its
maximum value again. Since the air at the base is still the shortest
distance (and may still be slightly preheated), a new
breakdown occurs there, and the process starts over.
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the relationship for the voltage in a transformer and solve for
the number of turns in the primary coil $N_1$.

$$
\frac{U_1}{U_2} = \frac{N_1}{N_2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad N_1 = N_2 \cdot \frac{U_1}{U_2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
U_1 = 230\ \mathrm{V} \\
U_2 = 20\ \mathrm{V}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
N_1 = N_2 \cdot \frac{230}{20} = N_2 \cdot 11.5
$$

This means that the number of turns in the primary coil $N_1$ must be 11.5 times as many
as the number of turns in the secondary coil $N_2$.

We choose, for example, $N_2 = 100$. Then

$$
N_1 = 11.5 \cdot 100 = 1\ 150
$$

**Answer:** For example 1 150 turns in the primary coil and 100 turns in the
secondary coil.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Transformer"
- A component that **changes voltage**. Sits in the lump on, for example,
  a phone charger.
- The **primary coil** is connected to the input voltage, the
  **secondary coil** to the device.
- An **iron core** links them and increases the magnetic flux density.
:::

::: sampunkt "How it works"
- The alternating current in the primary coil gives a **varying** magnetic field through
  the iron core.
- The varying flux induces a new voltage in the secondary coil.
- **Only alternating voltage works.** With direct voltage the flux does not change and
  nothing is induced.
:::

::: sampunkt "The formulas"
- $\dfrac{N_1}{N_2} = \dfrac{U_1}{U_2}$
- $\dfrac{N_1}{N_2} = \dfrac{I_2}{I_1}$
- Note that the current ratio is **inverted**. Voltage up means current
  down.
:::

::: sampunkt "Up or down"
- More turns on the secondary side **steps up** the voltage.
- Fewer turns **steps it down**. From
  $230\ \mathrm{V}$ in the socket to $20\ \mathrm{V}$ in the computer.
:::

::: sampunkt "Power is conserved"
- In an ideal transformer $P_1 = P_2$, that is,
  $U_1 I_1 = U_2 I_2$.
- So the current must decrease when the voltage increases. It is
  the principle of conservation of energy that lies behind the inverted current ratio.
:::
:::
