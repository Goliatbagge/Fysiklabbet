# Inclined planes

::: härledning "Remember: Components"
In the same way that several [[forces|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] can be replaced by a single force
(the resultant force), we can split one force into several forces. Several forces
that replace a single force are called **components**.

When we split a force into components, the components **replace** the original
force vector, which is then crossed out. We can calculate how large the components are using
right-angled triangles and trigonometry.

$$
\cos \alpha = \frac{F_x}{F} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_x = F \cdot \cos \alpha
$$

$$
\sin \alpha = \frac{F_y}{F} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_y = F \cdot \sin \alpha
$$
:::

A plane that is tilted relative to the horizontal plane is called
an **inclined plane**. When something is on an inclined plane it helps
to split the [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and points straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] into a component **down the plane** and a component
**perpendicular to the plane**. The angle between the gravitational force $F_G$ and
the component of the gravitational force perpendicular to the plane *F*₂ is, because of similarity,
equal to the angle of inclination α of the plane.

From the figure we can also see that the normal force $F_N$ is as large as
the component of the gravitational force perpendicular to the plane *F*₂. If the object is **at rest**
or sliding at **[[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]]**, the frictional force $F_f$ will be
as large as the component of the gravitational force down the plane *F*₁, since the resultant force
is equal to 0.

::: formel "Inclined planes"
$$
F_1 = m \cdot g \cdot \sin \alpha
$$

$$
F_2 = m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha
$$

where

- *F*₁ = component of the gravitational force down the plane (N)
- *F*₂ = component of the gravitational force perpendicular to the plane (N)
- *m* = mass of the object (kg)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg) (9.82 N/kg in Sweden)
- *α* = angle of the plane to the horizontal plane

We call the angle of inclination of the plane *v* and the angle between the gravitational force and
the component of the gravitational force perpendicular to the plane *x*. We want to show that these
are always equal, that is *x* = *v*. Using the fact that the angles in a triangle add up to 180°,
we can find the angle between the plane and the gravitational force:
180° − 90° − *v* = 90° − *v*.

This gives

$$
x + (90^{\circ} - v) = 90^{\circ}
\qquad (\text{together they form a right angle})
$$

$$
x + 90^{\circ} - v = 90^{\circ}
$$

We subtract 90° from both sides and solve for *x*.

$$
x - v = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x = v \qquad \text{Q.E.D.}
$$
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We sketch a figure, draw all the forces and split the gravitational force into
two components: *F*₁ down the plane and *F*₂ perpendicular to the plane.

The normal force $F_N$ is as large as *F*₂, which gives

$$
F_N = F_2 = m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha
= 0.30\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \cdot \cos 34^{\circ}
= 2.442\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \approx 2.4\ \mathrm{N}
$$

**Answer:** 2.4 N

**b)** Since the box slides at constant velocity down the plane, the
frictional force $F_f$ must be as large as the force down the plane *F*₁.

$$
F_f = F_1 = m \cdot g \cdot \sin \alpha
= 0.30\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \cdot \sin 34^{\circ}
= 1.647\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \approx 1.6\ \mathrm{N}
$$

**Answer:** 1.6 N
:::

::: textlosning
We sketch a figure and draw all the forces. Since the box accelerates, the
frictional force $F_f$ must be smaller than the force down the plane *F*₁.

The acceleration of the box depends on the resultant force. We solve for
the acceleration from [[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]].

$$
F_R = m \cdot a \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a = \frac{F_R}{m}
$$

We need to determine the resultant force $F_R$. From the figure we get

$$
F_R = F_1 - F_f
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_1 = m \cdot g \cdot \sin \alpha
   = 6.0\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \cdot \sin 31^{\circ}
   = 30.346\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \\
F_f = \mu \cdot F_N = \mu \cdot F_2 = \mu \cdot m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha
   = 0.35 \cdot 6.0 \cdot 9.82 \cdot \cos 31^{\circ}
   = 17.676\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_R = 30.346\ldots - 17.676\ldots = 12.669\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Substituting gives

$$
a = \frac{12.669\ldots}{6.0} = 2.111\ldots\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \approx 2.1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

**Answer:** 2.1 m/s²

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Splitting into components"
- A force can be **replaced** by two components in chosen directions. The
  original vector is then crossed out.
- $F_x = F \cdot \cos \alpha$ and $F_y = F \cdot \sin \alpha$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The gravitational force on an inclined plane"
- Split $F_G$ into a component **down** the plane and one **perpendicular**
  to the plane.
- $F_1 = m \cdot g \cdot \sin \alpha$ (down the plane)
- $F_2 = m \cdot g \cdot \cos \alpha$ (perpendicular to the plane)
- $\alpha$ is the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizontal.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why the angle is the same"
- The angle between $F_G$ and the perpendicular component is **equal to
  the angle of inclination of the plane**.
- The angle between the plane and the gravitational force is $90^\circ - v$, and
  together with $x$ it forms a right angle. So $x = v$.
:::

::: sampunkt "The forces on the plane"
- The **normal force** is always as large as the perpendicular component:
  $F_N = F_2$.
- **At rest or at constant velocity** the friction is as large as
  the component down the plane: $F_f = F_1$.
- If $F_f < F_1$ the object accelerates down the plane, with
  $F_R = F_1 - F_f$.
:::
