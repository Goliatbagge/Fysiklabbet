# Activity and half-life

The number of decays per second in a radioactive material is called its
**activity**, is denoted *A* and has the unit Bq (becquerel), which thus
stands for "decays per second".

::: demo "Activity"
We investigate the activity of three radioactive samples and some other
objects (for example luminous watches and a mobile phone) with a Geiger counter.
Each decay is registered with a beep and on a counter. We notice that
the activity of some samples and objects is higher than that of others. We
also note that the activity decreases with the distance to the sample.
:::

The activity is proportional to the number of atomic nuclei *N*. The more
atomic nuclei, the greater the activity. The constant of proportionality is called
the **decay constant**, is denoted *λ* (small lambda) and is different for
different substances. The higher the value of *λ*, the faster a substance decays.

::: formel "Activity"
$$
A = \lambda \cdot N
$$

where

- *A* = activity (Bq)
- *λ* = decay constant (1/s or s⁻¹)
- *N* = number of atomic nuclei
:::

The time it takes for half of the atomic nuclei to decay is called the
**[[half-life|The time it takes for half of the atomic nuclei in a radioactive sample to decay.]]**.

| Isotope | Half-life |
| --- | --- |
| Radon-222 | 3.8 days |
| Caesium-137 | 30 years |
| Carbon-14 | 5 730 years |
| Uranium-238 | 4.5 billion years |

The decay constant depends directly on the half-life of the substance.

::: formel "The decay constant"
$$
\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}
$$

where

- *λ* = the decay constant (1/s)
- $T_{1/2}$ = half-life (s)

::: härledning "Derivation: The decay constant (extension)"
We know that a radioactive sample decays with a certain half-life
*T* and therefore decreases exponentially. A general exponential function can be
written $y = C \cdot a^x$ or $y = C \cdot e^{kx}$ (1).

In applications about changes over time, *y* stands for the value after the time
*x* with the starting value *C*. In this case we let the value *y* stand for an amount
*N*. The starting value *C* is the initial amount, which we call $N_0$. We call
the time *t*. The constant *k* in the exponent corresponds to the decay constant
*λ*. Since we know that it is an exponentially decreasing function, the
exponent must be negative. Substituting gives

$$
N = N_0 \cdot e^{-\lambda t}
$$

At the time *t = T* (one half-life), half of the sample remains, that is
$N_0/2$. Substituting gives

$$
\frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 \cdot e^{-\lambda T}
$$

Divide both sides by $N_0$.

$$
\frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T}
$$

Take the logarithm of both sides, use the laws of logarithms and solve for *λ*:

$$
\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\lambda T
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
-\ln 2 = -\lambda T
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T} = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} \qquad \text{q.e.d.}
$$
:::
:::

In a radioactive material the activity decreases with time, since the amount of
it decreases through decay.

::: formel "The law of activity"
$$
A = A_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

where

- *A* = activity after the time *t* (Bq)
- $A_0$ = activity at *t* = 0 (Bq)
- *t* = time (any unit of time, but the same unit as the half-life $T_{1/2}$)
- $T_{1/2}$ = half-life
:::

In a radioactive material the number of nuclei also decreases with time, in exactly
the same way as the activity.

::: formel "The law of radioactive decay"
$$
N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

where

- *N* = the number of nuclei after the time *t*
- $N_0$ = the number of nuclei at *t* = 0
- *t* = time (any unit of time, but the same unit as the half-life $T_{1/2}$)
- $T_{1/2}$ = half-life
:::

## Carbon-14 dating

The radioactive [[isotope|Atoms of the same element (same *Z*) but with different numbers of neutrons (different *A*).]] carbon-14 is formed in the atmosphere when cosmic radiation
releases neutrons, which in turn transform nitrogen in the air into carbon-14. Carbon-14
reacts with oxygen in the air and forms carbon dioxide, which is taken up by plants and
animals when they breathe or eat plants. All living things therefore contain
a fairly [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] amount of carbon-14. When they die, the uptake of
carbon-14 stops and the level decreases.

Using the level of carbon-14 to date material that was once
alive, for example wood or skeletons, is called **carbon-14 dating**. After 10
halvings only 1 per mille remains ($0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$), so
carbon-14 dating is suitable for dates up to 5 730 · 10 ≈ 50 000 years.

::: exempel "Example 1: Activity of Cs-137 in 10 years"
**A sample of Cs-137 has an activity of 0.25 MBq. What is the activity
in 10 years?**

::: handskrift
typ: aktivitet
:::

::: textlosning
$$
A = A_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
A_0 = 0.25\ \mathrm{MBq} = 0.25 \cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{Bq} \\
t = 10\ \mathrm{years} \\
T_{1/2} = 30.08\ \mathrm{years}\ (\text{table})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
A = 0.25 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 0.5^{10/30.08}
= 198\,547.099\ldots\ \mathrm{Bq}
\approx 200\,000\ \mathrm{Bq} = 0.20\ \mathrm{MBq}
$$

**Answer:** 0.20 MBq
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Smoke detector with Am-241"
**A common type of smoke detector contains Am-241. Determine what proportion
of the sample remains after 100 years.**

::: handskrift
typ: brandvarnare
:::

::: textlosning
$$
N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
t = 100\ \mathrm{years} \\
T_{1/2} = 432.6\ \mathrm{years}\ (\text{table})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{100 / 432.6}
= N_0 \cdot 0.8519\ldots
\approx N_0 \cdot 0.85
$$

**Answer:** About 85 % remains.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: Shipwreck (carbon-14 dating)"
**A diver finds a shipwreck whose level of carbon-14 is 83.2 % of the
normal level. How old is the wreck?**

::: handskrift
typ: skeppsvrak
:::

::: textlosning
$$
N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{t / T_{1/2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
N = 0.832 \cdot N_0 \\
T_{1/2} = 5\,730\ \mathrm{years}\ (\text{table})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
0.832 \cdot N_0 = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{t/5730}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
0.832 = 0.5^{t/5730}
$$

Take the logarithm of both sides (lg = log₁₀) and apply the laws of logarithms:

$$
\lg 0.832 = \frac{t}{5\,730} \cdot \lg 0.5
$$

We solve for *t* and get

$$
t = \frac{5\,730 \cdot \lg 0.832}{\lg 0.5} = 1\,520.424\ldots\ \mathrm{years}
\approx 1\,520\ \mathrm{years}
$$

**Answer:** 1 520 years
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Activity"
- The number of **decays per second**. Denoted $A$ with the unit Bq
  (becquerel).
- $A = \lambda \cdot N$, that is proportional to the number of atomic nuclei.
- The activity decreases with the distance to the sample.
:::

::: sampunkt "The decay constant"
- $\lambda$ is different for different substances. A higher $\lambda$ means faster
  decay.
- $\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}$, the unit is 1/s.
:::

::: sampunkt "Half-life"
- The time it takes for **half** of the atomic nuclei to decay.
- Radon-222: 3.8 days. Caesium-137: 30 years. Carbon-14: 5 730 years. Uranium-238: 4.5
  billion years.
:::

::: sampunkt "The laws of activity and decay"
- $A = A_0 \cdot 0.5^{\,t/T_{1/2}}$
- $N = N_0 \cdot 0.5^{\,t/T_{1/2}}$
- Activity and number of nuclei decrease in **exactly the same way**.
- $t$ and $T_{1/2}$ can have any unit of time, as long as it is
  the **same**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Carbon-14 dating"
- Cosmic radiation forms carbon-14 in the atmosphere. It is taken up by all
  living things, which therefore have a constant level.
- When the organism **dies**, the uptake stops and the level starts to decrease.
- After ten halvings only one per mille remains, so the method works for up to
  about **50 000 years**.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to solve"
- Activity after a given time: substitute directly into the law of activity.
- **Age** from a known proportion: solve for $t$ by taking logarithms.
- The proportion that remains is $0.5^{\,t/T_{1/2}}$, regardless of how much
  you had from the start.
:::
:::
