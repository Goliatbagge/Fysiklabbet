# Power

::: demo "Introduction: Power"
If you walk up a flight of stairs you do a certain amount of [[work|Energy transferred when a force acts along a distance: *W* = *F* · *s* · cos *α*. SI unit joule (J).]], since you increase
your [[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]]. If you run up the stairs the same work is done,
since you increase your potential energy by the same amount in both cases. However, the
work is done faster when you run (and that is also why it feels harder).
:::

The energy converted (or the work done) per unit time is called
**[[power|Work or energy per unit time: *P* = *W* / *t*. SI unit watt (W).]]**, written *P*, and its [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] is W
(watt). The faster an energy conversion takes place, the greater the power.

::: formel "Power"
$$
P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t} \qquad\text{or}\qquad P = \frac{W}{\Delta t}
$$

where

- *P* = power (W or J/s)
- Δ*E* = energy converted (J or Ws)
- Δ*t* = the time for the energy conversion (s)
- *W* = work (Nm or J)
:::

Notice that we can express the units W and J with alternative units.

::: formel "Alternative units for energy and power"
$$
1\ \mathrm{W} = 1\ \mathrm{J/s}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
1\ \mathrm{J} = 1\ \mathrm{Ws}
$$
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Emilia on the stairs"
**Emilia, who has a mass of 60 kg, climbs a 4.0-metre-high flight of stairs. What
power does she develop when she a) walks up the stairs in 10 seconds,
b) runs up the stairs in 2.5 seconds?**

::: handskrift
typ: trappan
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We set up the formula for power.

$$
P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}
$$

The energy conversion Δ*E* is the same thing as the increase in potential energy.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\Delta E = m \cdot g \cdot \Delta h
   = 60\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} \cdot 4.0\ \mathrm{m}
   = 2\,356.8\ \mathrm{J} \\
\Delta t = 10\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
P = \frac{2\,356.8}{10} = 235.68\ \mathrm{W} \approx 240\ \mathrm{W} = 0.24\ \mathrm{kW}
$$

**Answer:** 0.24 kW

**b)** The energy conversion Δ*E* is the same as in part a, that is
2 356.8 J.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\Delta E = 2\,356.8\ \mathrm{J} \\
\Delta t = 2.5\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
P = \frac{2\,356.8}{2.5} = 942.72\ \mathrm{W} \approx 940\ \mathrm{W} = 0.94\ \mathrm{kW}
$$

**Answer:** 0.94 kW (four times as much power since it took a
quarter of the time)
:::
:::

## Kilowatt-hours

Energy consumption is often measured in kWh (kilowatt-hours) and the price of energy in kr/kWh
(Swedish kronor per kilowatt-hour). We find out how many joules 1 kWh
corresponds to using unit analysis. Remember that 1 Ws = 1 J according to the definition of
power, so we start by converting kWh to Ws and then go on to J.

$$
[1\ \mathrm{kWh}] = [1\,000\ \mathrm{W}] \cdot [1\ \mathrm{h}]
= [1\,000\ \mathrm{W}] \cdot [3\,600\ \mathrm{s}]
= [3\,600\,000\ \mathrm{Ws}]
= [3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{J}]
$$

::: formel "Converting between kilowatt-hours and joules"
$$
1\ \mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{J}
$$

$$
1\ \mathrm{J} = \frac{1}{3.6 \cdot 10^{6}}\ \mathrm{kWh}
$$
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Forgotten light bulb"
**You forget to switch off an 8.5 W light bulb.**

**a) How much energy does it convert if it is on continuously for two days?**
**b) What does the energy consumption cost if the price is 1.50 kr/kWh?**

::: handskrift
typ: glodlampa
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We set up the formula for power and solve for the energy converted
Δ*E*.

$$
P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \Delta E = P \cdot \Delta t
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
P = 8.5\ \mathrm{W} \\
\Delta t = 2.0\ \mathrm{d} = 48\ \mathrm{h} = 48 \cdot 3\,600\ \mathrm{s} = 172\,800\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\Delta E = 8.5 \cdot 172\,800 = 1\,468\,800\ \mathrm{J}
\approx 1\,500\,000\ \mathrm{J} = 1.5\ \mathrm{MJ}
$$

**Answer:** 1.5 MJ

**b)** We convert the energy consumption to kWh. The conversion factor between kWh
and J is 3.6 · 10⁶. When we convert from J to kWh we *divide* by
the conversion factor.

$$
1\,468\,800\ \mathrm{J} = \frac{1\,468\,800}{3.6 \cdot 10^{6}}\ \mathrm{kWh}
= 0.408\ \mathrm{kWh}
$$

Since 1 kWh costs 1.50 kr, the total cost is

$$
1.50\ \mathrm{kr/kWh} \cdot 0.408\ \mathrm{kWh} = 0.612\ \mathrm{kr} \approx 0.61\ \mathrm{kr}
$$

**Answer:** 0.61 kr or 61 öre
:::
:::

::: kuriosa "Power in electrical appliances"
To save energy and money it is a good idea to avoid using appliances
with high power, especially when the electricity price is high (if you have a variable electricity price).
In general, appliances that produce a lot of [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]] have a high
energy consumption.

| Appliance | Power (approximately) |

| Microwave oven | 500–1 000 W |
| Coffee maker | 800 W |
| Water heater | 1 000–6 000 W |
| Vacuum cleaner | 1 000–2 000 W |

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Power"
- **Power** ($P$) is the energy converted or the work done **per
  unit time**. The SI unit is W (watt).
- $P = \dfrac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$ or
  $P = \dfrac{W}{\Delta t}$
- The faster the conversion takes place, the greater the power.
:::

::: sampunkt "Same work, different power"
- Walking or running up the same stairs gives **the same work**, since
  the potential energy increases by the same amount.
- If you run in a quarter of the time, the power is **four times** as
  great. That is why it feels harder.
:::

::: sampunkt "The units"
- $1\ \mathrm{W} = 1\ \mathrm{J/s}$
- $1\ \mathrm{J} = 1\ \mathrm{Ws}$
:::

::: sampunkt "Kilowatt-hours"
- $1\ \mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{J}$, since
  $1\,000\ \mathrm{W}$ times $3\,600\ \mathrm{s}$.
- From J to kWh: **divide** by $3.6 \cdot 10^{6}$.
- From kWh to J: **multiply**.
- Cost: the energy in kWh times the price in kr/kWh.
:::

::: sampunkt "How to solve it"
- Always convert the time to seconds before substituting.
- If you are looking for the energy: $\Delta E = P \cdot \Delta t$.
- If you are looking for the time: $\Delta t = \dfrac{\Delta E}{P}$.
:::
:::
