# Friction

The [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] that tends to oppose the motion between two surfaces in contact
with each other is called **[[friction|The force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact.]]** or the **frictional force**, written $F_f$, and
like all forces it has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] N (newton).

Examples of frictional forces are when

- you rub your hands together (you feel the heat from the friction)
- a car rolls or brakes (friction between tyre and road and between brake pad
  and brake discs)
- you push something across a floor
- you walk (friction between shoe and floor is what moves us forward)

When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards ($F_1$) and the ground pushes you forwards with an equal and opposite frictional force ($F_f$): Newton's third law in practice.
:::

The size of the friction depends on the **[[normal force|The force a surface exerts perpendicular to an object resting on it.]]** (the force between the surfaces),
the material of the surfaces and what they look like (unevenness, even at the microscopic level,
matters). The fraction of the normal force that makes up the frictional force is called the
**coefficient of friction**, written *μ* (the small Greek letter "mu"). It has no unit
and is given in decimal form or as a percentage. *μ* = 0 (0 %) means no
friction and *μ* = 1 (100 %) means that the frictional force is as large as the
normal force.

::: demo "Pulling a block"
We pull a block across a table with a spring balance. We call the pulling force
$F_\text{drag}$ and the frictional force $F_f$.

1. $F_\text{drag} = 0\ \mathrm{N}$ (block at rest).
2. We pull on the spring balance with a small force, but the block is still at
   rest. An opposing force is exactly as large. This is the frictional force!
   **At rest:** $F_f = F_\text{drag}$.
3. $F_\text{drag}$ is increased so that the block just starts to slide at [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]].
   In this situation the frictional force is at its maximum. **At constant velocity:**
   $F_f = F_\text{drag}$.
4. $F_\text{drag}$ is increased further so that the block starts to accelerate with constant
   [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]]. In this situation the [[resultant force|The sum of all forces acting on an object (vector sum). Gives the object its acceleration.]] points to the right.
   **During acceleration:** $F_f < F_\text{drag}$.

::: formel "Frictional force"
$$
F_f = \mu \cdot F_N
$$

where

- $F_f$ = frictional force (N)
- *μ* = coefficient of friction (no unit)
- $F_N$ = normal force (N)
:::

| Material | Coefficient of friction (approximately) |
| --- | --- |
| wood on wood | 0.25 (25 %) |
| ice on ice | 0.05 (5 %) |
| steel on ice | 0.01 (1 %) (ice skates!) |
| Velcro on Velcro | 1.3 (130 %) (hooks interlock) |

::: textlosning
We draw the force diagram. In the limiting case the pushing force is as large as the
frictional force, so these [[vectors|A quantity with both magnitude and direction, for example velocity, force or acceleration. Differs from a scalar, which only has magnitude.]] should be equally long. Since the car is at
rest, it is also true that the normal forces $F_{N1}$ and $F_{N2}$ upwards together are
as large as the [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and points straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] $F_G$ downwards.

$$
F_\text{putt} = F_f = \mu \cdot F_N
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\mu = 0.70 \\
F_N = F_{N1} + F_{N2} = F_G = m \cdot g
     = 1\,200\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} = 11\,784\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_\text{putt} = 0.70 \cdot 11\,784 = 8\,248.8\ \mathrm{N}
\approx 8\,200\ \mathrm{N} = 8.2\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 8.2 kN
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We cannot calculate the frictional force with the formula for frictional force
since we do not know the coefficient of friction. Instead we set up an equation for the
forces and solve for the frictional force $F_f$.

$$
F_R = F_\text{drag} - F_f \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_f = F_\text{drag} - F_R
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_\text{drag} = 325\ \mathrm{N} \\
F_R = m \cdot a = 50\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 4.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 200\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_f = 325 - 200 = 125\ \mathrm{N} \approx 130\ \mathrm{N} = 0.13\ \mathrm{kN}
$$

**Answer:** 0.13 kN or 130 N

**b)** We set up the formula for friction and solve for the coefficient of friction.

$$
F_f = \mu \cdot F_N \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \mu = \frac{F_f}{F_N}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_f = 125\ \mathrm{N} \quad (\text{from the previous part}) \\
F_N = F_G = m \cdot g = 50\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} = 491\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\mu = \frac{125}{491} = 0.254\ldots \approx 0.25
$$

**Answer:** 0.25
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Friction"
- The **frictional force** $F_f$ opposes motion between two surfaces in
  contact. The SI unit is N.
- It depends on the **normal force** between the surfaces and on the material and
  unevenness of the surfaces, right down to the microscopic level.
- Friction is needed to walk: your foot pushes the ground backwards and the ground
  pushes you forwards.
:::

::: sampunkt "The coefficient of friction"
- $F_f = \mu \cdot F_N$
- The **coefficient of friction** $\mu$ is the fraction of the normal force that becomes
  friction. It has no unit.
- $\mu = 0$ means no friction, $\mu = 1$ that the friction is as large
  as the normal force.
- Steel on ice: 0.01. Wood on wood: 0.25. Velcro: 1.3.
:::

::: sampunkt "Three situations when you pull a block"
- **At rest**: $F_f = F_\text{drag}$. The friction grows exactly as much
  as you pull.
- **Constant velocity**: $F_f = F_\text{drag}$, and the friction is now
  at its maximum.
- **Acceleration**: $F_f < F_\text{drag}$, and the resultant drives
  the block forwards.
:::

::: sampunkt "Typical tasks"
- Setting something in motion: the force must be at least
  $F_f = \mu \cdot F_N$, where $F_N = m \cdot g$ on a horizontal surface.
- Known acceleration but unknown $\mu$: first find
  $F_f = F_\text{drag} - F_R$ with $F_R = m \cdot a$, and then
  $\mu = \dfrac{F_f}{F_N}$.
:::
:::
