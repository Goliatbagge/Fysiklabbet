# Plane simple pendulum

A body that swings in the vertical plane along a circular arc is called a **plane
[[pendulum|An object swinging back and forth about an equilibrium position. Simple pendulum: *T* = 2*π* · √(*L* / *g*).]]**. It can, for example, be a ball swinging back and forth
on a string. If we neglect (disregard) the mass of the string, this idealised
pendulum is called a **plane simple pendulum** (plane mathematical pendulum).

::: formel "Period of a simple pendulum at small amplitudes"
$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}
$$

where

- *T* = [[period|The time for one complete oscillation or one complete wave: *T* = 1 / *f*.]] (s)
- *l* = length of the pendulum (m)
- *g* = [[acceleration due to gravity|The acceleration a freely falling object gets from the gravitational force, about 9.82 m/s² at the Earth's surface. Written *g*.]] (m/s²) (9.82 m/s² in Sweden)

The circular arc from the vertical to the position of the pendulum bob is written *y*. The
[[forces|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] acting on the pendulum bob are the [[tension|The force acting along a taut rope, line or spring.]] $F_S$ from the string and
the weight *mg*. [[Air resistance|The force from the air that slows down objects moving through it. Depends on the object's shape, size and velocity.]] is neglected.

We replace the gravitational force with two components: one in the direction of the string and
one along the tangent to the circular arc. The forces in the direction of the string are
perpendicular to the direction of motion and therefore do not affect the motion.
The motion along the path is determined only by the tangential component $F_1$.

From the two similar triangles in the figure we get

$$
\frac{F_1}{mg} = \frac{d}{l} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_1 = mg \cdot \frac{d}{l} \qquad (1)
$$

If the [[amplitude|The maximum displacement of a wave from the equilibrium position.]] of the oscillation is much smaller than the length of the pendulum *l*, the
angle α becomes small, and **the distance *d* becomes almost as long as the arc *y***.
Check at α = 10°:

$$
d = l \cdot \sin 10^{\circ} \approx 0.174 \cdot l \qquad y = \frac{10^{\circ}}{360^{\circ}} \cdot 2\pi \cdot l \approx 0.175 \cdot l
$$

We see that *d* and *y* become almost identical. At small amplitudes we can
therefore replace *d* with *y* in (1):

$$
F_1 = mg \cdot \frac{y}{l} = \frac{mg}{l} \cdot y
$$

Under these conditions the restoring force $F_1$ is **proportional
to *y***, just as in a spring oscillation. The constant of proportionality
$\dfrac{mg}{l}$ plays the same role as the spring constant *k*.

In the expression $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$ for spring oscillations we replace
*k* with $\dfrac{mg}{l}$ and get

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m \cdot l}{m \cdot g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}
$$

QED.
:::
:::

The length of the pendulum is counted from the point of suspension to the swinging body's
**centre of gravity**. So at a given place the period is determined only by
the length of the pendulum: it is **independent** of the mass of what is swinging.

::: tips "Limitations of the formula"
The formula only applies if:

1. **The mass of the string** is negligible compared with the mass of the body. (Otherwise
   you have to take the moment of inertia into account.)
2. **The angle of deflection is small.** At 23° the error is about 1 %, at 45° about 4 %,
   and then it grows rapidly. The pendulum motion then also stops being
   harmonic.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: The seconds pendulum"
**How long is a "seconds pendulum", a pendulum where the time to go from one
turning point to the other is 1.00 second?**

::: handskrift
typ: sekundpendel
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for the period, square both sides and solve for
the length of the pendulum *l*.

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad T^{2} = 4\pi^{2} \cdot \frac{l}{g} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad l = \frac{T^{2} \cdot g}{4\pi^{2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
T = 2.00\ \mathrm{s} \\
g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
\end{array} \right]
$$

(The time 1.00 s from one turning point to the other corresponds to **half** a
period, so *T* = 2.00 s.)

$$
l = \frac{2.00^{2} \cdot 9.82}{4\pi^{2}} = 0.9949\ldots\ \mathrm{m} \approx 0.995\ \mathrm{m} = 99.5\ \mathrm{cm}
$$

**Answer:** 99.5 cm
:::
:::

::: kuriosa "The definition of 1 metre"
In the 16th century the length of a seconds pendulum was used as
the definition of 1 metre, but it was discovered that the same length did not apply at
the equator. Why?

**Answer:** The acceleration due to gravity *g* is slightly lower at the equator because of
the flattening of the Earth, so the time for one swing becomes slightly longer there.
The pendulum therefore needs to be shorter to keep the same period.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Plane simple pendulum"
- A body that swings in the vertical plane along a circular arc.
- If the mass of the string is neglected, it is called a **simple pendulum**.
:::

::: sampunkt "The period"
- $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$
- **The mass does not matter**, only the length of the pendulum and the acceleration due to gravity.
- The length of the pendulum is measured from the point of suspension to the body's **centre of gravity**.
:::

::: sampunkt "The forces"
- The **tension** along the string and the **gravitational force** downwards.
- Split the gravitational force into a component along the string and one along
  the tangent.
- Only the **tangential** component drives the motion. The one along the string
  is perpendicular to the motion and does nothing.
:::

::: sampunkt "The small-angle approximation"
- At small amplitudes the distance $d$ is almost as long as the arc $y$. At
  $10^\circ$ the difference is less than one per cent.
- Then the restoring force becomes
  $F_1 = \dfrac{mg}{l} \cdot y$, that is, **proportional to $y$**,
  just as in a spring.
- $\dfrac{mg}{l}$ plays the same role as the spring constant $k$. Substitute into
  the spring formula, and $m$ cancels.
:::

::: sampunkt "Limitations of the formula"
- The mass of the string must be **negligible**.
- The angle of deflection must be **small**: at $23^\circ$ the error is about
  1 %, at $45^\circ$ about 4 %, and then it grows quickly.
- At large amplitudes the motion is no longer harmonic.
:::

::: sampunkt "The seconds pendulum"
- A pendulum that takes $1.00\ \mathrm{s}$ between the turning points. So the whole period is
  $2.00\ \mathrm{s}$.
- The length is about $99.5\ \mathrm{cm}$.
- It was once used as the definition of the metre, but it did not work:
  $g$ is lower at the equator, so the pendulum must be shorter there.
:::
:::
