# Summary: Systems of linear equations

This chapter was about solving systems of two linear equations
in two unknowns, both graphically and algebraically. You have seen how
the lines' *k*- and *m*-values decide whether a system of equations has one
solution, no solutions or infinitely many solutions, and you
have practised two algebraic methods: the substitution method and
the elimination method. You have also seen how systems of equations are used to
solve word problems with two unknown quantities. After this chapter you should be able to
choose a suitable method and solve a system of linear equations both
graphically and algebraically.

## Concepts to know

- **system of equations**: several equations that are to have a common solution;
  written together with a curly bracket.
- **graphical solution**: solving a system of equations by drawing the
  graphs of the equations and reading off the point of intersection.
- **point of intersection**: the point where the graphs meet; its *x*-coordinate is
  the solution for $x$ and the *y*-coordinate is the solution for $y$.
- **number of solutions**: decided by the lines' *k*- and *m*-values: different
  *k*-values give one solution, the same *k*-value but a different *m*-value gives
  no solutions, and the same *k*- and *m*-value gives infinitely many
  solutions.
- **parallel lines**: lines with the same *k*-value but a different
  *m*-value; they have no common point, so the system of equations then has no
  solution.
- **coinciding lines**: lines with the same *k*- and *m*-value; they
  lie on top of each other, so the system of equations then has infinitely many
  solutions.
- **the substitution method**: an algebraic method of solution where a variable
  is solved for from one equation and replaced (substituted) in the other.
- **the elimination method**: an algebraic method of solution where the equations
  are added side by side so that a variable is eliminated.
- **eliminate a variable**: make a variable's term disappear, for example
  by adding two equations where the term has opposite signs in front
  of it.
- **side-by-side addition**: adding two equations term by term,
  left-hand sides together and right-hand sides together.
- **coefficient**: the number in front of a variable in a term, for example 5 in
  the term $5x$.
- **labelling equations**: numbering the equations (1), (2),
  (3) … so that you can refer to them in the solution.
- **solution to a system of equations**: the pair of values $(x, y)$ that
  satisfies both equations at the same time; given with a curly bracket.

## Formulas

::: formel "The methods of the chapter"
**System of linear equations**

$$
\begin{cases}
y = k_1 x + m_1 \\
y = k_2 x + m_2
\end{cases}
$$

where $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the slopes of the lines (*k*-values) and $m_1$ and
$m_2$ are where the lines cross the *y*-axis (*m*-values).

**Number of solutions**

| The lines' *k*- and *m*-values | Number of solutions |
| --- | --- |
| Different *k*-values | One solution |
| Same *k*-value, different *m*-values | No solutions |
| Same *k*-value, same *m*-value | Infinitely many solutions |

**The substitution method**

1. Solve for a variable from one of the equations.
2. Replace (substitute) the variable with the expression in the equation you have not
   used.
3. Solve the new equation and find the value of the remaining
   variable.
4. Substitute the value into one of the original equations to get
   the value of the other variable.

**The elimination method**

1. Make sure that the same variable term appears with opposite signs in
   both equations. If needed, multiply one or both
   equations by a number (all terms in the equation must then be
   multiplied).
2. Add the equations side by side so that the variable is eliminated.
3. Solve the remaining equation.
4. Substitute the value into one of the original equations to get
   the value of the other variable.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- A system of equations is marked with a curly bracket, and the solution
  $(x, y)$ must satisfy **both** equations at the same time.
- A graphical solution gives the answer directly: the *x*-coordinate of the point of intersection is
  the solution for $x$, the *y*-coordinate the solution for $y$.
- The number of solutions is decided by the lines' *k*- and *m*-values alone.
  Without drawing anything, you can decide whether the system has one, no or
  infinitely many solutions.
- The substitution method works best when a variable already stands alone on
  one side, or is easy to solve for (for example with no coefficient or
  minus sign in front).
- The elimination method works best when the same variable term already appears in
  both equations with opposite signs, or can easily be made to do so by
  multiplication.
- If it is not enough to multiply **one** equation to get the same
  variable term with opposite signs, multiply **both** equations:
  by each other's coefficients in front of the variable to be eliminated,
  and with the opposite sign on one of them.
- If you multiply an equation by a number, **all** terms in the
  equation must be multiplied, not just the one you want to match.
- Label the equations (1), (2), (3) … so that you can easily refer
  to them in your solution, especially when you derive a new equation from
  one of the original ones.
- When you have found one variable: substitute the value into one of the
  **original** equations (or one derived earlier) to
  find the other.
- In problem solving: define your variables clearly before you
  set up the system of equations, and always finish by interpreting
  the answer in words, not just in numbers.
- It is a good idea to check your answer by substituting both values into
  **both** of the original equations. If both equalities hold,
  the answer is correct.

## Before the test

- Do you know what a system of equations is, and why the solution is written with a
  curly bracket?
- Can you solve a system of equations graphically by drawing the graphs
  and reading off the coordinates of the point of intersection?
- Do you know how the lines' *k*- and *m*-values decide whether the system has one
  solution, no solutions or infinitely many solutions?
- Can you solve a system of equations with the substitution method, even when
  no variable stands alone on one side from the start?
- Can you solve a system of equations with the elimination method, and decide when
  you need to multiply one or both equations before you add?
- Do you remember to multiply **all** terms in an equation when you
  scale it?
- Can you put labels (1), (2), (3) on the equations and refer to
  them in your solution?
- Can you substitute your solution into one of the original equations to
  find the other variable?
- Can you set up your own system of equations from a word problem with
  two unknowns, for example cows and hens or the sum and difference of two
  numbers?
- Do you finish problem-solving tasks by interpreting the answer in words, not
  just with numbers?
- Do you check your answer by substituting both values into both of the
  original equations?
