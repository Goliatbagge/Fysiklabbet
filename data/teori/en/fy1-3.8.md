# Slanting tension forces

A lamp, a sign or a box often hangs from **two strings** that slope in opposite directions. Each string pulls on the object with a [[tension|The force acting along a taut rope, line or spring.]] that is directed **along the string**. Neither of the tension forces points straight up, but together they still hold the object up.

The object hangs at rest. According to Newton's first law the resultant force is then zero: the tension forces $F_{S1}$ and $F_{S2}$ together cancel the [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and points straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] $F_G$. We call the angles *α* and *β*. They are the angles of the strings to the ceiling, that is to the horizontal.

## Split the tension forces into components

Since the tension forces slope, we split each of them into a **horizontal** and a **vertical** component, in the same way as the gravitational force was split up in the section on inclined planes. We draw the object as a point and draw a horizontal line through it. The line is parallel to the ceiling, so the string makes **the same angle** with it as with the ceiling.

The tension is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle. The horizontal component lies next to the angle (adjacent leg) and is found with cosine. The vertical component lies opposite the angle (opposite leg) and is found with sine.

Now we can set up a condition in each direction:

- **Horizontally (*x*-direction):** the object does not move sideways. So the horizontal components are **equal in size but opposite in direction**.
- **Vertically (*y*-direction):** the object does not fall. **Together** the vertical components carry the whole gravitational force.

::: formel "Equilibrium for an object hanging from two slanting strings"
$$
F_{S1} \cdot \cos \alpha = F_{S2} \cdot \cos \beta \qquad (x\text{-direction})
$$

$$
F_{S1} \cdot \sin \alpha + F_{S2} \cdot \sin \beta = F_G \qquad (y\text{-direction})
$$

where

- $F_{S1}$ = tension in string 1 (N)
- $F_{S2}$ = tension in string 2 (N)
- $F_G$ = gravitational force on the object (N), $F_G = m \cdot g$
- *α* = angle between string 1 and the ceiling (the horizontal)
- *β* = angle between string 2 and the ceiling (the horizontal)

::: härledning "Derivation: The symmetric case"
If the strings slope equally, $\alpha = \beta$. The condition in the *x*-direction then gives $F_{S1} = F_{S2}$, so both strings are equally taut. We call the tension $F_S$. The condition in the *y*-direction becomes

$$
F_S \cdot \sin \alpha + F_S \cdot \sin \alpha = F_G
$$

$$
2 \cdot F_S \cdot \sin \alpha = F_G
$$

We divide both sides by $2 \cdot \sin \alpha$.

$$
\frac{2 \cdot F_S \cdot \sin \alpha}{2 \cdot \sin \alpha} = \frac{F_G}{2 \cdot \sin \alpha}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
F_S = \frac{F_G}{2 \cdot \sin \alpha}
$$

So each string carries half the gravitational force with its vertical component.
:::
:::

::: tips "How to solve tasks with slanting tension forces"
1. Draw the gravitational force $F_G$ straight down and the tension forces along the strings.
2. Mark the angles of the strings to the horizontal.
3. Split each tension force into a horizontal component ($F_S \cdot \cos$) and a vertical component ($F_S \cdot \sin$).
4. Set up equilibrium in the *x*-direction and in the *y*-direction.
5. **Symmetric case:** $F_S = \dfrac{F_G}{2 \cdot \sin \alpha}$ directly.
6. **Asymmetric case:** solve for one of the tension forces from the condition in the *x*-direction and substitute it into the condition in the *y*-direction. Then only one unknown remains.
:::

::: tips "Sketching technique in the symmetric case"
Draw the gravitational force downwards. Then draw a **dashed horizontal line half the length of the gravitational force above** the object. Both tension forces should end on that line, since their vertical components should each be half the gravitational force. The lengths of the tension arrows then follow from the angles of the strings.
:::

A shallow string must be pulled tighter than a steep one. After all, the vertical component $F_S \cdot \sin \alpha$ has to carry its share of the weight, and when the angle gets smaller, $\sin \alpha$ gets smaller. At $30^\circ$ in the symmetric case the tension in each string is as large as the whole gravitational force, and for even shallower strings it is larger. That is why a line can never be pulled completely straight with something hanging from it.

::: förklaring "Explanation: Graphical solution with a parallelogram"
The tension forces can also be found by drawing, without trigonometry.

1. Draw the gravitational force $F_G$ straight down. Draw a dashed arrow straight up that is **as long as the gravitational force**. That is the force the tension forces should give together.
2. Place a ruler parallel to one of the strings and slide it until it passes through the tip of the dashed arrow. Draw a dashed line from the tip to the direction of the other string. The tension in the other string goes from the object to the point of intersection.
3. Do the same with the other string. The tension forces form a **parallelogram** with the dashed arrow as its diagonal. Measure the lengths of the arrows and convert them to newtons using the scale of the figure.

::: textlosning
We draw the forces on the lamp: the gravitational force $F_G$ straight down and the tension forces along the strings. The strings slope equally, so they are equally taut. We call the tension $F_S$.

The vertical component lies opposite the angle $40^\circ$, and the tension is the hypotenuse. This gives

$$
\sin 40^{\circ} = \frac{F_y}{F_S} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_y = F_S \cdot \sin 40^{\circ}
$$

The lamp hangs at rest, so two such vertical components together carry the gravitational force.

$$
2 \cdot F_S \cdot \sin 40^{\circ} = F_G \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F_S = \frac{F_G}{2 \cdot \sin 40^{\circ}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F_G = m \cdot g = 4.0\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} = 39.28\ \mathrm{N}
\end{array} \right]
$$

Substitution gives

$$
F_S = \frac{39.28}{2 \cdot \sin 40^{\circ}} = 30.554\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \approx 31\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Reasonableness: each string carries half the weight, 19.64 N, with its vertical component. The strings slope, so the tension has to be larger than that.

**Answer:** 31 N

::: textlosning
The strings slope by different amounts, so they do **not** carry half the weight each. We split the tension forces into components and set up a condition in each direction.

**Horizontally:** the box is at rest sideways, so the horizontal components are equal in size. They lie next to the angles and are found with cosine.

$$
F_{S1} \cdot \cos 50^{\circ} = F_{S2} \cdot \cos 30^{\circ} \tag{1}
$$

**Vertically:** the vertical components lie opposite the angles and together carry the gravitational force.

$$
F_{S1} \cdot \sin 50^{\circ} + F_{S2} \cdot \sin 30^{\circ} = F_G \tag{2}
$$

The gravitational force is

$$
F_G = m \cdot g = 5.0 \cdot 9.82 = 49.1\ \mathrm{N}
$$

From (1) we solve for $F_{S1}$ by dividing both sides by $\cos 50^{\circ}$:

$$
F_{S1} = F_{S2} \cdot \frac{\cos 30^{\circ}}{\cos 50^{\circ}} = 1.347\ldots \cdot F_{S2}
$$

We substitute $F_{S1} = 1.347\ldots \cdot F_{S2}$ and $F_G = 49.1\ \mathrm{N}$ into (2), $F_{S1} \cdot \sin 50^{\circ} + F_{S2} \cdot \sin 30^{\circ} = F_G$:

$$
1.347\ldots \cdot F_{S2} \cdot \sin 50^{\circ} + F_{S2} \cdot \sin 30^{\circ} = 49.1
$$

Here $1.347\ldots \cdot \sin 50^{\circ} = 1.032\ldots$ and $\sin 30^{\circ} = 0.5$.

$$
1.032\ldots \cdot F_{S2} + 0.5 \cdot F_{S2} = 49.1
$$

$$
1.532\ldots \cdot F_{S2} = 49.1
$$

We divide both sides by $1.532\ldots$.

$$
F_{S2} = \frac{49.1}{1.532\ldots} = 32.047\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Finally we substitute $F_{S2}$ into $F_{S1} = 1.347\ldots \cdot F_{S2}$:

$$
F_{S1} = 1.347\ldots \cdot 32.047\ldots = 43.177\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \approx 43\ \mathrm{N}
$$

The tension in string 2 is rounded in the same way:

$$
F_{S2} = 32.047\ldots\ \mathrm{N} \approx 32\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Reasonableness: the steeper string 1 carries the most, and both tension forces are smaller than the gravitational force 49.1 N.

**Answer:** $F_{S1} \approx 43\ \mathrm{N}$ and $F_{S2} \approx 32\ \mathrm{N}$

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "An object hanging from two strings"
- The tension acts **along the string**.
- The object hangs at rest, so **the resultant force is zero**.
- *α* and *β* are the angles of the strings to the ceiling (the horizontal).
:::

::: sampunkt "Components"
- Horizontal component: $F_S \cdot \cos \alpha$ (next to the angle).
- Vertical component: $F_S \cdot \sin \alpha$ (opposite the angle).
- The angle at the object is the same as the angle to the ceiling.
:::

::: sampunkt "The equilibrium conditions"
- $x$-direction: $F_{S1} \cdot \cos \alpha = F_{S2} \cdot \cos \beta$, equal in size but opposite in direction.
- $y$-direction: $F_{S1} \cdot \sin \alpha + F_{S2} \cdot \sin \beta = F_G$, together they carry the weight.
:::

::: sampunkt "Symmetric case"
- Equal angles give equal tension forces.
- $2 \cdot F_S \cdot \sin \alpha = F_G$, so $F_S = \dfrac{F_G}{2 \cdot \sin \alpha}$.
- Sketch: both arrows end half of $F_G$ above the object.
:::

::: sampunkt "Asymmetric case"
- Solve for $F_{S1}$ from the condition in the $x$-direction and substitute into the condition in the $y$-direction.
- The steeper string carries the most.
- A shallow string must be pulled tight, since $\sin \alpha$ is small.
:::
