# Resistance and Ohm's law

The opposition to the motion of the conduction electrons in a circuit is called **[[resistance|Opposition to electric current. According to Ohm's law: *U* = *R* · *I*. SI unit ohm (Ω).]]**,
written *R*, and has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] Ω (ohm).

::: tips "Introduction: resistance and the bow analogy"
Earlier we made an analogy with a bow, where the tension in the string
corresponds to the electric [[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]] and the [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of the arrow to the electric
[[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]]. The resistance would here correspond to [[air resistance|The force from the air that slows down objects moving through it. Depends on the shape, size and speed of the object.]]. If we shot
the arrow under water, the resistance would be higher and the arrow (the current) would
go more slowly, even though we have the same tension in the bow.
:::

The larger the resistance, the lower the current. This relationship is called
**[[Ohm's law|The relationship between voltage, current and resistance: *U* = *R* · *I*.]]**.

::: formel "Ohm's law"
$$
U = R \cdot I
$$

where

- *U* = voltage (V)
- *R* = resistance (Ω)
- *I* = current (A)
:::

An electrical component whose only property is to provide resistance is called a
**resistor**. With the help of resistors the current in a
circuit can be regulated.

::: textlosning
We set up Ohm's law and solve for the resistance *R*.

$$
U = R \cdot I \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad R = \frac{U}{I}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
U = 1.5\ \mathrm{V} \\
I = 0.12\ \mathrm{mA} = 0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{A}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
R = \frac{1.5}{0.12 \cdot 10^{-3}} = 12\,500\ \mathrm{\Omega}
\approx 13\,000\ \mathrm{\Omega} = 13\ \mathrm{k\Omega}
$$

**Answer:** 13 kΩ or 13 000 Ω
:::
:::

## Resistance in conductors

There is also a small resistance in the conductor itself. The longer the conductor, the
greater the resistance, since more atoms in the conductor hinder the motion of the
electrons. **Increased length gives increased resistance.**

The thicker the conductor, the more electrons can pass through a cross-section, which
at the same voltage gives an increased current. **Increased cross-sectional area gives lower
resistance.** Most conductors have a circular cross-section, and the cross-sectional area
is then the area of the circle.

Remember: the area of a circle $A = \pi \cdot r^2$.

Finally, the material and [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance are moving. SI unit kelvin (K).]] of the conductor also play a part. Some materials
conduct current better than others, depending on what they look like at the atomic level. The higher
the temperature in the conductor, the higher the resistance, since the atoms in
the material move more and hinder the electrons to a greater extent.
The material property that affects the resistance is called **resistivity**,
written *ρ* (the Greek letter "rho"); it is different for different substances and can
be looked up in a table. It has the unit Ω·m (ohm-metre).

::: formel "Resistance in conductors"
$$
R = \rho \cdot \frac{l}{A}
$$

where

- *R* = resistance in the conductor (Ω)
- *ρ* = resistivity (Ω·m)
- *l* = length of the conductor (m)
- *A* = cross-sectional area of the conductor (m²)
:::

We set up the formula for resistance in a conductor.

::: handskrift
typ: aluminiumledare
:::

::: textlosning
$$
R = \rho \cdot \frac{l}{A}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\rho = 0.027\ \mathrm{\mu \Omega \cdot m} = 0.027 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{\Omega \cdot m}\ (\text{table}) \\
l = 2.0\ \mathrm{m} \\
A = 0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2} = 0.15 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
R = 0.027 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \frac{2.0}{0.15 \cdot 10^{-4}}
= 0.0036\ \mathrm{\Omega} = 3.6\ \mathrm{m\Omega}
$$

**Answer:** 3.6 mΩ or 3.6 · 10⁻³ Ω or 0.0036 Ω
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Resistance"
- The opposition to the motion of the conduction electrons. Written $R$, with
  the unit Ω (ohm).
- A larger resistance gives a **lower** current at the same voltage.
- A **resistor** is a component whose only job is to provide resistance,
  and which thereby regulates the current.
:::

::: sampunkt "Ohm's law"
- $U = R \cdot I$
- Rearranged: $I = \dfrac{U}{R}$ and $R = \dfrac{U}{I}$.
- Protective resistance: the smallest $R$ that keeps the current below the component's
  limit.
:::

::: sampunkt "What the resistance of a conductor depends on"
- A **longer** conductor gives a **larger** resistance: more atoms in the way.
- A **thicker** conductor gives a **smaller** resistance: more electrons can
  pass.
- A **higher temperature** gives a larger resistance: the atoms move more and are
  more in the way.
- The material matters and is described by the **resistivity**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Resistance in conductors"
- $R = \rho \cdot \dfrac{l}{A}$
- $\rho$ is the **resistivity** (Ω · m), different for different substances, looked up in a
  table.
- $l$ is the length (m) and $A$ the cross-sectional area (m²).
- Circular cross-section: $A = \pi r^{2}$. Convert the area to square metres before
  substituting.
:::
:::
