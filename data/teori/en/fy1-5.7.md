# The ideal gas law and the kelvin scale

::: demo "Simulation: gas pressure, volume and temperature"
We run a simulation and investigate how the volume and the [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance are moving. SI unit kelvin (K).]] affect
the [[pressure|Force per unit area: *p* = *F* / *A*. SI unit pascal (Pa) = N/m².]] of a gas. The pressure comes from collisions between gas molecules. We
see that the number of collisions (the pressure) increases when we **decrease the volume** and
when we **increase the temperature**.
:::

The relationship between temperature, pressure and volume of gases is called the **ideal
gas law** and is used, among other things, as a simple model of the atmosphere.

We ignore the fact that the gas molecules affect each other with electromagnetic
[[forces|An influence that can change the motion or shape of an object. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]], which they do in real gases, and instead regard them as "balls" that
collide elastically. The [[effect|Work or energy per unit time: *P* = *W* / *t*. SI unit watt (W).]] of the electromagnetic forces is, however,
negligible for most gases, for example for air. Gases where we ignore
this kind of interaction between gas molecules are called **ideal**, hence the
"ideal" gas law.

::: formel "The ideal gas law"
For a fixed number of gas molecules,

$$
\frac{p_1 \cdot V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 \cdot V_2}{T_2}
$$

where

- $p_1$, $p_2$ = gas pressure in state 1 and state 2 respectively (any pressure unit, but the same)
- $V_1$, $V_2$ = volume of the gas in state 1 and state 2 respectively (any volume unit, but the same)
- $T_1$, $T_2$ = temperature of the gas in state 1 and state 2 respectively (**K**)
:::

We do not need to give the measured values of gas pressure and volume in their [[SI units|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] when
we have a relationship like the ideal gas law. The main thing is that we use
**the same unit** on both sides of the equation. So it is fine to give,
for example, the pressure in kPa or the volume in litres.

## The kelvin scale

The temperature in the ideal gas law must **always be given in the unit K (kelvin)**
and not °C (degrees Celsius)! This is because 20 °C is physically not
twice as high a temperature as 10 °C. However, 20 K is twice as high a
temperature as 10 K.

The unit kelvin is written K (without a degree sign) and starts from **absolute
zero**, that is 0 K corresponds to −273 °C (where the atoms are completely
still), and then has the same scale steps as the Celsius scale. A temperature in
kelvin can therefore not be negative.

::: formel "The kelvin scale"
$$
T_K = T_C + 273
$$

where

- $T_K$ = temperature (K)
- $T_C$ = temperature (°C)
:::

| Celsius | Kelvin |
| --- | --- |
| −273 °C | 0 K |
| 0 °C | 273 K |
| 20 °C | 293 K |

::: exempel "Example 1: An air mass rises"
**An air mass with volume 1 200 m³, pressure 101 kPa and temperature 15 °C
moves vertically upwards. The pressure falls to 80 kPa and the volume increases to
1 400 m³. Calculate the new temperature of the air mass. Answer in °C.**

::: handskrift
typ: luftmassa
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the ideal gas law and solve for the new temperature $T_2$.

$$
\frac{p_1 \cdot V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 \cdot V_2}{T_2}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
T_2 = \frac{p_2 \cdot V_2 \cdot T_1}{p_1 \cdot V_1}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
p_1 = 101\ \mathrm{kPa} \\
V_1 = 1\,200\ \mathrm{m^3} \\
T_1 = 15\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 15 + 273 = 288\ \mathrm{K} \\
p_2 = 80\ \mathrm{kPa} \\
V_2 = 1\,400\ \mathrm{m^3}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
T_2 = \frac{80 \cdot 1\,400 \cdot 288}{101 \cdot 1\,200}
= 266.138\ldots\ \mathrm{K} \approx 266\ \mathrm{K} = (266 - 273)\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
= -7\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
$$

**Answer:** −7 °C
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Classroom warming up"
**There are 30 students in a classroom. The temperature at the start of the lesson is 20 °C
and the pressure is 103 kPa. When the lesson ends, the temperature has risen to
28 °C. How large is the new pressure in the classroom?**

::: handskrift
typ: klassrum
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the ideal gas law and solve for the new pressure $p_2$.

$$
\frac{p_1 \cdot V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 \cdot V_2}{T_2}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
p_2 = \frac{p_1 \cdot V_1 \cdot T_2}{T_1 \cdot V_2}
$$

The volume of the air inside the classroom is the same before and after, so $V_1 = V_2$.
This means that we can cancel $V_1$ and $V_2$ in the relationship above, so

$$
p_2 = \frac{p_1 \cdot T_2}{T_1}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
p_1 = 103\ \mathrm{kPa} \\
T_1 = 20\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 293\ \mathrm{K} \\
T_2 = 28\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 301\ \mathrm{K}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
p_2 = \frac{103 \cdot 301}{293} = 105.812\ldots\ \mathrm{kPa} \approx 110\ \mathrm{kPa}
$$

**Answer:** 110 kPa
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The ideal gas law"
- The relationship between pressure, volume and temperature for a **fixed number** of
  gas molecules.
- $\dfrac{p_1 \cdot V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2 \cdot V_2}{T_2}$
- The pressure increases when the volume **decreases** and when the temperature **increases**.
:::

::: sampunkt "What ideal means"
- The gas molecules are regarded as balls that collide elastically.
- We ignore the electromagnetic forces between them. For air and
  most gases, that effect is negligible.
:::

::: sampunkt "The units"
- Pressure and volume may be given in **any** unit, for example kPa and
  litres.
- The only requirement is that **the same unit** is used on both sides of the
  equals sign.
- **The temperature must always be in kelvin.**
:::

::: sampunkt "The kelvin scale"
- $T_K = T_C + 273$
- Written K without a degree sign and starts from **absolute zero**,
  where the atoms are still.
- $0\ \mathrm{K}$ corresponds to $-273\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the scale steps are the same size
  as in Celsius.
- A temperature in kelvin can **never** be negative.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why not Celsius"
- $20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is physically not twice as warm as
  $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
- But $20\ \mathrm{K}$ is twice as much as $10\ \mathrm{K}$, and that is what is needed when
  the temperature appears in a ratio.
:::
:::
