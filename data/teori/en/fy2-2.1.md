# Hooke's law

If a coil spring is loaded with different weights, it will be extended. The
greater the [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] *F* loading the spring, the greater the extension
Δ*l*. The spring force upwards becomes equally large, since equilibrium of forces is established.

The force is proportional to the extension with a certain
[[constant of proportionality|The number relating two proportional quantities. If *F* is proportional to Δ*l*, then *F* = *k* · Δ*l*, where *k* is the constant of proportionality.]] *k*. This constant is called the **spring constant** and is a measure of
how "stiff" the spring is. The greater the spring constant, the more force is needed
to stretch the spring.

The relationship between the force and the extension of the spring is called **Hooke's law**.

::: formel "Hooke's law"
$$
F = k \cdot \Delta l
$$

where

- *F* = force loading the spring or force from the spring (N)
- *k* = spring constant (N/m)
- Δ*l* = extension of the spring (m)

So from the extension we can determine the weight of what we hang on
the spring. This is the principle a **spring balance** (newton meter) is based on.

## Force in a spring

Two forces act on a weight hanging on a spring:
the spring force upwards and the [[gravitational force|The force with which the Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. The gravitational force gives an object its weight and is directed straight down towards the centre of the Earth.]] downwards. Together they make up the
**[[resultant force|The sum of all forces acting on an object (vector sum). Gives the object's acceleration.]]**.

When the weight hangs at rest, the gravitational force is as large as the spring force. This
position is called the **equilibrium position**, and there the resultant force is 0.

If we take hold of the weight and pull it downwards, we change its position relative
to the equilibrium position. The position of the weight relative to the equilibrium position is written
*y* and is called the **displacement**.

::: formel "Displacement"
- *y* < 0 (negative): the weight is **below** the equilibrium position
- *y* > 0 (positive): the weight is **above** the equilibrium position
- *y* = 0: the weight is **at** the equilibrium position
:::

If we release the weight after pulling it downwards, it starts to oscillate up
and down about the equilibrium position. The distance between a turning point and
the equilibrium position is called the **[[amplitude|The maximum displacement of a wave from the equilibrium position.]]**.

The amplitude decreases and the weight finally stops. Such a decaying
oscillation is called a **damped oscillation**. It is caused by energy losses in the form
of [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]] in the spring.

If we removed the energy losses, the spring would continue to oscillate unchanged
between the same turning points, with unchanged amplitude. Such an oscillation is called
a **[[harmonic oscillation|An oscillation where the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from the equilibrium position.]]**. In problems we ignore energy losses, unless
otherwise stated.

## Resultant force on a weight on a spring

When a weight [[oscillates|An object swinging back and forth about an equilibrium position. Simple pendulum: *T* = 2*π* · √(*L* / *g*).]] on a spring, the resultant force $F_R$ is always
directed **towards the equilibrium position**.

::: formel "Resultant force on a weight on a spring"
$$
F_R = -k \cdot y
$$

where

- $F_R$ = resultant force (N)
- *k* = spring constant (N/m)
- *y* = displacement (m)

The minus sign indicates that the force has the opposite direction to the displacement:
it is always directed towards the equilibrium position.

## Speed and acceleration in spring oscillation

From the formula $F_R = -k \cdot y$ we can also say something about the [[velocity|Displacement per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]]
and the [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] in different parts of the motion.

- The **[[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]]** is **greatest at the equilibrium position**, since there is no
  resultant force there to brake the motion. The speed is **0 at
  the turning points**, since the weight stops there.

- According to [[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]], $a = \dfrac{F_R}{m}$. Since the mass is
  constant, the **acceleration** is **greatest at the turning points** (where
  the resultant is greatest) and **0 at the equilibrium position**.

::: formel "Overview: speed and acceleration"

| Position | Speed | Acceleration | Resultant force |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equilibrium position | Maximum | 0 | 0 |
| Turning point | 0 | Maximum | Maximum |

::: exempel "Example 1: Weight on a spring"
**A weight with mass 250 g is hung on a spring, which is then extended by 8.0 cm. You
then pull the weight down 5.0 cm and release it.**

**a) Determine the spring constant.**
**b) How large is the acceleration at the moment of release?**

::: handskrift
typ: fjader
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We set up Hooke's law and solve for the spring constant *k*.

$$
F = k \cdot \Delta l \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad k = \frac{F}{\Delta l} \qquad (1)
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F = m \cdot g = 0.250 \cdot 9.82 = 2.455\ \mathrm{N} \\
\Delta l = 8.0\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.080\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
k = \frac{2.455}{0.080} = 30.6875\ \mathrm{N/m}
$$

**Answer:** 31 N/m

**b)** We set up Newton's second law and solve for the acceleration *a*.

$$
F_R = m \cdot a \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a = \frac{F_R}{m} \qquad (1)
$$

We calculate the resultant force $F_R$ with $F_R = -k \cdot y$.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 30.6875\ \mathrm{N/m} \\
y = -5.0\ \mathrm{cm} = -0.050\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
F_R = -30.6875 \cdot (-0.050) = 1.534\ldots\ \mathrm{N}
$$

(a positive value indicates that the force is directed upwards, towards the equilibrium position).
Substitution into (1) gives

$$
a = \frac{1.534\ldots}{0.250} = 6.1375\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} \approx 6.1\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}
$$

**Answer:** 6.1 m/s²
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Hooke's law"
- $F = k \cdot \Delta l$
- The force is **proportional** to the extension of the spring.
- The **spring constant** $k$ (N/m) is a measure of how stiff the spring is.
  A greater $k$ means a stiffer spring.
- A **spring balance** is based on exactly this relationship.
:::

::: sampunkt "Equilibrium position and displacement"
- At the **equilibrium position** the spring force is as large as the gravitational force, so
  $F_R = 0$.
- The **displacement** $y$ is the position relative to the equilibrium position.
- $y < 0$ below the equilibrium position, $y > 0$ above, $y = 0$ at it.
:::

::: sampunkt "Resultant force"
- $F_R = -k \cdot y$
- The minus sign means that the force is always directed **towards the
  equilibrium position**.
- That is why the weight oscillates back and forth instead of
  stopping.
:::

::: sampunkt "Amplitude and damping"
- The **amplitude** is the distance between a turning point and the equilibrium position.
- **Damped oscillation**: the amplitude decreases, since energy is lost
  as heat.
- **Harmonic oscillation**: no energy losses, so the amplitude is
  unchanged. This is what we assume unless otherwise stated.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the speed and the acceleration are greatest"
- The **speed** is greatest at the **equilibrium position** and zero at the **turning points**.
- The **acceleration** is greatest at the **turning points**, where the resultant is
  greatest, and zero at the equilibrium position.
- So they are opposites of each other.
:::
