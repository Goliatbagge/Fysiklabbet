# Summary: Pressure

This chapter was about how densely packed matter is and how a force
is distributed over a surface: density, pressure between solid bodies, liquid pressure,
air pressure and total pressure. You met Archimedes' principle for the buoyant force in
liquids and gases, the ideal gas law with the kelvin scale and Pascal's principle for
hydraulics. After the chapter you should be able to calculate with all these relationships and
explain everyday phenomena such as skis on snow, diving, hot-air balloons and
hydraulic jacks.

## Concepts to know

- **Density**: mass per unit volume, $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$, SI unit
  kg/m³; determines how densely packed a material is and therefore whether an object
  floats in a medium.
- **Pressure**: force per unit area, $p = F/A$, measured in Pa (pascal) or N/m².
- **Pressure force**: the force *F* acting perpendicular to a surface; not the same
  thing as the pressure.
- **Liquid pressure (hydrostatic pressure)**: pressure caused by the weight of a column of
  liquid; depends only on the depth, not on the shape of the container or the amount of liquid.
- **Air pressure (gas pressure)**: pressure caused by the weight of the atmosphere; about
  101.3 kPa at sea level (1 atm).
- **Total pressure**: the sum of air pressure and liquid pressure at a certain depth.
- **Buoyant force**: the upward force a liquid or gas exerts on an
  immersed object.
- **Archimedes' principle**: the buoyant force is equal to the weight of the
  displaced liquid or gas.
- **The ideal gas law**: the relationship between pressure, volume and temperature for a
  fixed amount of gas.
- **The kelvin scale**: an absolute temperature scale that starts from absolute
  zero, $0\ \mathrm{K} = -273\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.
- **Pascal's principle**: a pressure increase in an enclosed liquid is transmitted
  unchanged throughout the liquid.

## Formulas

::: formel "The chapter's formulas"
**Density**

$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
$$

where

- *ρ* = density (kg/m³)
- *m* = mass (kg)
- *V* = volume (m³)

Unit conversion: $1\ \mathrm{g/cm^3} = 1\,000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

**Pressure between solid bodies**

$$
p = \frac{F}{A}
$$

where

- *p* = pressure (Pa or N/m²)
- *F* = pressure force (N)
- *A* = the area the pressure force acts on (m²)

**Liquid pressure**

$$
p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h
$$

where

- *p* = liquid pressure (Pa)
- *ρ* = density of the liquid (kg/m³)
- *g* = gravitational field strength (N/kg)
- *h* = liquid depth (m)

**Total pressure**

$$
p = p_0 + \rho \cdot g \cdot h
$$

where $p_0$ is the air pressure on the liquid surface (Pa).

**Buoyant force (Archimedes' principle)**

$$
F_L = \rho \cdot g \cdot V
$$

where

- $F_L$ = buoyant force (N)
- *ρ* = density of the displaced liquid or gas (kg/m³)
- *V* = the displaced volume / the immersed volume of the object (m³)

**The ideal gas law**

$$
\frac{p_1 \cdot V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 \cdot V_2}{T_2}
$$

where $p_1, p_2$ = gas pressure and $V_1, V_2$ = gas volume in any but the same
unit in each state, and $T_1, T_2$ = gas temperature (K) in each
state.

**The kelvin scale**

$$
T_K = T_C + 273
$$

where $T_K$ = temperature (K) and $T_C$ = temperature (°C).

**Pascal's principle**

$$
\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}
$$

where $F_1, F_2$ = the forces on each piston (N) and $A_1, A_2$ = the areas
of each piston (same unit).
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- The density of water, $\rho_\text{water} \approx 1\,000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$,
  is worth knowing by heart. It tells you directly that 1.0 litre of water has a mass of
  1.0 kg, and it is needed both for liquid pressure and for Archimedes' principle.
- Pressure force and pressure are different quantities. The same force gives different pressures
  depending on how large an area it is spread over (skis on snow, a stiletto heel
  compared with the tracks of a tank).
- The liquid pressure depends only on the depth *h*, not on the shape of the container or the
  amount of liquid.
- Liquid pressure always acts **perpendicular** to the surface it presses on.
- If you calculate the pressure force on an immersed body part (for example an eardrum)
  you should usually use only the **liquid pressure**: the air pressure is also present on the
  inside and cancels itself out.
- When something floats, $F_L = F_G$; when something sinks,
  $F_L < F_G$.
- In Archimedes' principle you use the density of the **displaced medium**
  (the liquid or gas), never the density of the object itself.
- An object floats if its overall density is lower than the density of the medium.
  That is why an iron ship floats (lots of air on board)
  while a solid iron cube sinks.
- In the ideal gas law it is enough for pressure and volume to have **the same unit** on
  both sides, but the temperature must always be given in **kelvin**, never
  Celsius.
- If the volume is constant (for example in a room), *V* can be cancelled from the ideal
  gas law and the relationship simplifies to $p_1/T_1 = p_2/T_2$.
- Pascal's principle explains why a small force on a small piston area can
  give a large force on a large piston area: the basis of hydraulic
  amplification.

## Before the test

- Do you know the formula for density and can you solve it for mass and for volume?
- Can you switch between kg/m³ and g/cm³ (conversion factor 1 000)?
- Can you tell pressure and pressure force apart, and explain why a
  stiletto heel gives a much higher pressure than the tracks of a tank?
- Can you calculate the liquid pressure at a given depth, and explain why it
  depends only on the depth?
- Can you calculate the total pressure at a depth in a liquid, with
  the air pressure included?
- Can you explain why only the liquid pressure (not the air pressure) gives a
  net force on, for example, an eardrum under water?
- Can you use Archimedes' principle to calculate the buoyant force on a
  completely or partly immersed object?
- Can you decide whether an object floats or sinks by comparing
  the buoyant force and the gravitational force?
- Can you explain why a hot-air balloon or a helium balloon rises?
- Can you convert between Celsius and kelvin, and explain why the ideal
  gas law requires kelvin?
- Can you use the ideal gas law to solve for an unknown pressure,
  volume or temperature?
- Can you explain Pascal's principle and calculate the force amplification in a
  hydraulic jack?
