# The structure of the universe

The universe is built up in **levels**, a bit like boxes inside boxes. Our Earth
lies in a **solar system**, the solar system is a tiny part of a
**galaxy**, the galaxy belongs to a **group of galaxies**, and the groups are joined together
in enormous **superclusters**. To understand astronomy you have to keep
these levels apart, and above all get a feeling for how **different in size** they
are.

::: tips "The most common misunderstanding: solar system ≠ galaxy"
A **solar system** is *one* star with its planets. A **galaxy** is
*hundreds of billions* of stars, and almost all of those stars have their own
solar systems. So our solar system is just one of unimaginably many dots in
the Milky Way. Saying "the solar system" when you mean "the galaxy" is like
saying "the house" when you mean "the whole country".
:::

## Distances are measured in light years

On Earth, metres and kilometres are enough. In space the numbers become so large that we
switch to the unit **light year**: the distance light travels in one year.

::: formel "One light year"
$$
1\ \text{light year} = c \cdot t = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\ \tfrac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}} \cdot 1\ \text{year} \approx 9.5 \cdot 10^{15}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

where

- *c* = the speed of light, $3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$
- *t* = one year ≈ $3.16 \cdot 10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$
:::

So a [[light year|The distance light travels in one year in a vacuum, about 9.5 · 10¹⁵ m (almost 10 trillion km). A measure of distance, not of time.]] is almost **10 trillion kilometres**. Since
light takes time to travel, it also means that we see everything distant as it
**looked in the past**: sunlight is 8 minutes old when it reaches us, and a
galaxy 2 million light years away we see as it looked 2 million years
ago.

::: kuriosa "Olbers' paradox: why is the night sky dark?"
That the night sky is dark sounds obvious, but it is in fact an
old riddle. If the universe were **infinitely large, infinitely old and
evenly filled with stars**, every line of sight would sooner or later
hit the surface of a star, just as every direction you look in a dense
forest hits a tree trunk. The whole sky would then shine as brightly as the
surface of the Sun, even in the middle of the night. The astronomer Heinrich Olbers made
the riddle famous in the 1820s. The solution came with modern cosmology:
the universe has a **finite age**, so light has only had time to reach
us from stars within about 13.8 billion light years, and the stars
have not existed for all eternity. In addition, the expansion of the universe
stretches the light from the most distant galaxies until it ends up
outside the visible range. So the darkness between the stars shows
that the universe has a beginning: the night sky is one of the simplest
pieces of evidence for the Big Bang.
:::

## Level 1: The solar system

Our solar system consists of the **Sun** in the centre and everything held by
its gravity: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids
and comets. The Sun contains over **99.8%** of the mass of the whole solar system;
the planets are just crumbs in comparison.

Light from the Sun takes **8 minutes** to reach Earth and just over **4 hours**
to reach Neptune. The whole solar system is about **0.001 light years** across,
small by astronomical standards.

## Level 2: The Milky Way

Our solar system is one of an estimated **200–400 billion stars**
in our galaxy, the **Milky Way**. A [[galaxy|An enormous, gravitationally bound collection of hundreds of billions of stars, gas and dust. The Milky Way is our own galaxy.]] is a gigantic,
gravitationally bound swirl of stars, gas and dust.

- The Milky Way is a **spiral galaxy**, about **100 000 light years** in
  diameter.
- The Sun is not in the centre but out in one of the spiral arms, about
  **27 000 light years** from the centre.
- The Sun takes about **230 million years** to go one full lap around
  the centre of the galaxy: a *galactic year*.
- In the centre of the Milky Way there is a **supermassive black hole**,
  *Sagittarius A\** (see section 5.5).

::: tips "Feel the difference in scale"
The solar system is about **0.001 light years** across. The Milky Way is
**100 000 light years** across. So the galaxy is about **a hundred million
times** larger than the solar system. If the solar system were shrunk to the size
of a coin, the Milky Way would still be as large as the whole of Sweden.
:::

## Level 3: The Local Group of galaxies

Galaxies are bound together in groups. The Milky Way belongs to the **Local Group**:
a collection of just over **50 galaxies** within a region about
**10 million light years** across.

- The two heaviest members are the **Milky Way** and
  the **Andromeda Galaxy**, which lies about **2.5 million light years** away.
- The rest are mostly small **dwarf galaxies** orbiting the two large ones.
- Andromeda and the Milky Way are approaching each other and will **merge**
  in about 4–5 billion years.

## Level 4: Superclusters and the large-scale structure

Groups of galaxies and larger **clusters of galaxies** are in turn joined together in even
larger structures called **superclusters**. The Local Group lies on the outskirts
of the supercluster **Laniakea**, which contains about **100 000 galaxies** and
stretches over about **500 million light years**.

On the very largest scale, the galaxies are not evenly spread out. They lie
along **filaments** and **walls** with enormous, almost empty **bubbles**
(*voids*) in between: a pattern called **the cosmic web**, which
resembles a gigantic sponge or a spider's web.

## The whole ladder: a feeling for scale

| Level | Typical size | Contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The solar system | ~0.001 light years | 1 star + planets |
| The Milky Way | ~100 000 light years | Hundreds of billions of stars |
| The Local Group | ~10 million light years | Just over 50 galaxies |
| Laniakea (supercluster) | ~500 million light years | ~100 000 galaxies |
| The observable universe | ~93 billion light years | Billions of galaxies |

Each step in the table is **thousands of times** larger than the one before.
It is precisely these jumps that make the scales of astronomy so hard to grasp,
and so fascinating.

::: kuriosa "Earth as a grain of sand"
If we shrink the Sun to a dot the size of **the full stop at the end of this
sentence**, Earth lies about ten centimetres away, and the whole
solar system fits on a desk. On the same scale, the nearest
*neighbouring star* would be several kilometres away, and the Milky Way would be as
wide as the distance from Earth to the Moon. Between the stars, space is
almost completely empty.
:::

::: kuriosa "Hubble Ultra Deep Field: thousands of galaxies in an empty patch"
In the autumn of 2003, astronomers pointed the Hubble Space Telescope at a small patch in
the constellation Fornax (the Furnace) where nothing at all could be seen: no bright stars, just
darkness. The patch is about a tenth as wide as the full Moon. The telescope
collected light from there for a total of just over **eleven days**.

The image that emerged contains about **10 000 galaxies**. Almost every
point of light is a whole galaxy with billions of stars, and the light from the faintest
has travelled for almost 13 billion years. The patch is just one of about
13 million equally large patches across the whole sky. If it looks the same in every
direction, the observable universe therefore contains about
$10\,000 \cdot 13\,000\,000 \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{11}$ galaxies, that is, more than a hundred billion, and
those are only the ones Hubble is able to see.
