# Spring energy and connected springs

A weight that [[oscillates|An object swinging back and forth about an equilibrium position. Simple pendulum: *T* = 2*π* · √(*L* / *g*).]] up and down on a coil spring (also called an **elastic
pendulum**) alternates between **[[potential energy|The energy an object has because of its position in a force field, for example *E*ₚ = *m* · *g* · *h* in a gravitational field.]]** and
**[[kinetic energy|The energy an object has because of its motion: *E*ₖ = ½ · *m* · *v*².]]**. The sum of the potential energy and the kinetic energy at
a given time is called the **oscillation energy**. It is not all the energy stored
in the spring, only the part that belongs to the oscillation itself.

By potential energy we here mean **the potential energy of the oscillation**:
the elastic energy of the spring and the gravitational potential energy of the weight together, counted
from the **equilibrium position**. It is 0 at the equilibrium position and depends only on how
far from the equilibrium position the weight is, not on whether the weight is above
or below it.

If we ignore energy losses, for example in the form of [[heat|Energy transferred between objects at different temperatures. Measured in joules (J).]], the
oscillation energy is **[[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]]**.

::: tips "Where is the energy?"
- **The turning points**: the potential energy is at its largest, the kinetic energy
  is 0 (the weight is momentarily at rest).
- **The equilibrium position**: the potential energy is 0, the kinetic energy is
  at its largest.
:::

::: förklaring "Explanation: Why is the energy equally large at both turning points?"
The spring itself is much more stretched at the lower turning point than at the upper one,
so it has **more elastic energy** down there. But at the same time the weight has
sunk and **lost just as much gravitational potential energy**. It is the gravitational force that
fills up the spring when the weight moves downwards and takes the energy back when
it moves upwards.

An example: a weight hangs on a spring with $k = 45\ \mathrm{N/m}$ and
stretches it $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ at the equilibrium position. The weight oscillates with
amplitude $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$. The gravitational potential energy is counted from the equilibrium position.

| Position | Extension | Elastic energy | Gravitational potential energy | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower turning point | 0.50 m | 5.625 J | −2.7 J | 2.925 J |
| Equilibrium position | 0.30 m | 2.025 J | 0 | 2.025 J |
| Upper turning point | 0.10 m | 0.225 J | 2.7 J | 2.925 J |

The elastic energy of the spring differs by 5.4 J between the turning points, but
the sum is equally large at both: 2.925 J, that is, 0.90 J more than at
the equilibrium position. It is this increase, counted from the equilibrium position, that we
call the potential energy of the oscillation, and it agrees with the formula for
the oscillation energy below:

$$
\frac{k \cdot A^{2}}{2} = \frac{45 \cdot 0.20^{2}}{2} = 0.90\ \mathrm{J}
$$

This always holds, not just in the example. Call the extension of the spring at
the equilibrium position $d$, where $k \cdot d = m \cdot g$, and the distance of the weight below
the equilibrium position $y$. The elastic energy and the gravitational potential energy are
together, when we replace $m \cdot g$ with $k \cdot d$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{k \cdot (d + y)^{2}}{2} - k \cdot d \cdot y \\
&= \frac{k \cdot d^{2}}{2} + k \cdot d \cdot y + \frac{k \cdot y^{2}}{2} - k \cdot d \cdot y \\
&= \frac{k \cdot d^{2}}{2} + \frac{k \cdot y^{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the energy at the equilibrium position, which we count as zero. What
remains is $\dfrac{k \cdot y^{2}}{2}$, which is equally large for $y = A$ and
$y = -A$.
:::

::: formel "Oscillation energy in a spring"
$$
E = \frac{k \cdot A^{2}}{2}
$$

where

- *E* = oscillation energy (J)
- *k* = spring constant (N/m)
- *A* = [[amplitude|The maximum displacement of a wave from the equilibrium position.]] of the oscillation (m)

::: härledning "Derivation: The oscillation energy"
We study a weight on a spring that we pull down from the equilibrium position and
release at a turning point. At the moment of release, all the oscillation energy *E* is
potential energy $E_p$:

$$
E = E_p \qquad (1)
$$

The potential energy $E_p$ corresponds to the **[[work|Energy transferred when a force acts along a distance: *W* = *F* · *s* · cos *α*. SI unit joule (J).]]** done to
pull the weight down from the equilibrium position. From before we have that

$$
\text{work} = \text{force} \cdot \text{distance}
$$

However, the [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] *F* we apply is **not constant**, but increases in proportion
to the extension according to Hooke's law. At the equilibrium position, the spring force and
the gravitational force cancel each other out, so the force we ourselves have to apply is
$k \cdot \Delta l$, where $\Delta l$ is counted from the equilibrium position.

Since work = force · [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]], the work corresponds to the **area** between
the graph and the *x*-axis. The area forms a triangle.

The base of the triangle is how far we pull the weight down, that is, the amplitude
of the oscillation *A*. The height is the largest force we apply, which is $k \cdot A$
(from Hooke's law).

$$
E = E_p = \text{the work} = \text{the triangle area} = \frac{\text{base} \cdot \text{height}}{2} = \frac{A \cdot (k \cdot A)}{2} = \frac{k \cdot A^{2}}{2}
$$

QED.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Potential energy in different positions"
**A weight hangs on a spring with spring constant 45 N/m. The weight is pulled down
20 cm from the equilibrium position and released. Determine the potential energy
of the oscillation at**

**a) the lowest turning point**
**b) the highest turning point**
**c) the equilibrium position**

::: handskrift
typ: fjaderenergi
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** At the turning points, the oscillation energy consists only of potential energy.
The potential energy there is as large as the oscillation energy.

$$
E = \frac{k \cdot A^{2}}{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 45\ \mathrm{N/m} \\
A = 20\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.20\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E = \frac{45 \cdot 0.20^{2}}{2} = 0.90\ \mathrm{J}
$$

**Answer:** 0.90 J

**b)** The upper turning point is as far from the equilibrium position as the
lower one, that is, at the distance *A*. The potential energy is therefore
the same, even though the spring itself is less stretched there. The weight's higher
position in the gravitational field makes up the difference.

**Answer:** 0.90 J

**c)** At the equilibrium position, the oscillation energy consists only of kinetic energy.
The potential energy there is 0.

**Answer:** 0
:::
:::

::: textlosning
The difference in total [[mechanical energy|The sum of kinetic energy and potential energy in a system.]] between the turning points corresponds to
the heat energy.

We calculate the oscillation energy at the first upper turning point $E_1$
and at the second upper turning point $E_2$.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 28\ \mathrm{kN/m} = 28 \cdot 10^{3}\ \mathrm{N/m} \\
A_1 = 5.0\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.050\ \mathrm{m} \\
A_2 = 2.5\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.025\ \mathrm{m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
E_1 = \frac{k \cdot A_1^{2}}{2} = \frac{28 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot (0.050)^{2}}{2} = 35\ \mathrm{J}
$$

$$
E_2 = \frac{k \cdot A_2^{2}}{2} = \frac{28 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot (0.025)^{2}}{2} = 8.75\ \mathrm{J}
$$

The difference, that is, the decrease in mechanical energy, corresponds to the heat energy.

$$
E_\mathrm{heat} = \Delta E = E_1 - E_2 = 35 - 8.75 = 26.25\ \mathrm{J} \approx 26\ \mathrm{J}
$$

**Answer:** 26 J

## Springs in series and in parallel

Springs can be connected in two ways. In a **series connection** the
springs are placed one after the other, so that one hangs from the other. In a
**parallel connection** they are placed side by side and carry the load
together. In both cases the springs together behave like **a single
spring** with a spring constant of its own, the **resultant
spring constant** $k_R$. Here we connect *n* **identical** springs,
all with the same spring constant *k*.

- **In the series connection**, each spring carries the whole weight and is extended as
  much as when it hangs alone. The pair is therefore extended twice as
  much, and the spring is **softer**: $k_R = \dfrac{k}{2}$.
- **In the parallel connection**, the springs share the weight. Each one carries
  half the weight and is extended only half as much. The pair is
  **stiffer**: $k_R = 2 \cdot k$.

In the simulation below you hang the same mass on one spring, on two
springs in series and on two springs in parallel. Compare the extensions,
and then set all three oscillating. The softest connection oscillates
most slowly.

typ: kopplade-fjadrar
:::

::: formel "Springs in series"
$$
k_R = \frac{k}{n}
$$

where

- $k_R$ = resultant spring constant (N/m)
- *k* = spring constant of each of the springs (N/m)
- *n* = number of springs

::: härledning "Derivation: Springs in series"
The springs are placed one after the other, so **the same force** *F* acts on each
of them. They have the same spring constant *k*, so Hooke's law gives the same
extension for each spring:

$$
\Delta l_1 = \frac{F}{k}
$$

There are *n* springs, so together they are extended *n* times as much
as a single spring:

$$
\Delta l = n \cdot \frac{F}{k}
$$

Hooke's law also applies to the whole connection, $F = k_R \cdot \Delta l$,
that is, $k_R = \dfrac{F}{\Delta l}$. We substitute the total extension:

$$
k_R = \frac{F}{\Delta l} = \frac{F}{n \cdot \dfrac{F}{k}} = \frac{F \cdot k}{n \cdot F} = \frac{k}{n}
$$

QED.
:::
:::

::: formel "Springs in parallel"
$$
k_R = n \cdot k
$$

where

- $k_R$ = resultant spring constant (N/m)
- *k* = spring constant of each of the springs (N/m)
- *n* = number of springs

::: härledning "Derivation: Springs in parallel"
The springs are placed side by side and carry the same bar, so they are extended
**equally much**, Δ*l*. Each spring pulls on the bar with the force
$k \cdot \Delta l$ according to Hooke's law. There are *n* springs, and
together they carry the whole force *F*:

$$
F = n \cdot k \cdot \Delta l
$$

For the whole connection, $F = k_R \cdot \Delta l$. We compare the two
expressions and see that

$$
k_R = n \cdot k
$$

QED.
:::
:::

::: tips "Remember"
- **Series** gives a **softer** spring: the spring constant is divided by the number of
  springs. Two springs give half the spring constant, three give a third.
- **Parallel** gives a **stiffer** spring: the spring constant is multiplied
  by the number of springs. Two springs give twice the spring constant, three give
  three times as much.
- The resultant spring constant is used just like an ordinary
  spring constant, in Hooke's law and in $E = \dfrac{k_R \cdot A^{2}}{2}$.
:::

::: exempel "Example 3: Two springs in series and in parallel"
**Two identical springs have a spring constant of 40 N/m each. A weight with
mass 0.50 kg is hung on the springs. How much is the pair of springs extended if
the springs are**

**a) connected in series**
**b) connected in parallel**

::: handskrift
typ: kopplade-fjadrar
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The springs in series behave like a single spring with the resultant
spring constant $k_R$. We determine it first.

$$
k_R = \frac{k}{n}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 40\ \mathrm{N/m} \\
n = 2
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
k_R = \frac{40}{2} = 20\ \mathrm{N/m}
$$

The weight hangs at rest, so the spring force is as large as the gravitational force.
Hooke's law gives the extension.

$$
F = k_R \cdot \Delta l \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \Delta l = \frac{F}{k_R}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F = m \cdot g = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg} = 4.91\ \mathrm{N} \\
k_R = 20\ \mathrm{N/m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\Delta l = \frac{4.91}{20} = 0.2455\ \mathrm{m} \approx 0.25\ \mathrm{m}
$$

**Answer:** 0.25 m

**b)** In parallel, the spring constant is multiplied by the number of springs,
still $n = 2$.

$$
k_R = n \cdot k
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
k = 40\ \mathrm{N/m} \\
n = 2
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
k_R = 2 \cdot 40 = 80\ \mathrm{N/m}
$$

The same weight as in a), now with the new spring constant.

$$
\Delta l = \frac{F}{k_R}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
F = 4.91\ \mathrm{N} \\
k_R = 80\ \mathrm{N/m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\Delta l = \frac{4.91}{80} = 0.061375\ \mathrm{m} \approx 0.061\ \mathrm{m}
$$

The parallel connection is four times as stiff as the series connection and
is therefore extended only a quarter as much.

**Answer:** 0.061 m
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The energy alternates"
- A weight on a spring, an **elastic pendulum**, alternates between
  **potential energy** and **kinetic energy**.
- The potential energy is that of the spring and the gravitational force together,
  counted from the **equilibrium position**.
- The sum is called the **oscillation energy**.
- Without energy losses it is **constant**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the energy is"
- **The turning points**: the potential energy is largest, the kinetic energy is 0.
- **The equilibrium position**: the potential energy is 0, the kinetic energy is
  largest.
:::

::: sampunkt "The formula"
- $E = \dfrac{k A^{2}}{2}$
- $A$ is the amplitude. The energy grows with the **square** of the amplitude:
  double the amplitude gives four times the energy.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the formula comes from"
- The force is **not constant**, but grows linearly according to Hooke's law.
- The work is therefore the **area** under the force–extension graph, that is, a
  triangle.
- The base is $A$ and the height is $kA$, which gives
  $\dfrac{A \cdot kA}{2}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Damping"
- The energy that becomes heat is the **difference** between the energy at the two
  turning points.
- $E_v = \dfrac{k A_1^{2}}{2} - \dfrac{k A_2^{2}}{2}$
- A halved amplitude means that three quarters of the energy has been lost.
:::

::: sampunkt "Connected springs"
- *n* identical springs with spring constant *k*.
- **Series**: $k_R = \dfrac{k}{n}$. The same force in all of them, the extensions
  add up. **Softer.**
- **Parallel**: $k_R = n \cdot k$. The same extension, the force is shared.
  **Stiffer.**
:::
:::
