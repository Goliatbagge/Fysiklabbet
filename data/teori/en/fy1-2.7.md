# Motion with constant acceleration

If something changes its [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] at a steady rate, it has a **[[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]]**,
or performs a **uniformly accelerated motion**.

::: formel "Velocity and displacement at constant acceleration"
$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t
$$

$$
s = (s_0) + v_0 \cdot t + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

where

- *v* = velocity (m/s)
- *v*₀ = [[initial velocity|The velocity at the time *t* = 0 (when the motion starts). Written *v*₀.]] / starting velocity (m/s)
- *a* = acceleration (m/s²)
- *t* = time (s)
- *s* = [[displacement|The vector from the start position to the end position. Differs from distance, which is the total length of path.]] (m)
- *s*₀ = starting position (m)

::: härledning "Derivation: Velocity at constant acceleration"
We already know that for constant acceleration

$$
a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
$$

If we solve for the change in velocity Δ*v* we get

$$
\Delta v = a \cdot \Delta t
$$

If we have an initial velocity *v*₀ at the time *t* = 0, we
have to add it to get the final velocity, that is

$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t \qquad \text{QED}
$$
:::

::: härledning "Derivation: Displacement at constant acceleration"
We have already established that the area under a *v-t* graph corresponds to
the displacement. We draw a general *v-t* graph of a motion with
initial velocity *v*₀ and constant acceleration.

From the graph we get the displacement as

$$
\Delta s = \text{rectangle area} + \text{triangle area}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
\text{Rectangle area} = \text{base} \cdot \text{height} = t \cdot v_0 = v_0 \cdot t \\
\text{Triangle area} = \dfrac{\text{base} \cdot \text{height}}{2}
   = \dfrac{t \cdot \Delta v}{2} = \dfrac{\Delta v \cdot t}{2}
   = \dfrac{a \cdot t \cdot t}{2} = \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\Delta s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2} \qquad \text{QED}
$$
:::
:::

## Three special cases

::: formel "Three special cases"
**Braking**: *s* in the formula can be given as a position (with sign) AND as a [[distance|The total length of path an object has moved. A scalar quantity, measured in metres.]]
(without sign). It is important to calculate with signs if the motion at some stage involves
braking, for example when something is thrown upwards.

**Without a starting velocity**: for acceleration without an initial velocity,
*v*₀ = 0. The simplified formulas are then

$$
v = a \cdot t \qquad\text{and}\qquad s = \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

**[[Free fall|When an object falls completely without air resistance, so that only the gravitational force acts on it. All objects then fall equally fast, regardless of mass.]]**: in free fall $a = -g$ where $g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
The formulas for velocity and displacement in free fall are then

$$
v = v_0 - g \cdot t \qquad\text{and}\qquad s = v_0 \cdot t - \frac{g \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$
:::

::: formel "Average velocity at constant acceleration"
$$
v_m = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}
$$

where

- *v*ₘ = [[average velocity|The mean velocity over a time interval: the displacement Δ*s* divided by the time Δ*t*.]]
- *v*₀ = initial velocity
- *v* = final velocity

The formula applies **only** when the acceleration is constant. Then the velocity increases (or
decreases) at a steady rate, and the average velocity is simply
the mean of the starting and final velocities.

::: härledning "Derivation: Average velocity at constant acceleration"
The average velocity is, as in all motion, the displacement divided by the time:

$$
v_m = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}
$$

During the time *t*, according to the formula above, the object has moved

$$
\Delta s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

We substitute this into the definition and divide each term by *t*:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_m &= \frac{v_0 \cdot t + \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}}{t} \\
&= \frac{v_0 \cdot t}{t} + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2 \cdot t} \\
&= v_0 + \frac{a \cdot t}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

From the velocity formula $v = v_0 + a \cdot t$ we know that $a \cdot t = v - v_0$.
We replace $a \cdot t$ with that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_m &= v_0 + \frac{v - v_0}{2} \\
&= \frac{2 \cdot v_0}{2} + \frac{v - v_0}{2} \\
&= \frac{2 \cdot v_0 + v - v_0}{2} \\
&= \frac{v_0 + v}{2} \qquad \text{QED}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The same thing in the *v-t* graph.** The displacement is the area under the graph. When
the acceleration is constant the graph is a straight line, and then the
average velocity *v*ₘ lies exactly halfway between *v*₀ and *v*. The rectangle with
height *v*ₘ has the same area as the region under the line: the orange triangle
that the rectangle includes **above** the line on the left is exactly as large
as the orange triangle it misses **below** the line on the right.

The area of the rectangle, $v_m \cdot t$, must therefore be equal to the displacement
$\Delta s$, that is

$$
v_m = \frac{\Delta s}{t}
$$

which is exactly the definition of average velocity. That *v*ₘ lies halfway
between *v*₀ and *v* is therefore the same thing as $v_m = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}$.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: The overtaking car"
**A car with a velocity of 18 m/s is going to overtake and increases its [[speed|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]] with a
constant acceleration of 2.0 m/s².**

**a) What is the car's velocity after 3.0 s?**
**b) How far has it driven during this time?**
**c) After 3.0 seconds the driver has to abandon the overtaking and brake down to a velocity of 16 m/s. How long does it take if the [[deceleration|Negative acceleration: when the velocity of an object decreases (braking).]] is 4.0 m/s²?**

::: handskrift
typ: omkorning
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** We write down the formula for velocity at constant acceleration.

$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
v_0 = 18\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
a = 2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \\
t = 3.0\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
v = 18 + 2.0 \cdot 3.0 = 18 + 6 = 24\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

**Answer:** 24 m/s

**b)** We write down the formula for distance at constant acceleration.

$$
s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}
$$

Substituting the same values as in part a gives

$$
s = 18 \cdot 3.0 + \frac{2.0 \cdot 3.0^{2}}{2}
= 54 + \frac{18}{2} = 54 + 9 = 63\ \mathrm{m}
$$

**Answer:** 63 m

**c)** We write down the formula for velocity at constant acceleration and solve
for the time *t*.

$$
v = v_0 + a \cdot t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t = \frac{v - v_0}{a}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
v = 16\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
v_0 = 24\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (\text{the velocity at 3.0 s from part a}) \\
a = -4.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \quad (\text{minus sign: negative acceleration})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
t = \frac{16 - 24}{-4.0} = \frac{-8.0}{-4.0} = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}
$$

**Answer:** 2.0 s
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Find your reaction time"
**Let your friend hold up a ruler vertically while you hold your thumb and
index finger so that the top edge of your fingers is at the ruler's zero mark.
When your friend lets go, you pinch your fingers together as quickly as possible. Read off
on the ruler where the top edge of your fingers ended up. Then calculate your
reaction time.**

::: handskrift
typ: reaktionstid
:::

::: textlosning
The ruler falls with the constant acceleration *g* = 9.82 m/s² and we get
a falling distance that we read off at the fingers. (Since we are looking at the distance,
we do not need to worry about minus signs.) We write down the formula for distance
at constant acceleration and solve for the time *t*. Since *v*₀ = 0, that
term can be left out, so

$$
s = \frac{g \cdot t^{2}}{2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot s}{g}}
$$

If, for example, your fingers ended up at 12.0 cm, then *s* = 12.0 cm =
0.120 m and *g* = 9.82 m/s², which gives the reaction time

$$
t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 0.120}{9.82}} = 0.1563\ldots\ \mathrm{s}
$$

**Answer:** 0.156 s
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Uniformly accelerated motion"
- The velocity changes at a steady rate, that is **constant acceleration**.
- $v = v_0 + a \cdot t$
- $s = s_0 + v_0 \cdot t + \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}$
- $s_0$ is the starting position and is included only when the motion does not start at the origin.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the formula comes from"
- The velocity formula: $\Delta v = a \cdot \Delta t$ plus
  the initial velocity.
- The distance formula: the area under the *v-t* graph, that is the rectangle
  $v_0 \cdot t$ plus the triangle $\dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Without a starting velocity"
- If the motion starts from rest, $v_0 = 0$ and the formulas simplify:
- $v = a \cdot t$ and $s = \dfrac{a \cdot t^{2}}{2}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Free fall"
- In free fall $a = -g$ with
  $g \approx 9.82\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
- $v = v_0 - g \cdot t$
- $s = v_0 \cdot t - \dfrac{g \cdot t^{2}}{2}$
:::

::: sampunkt "Braking and signs"
- **Always calculate with signs** when the motion changes direction, for example
  when something is thrown upwards.
- $s$ can mean both position (with sign) and distance (without sign). Keep
  them apart.
- A deceleration is substituted as a negative acceleration.
:::

::: sampunkt "Average velocity"
- At constant acceleration: $v_m = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}$.
- Applies because the velocity changes steadily: $v_m$ lies halfway between
  $v_0$ and $v$, and the rectangle with height $v_m$ has the same area as the region
  under the *v-t* line.
- If the equation becomes a quadratic equation, it is fine to solve it graphically
  or with CAS in GeoGebra. Write in your solution what you did.
:::
:::
