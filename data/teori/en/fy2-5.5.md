# Black holes

A **black hole** is a region in space where mass is so tightly
packed that gravity becomes extreme: so strong that **nothing**,
not even [[light|Electromagnetic radiation. In a vacuum, light travels at the speed *c* ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s.]], can get out once it has come inside a certain boundary.
That is why it looks **black**: no radiation reaches us from there.

Despite the name, a black hole is not a "hole" in empty space but,
on the contrary, a place with an enormous amount of mass in a very small volume. To
understand how this is possible, we start with an idea you already know:
**escape velocity**.

## Escape velocity

To leave a celestial body, an object must be launched so fast that
gravity never manages to slow it down to a standstill. The lowest
such speed is called the [[escape velocity|The lowest speed an object must have to escape completely from the gravity of a celestial body.]]. Setting the kinetic energy equal
to the work gravity can do on the way out gives:

::: formel "Escape velocity"
$$
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}
$$

where

- $v_f$ = escape velocity (m/s)
- *G* = the gravitational constant, $6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N \cdot m^2/kg^2}$
- *M* = mass of the celestial body (kg)
- *r* = distance from the centre (m)
:::

Notice that the escape velocity **increases** the larger the mass *M* is,
and the **smaller** the radius *r* is. If you squeeze the same mass into a
smaller ball, the escape velocity rises. The question then becomes: what happens if you
pack the mass so tightly that the escape velocity reaches all the way up to
**the speed of light** *c*?

## The event horizon and the Schwarzschild radius

If the escape velocity becomes equal to the speed of light *c*, not
even light can escape. Setting $v_f = c$ and solving for the radius gives the
distance inside which nothing can escape, the **Schwarzschild radius**:

::: formel "The Schwarzschild radius"
$$
c = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r_s}} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^{2}}
$$

where

- $r_s$ = Schwarzschild radius (m)
- *G* = the gravitational constant, $6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N \cdot m^2/kg^2}$
- *M* = mass of the black hole (kg)
- *c* = the speed of light, $3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$
:::

The spherical surface at the distance $r_s$ from the centre is called the
[[event horizon|The boundary surface around a black hole. Inside it nothing, not even light, can get out.]]. It is not a solid surface you can
touch: it is a **boundary for information**. Everything that passes in through
the event horizon is cut off for ever from the rest of the universe. That
is why we can never see *into* a black hole; we can only observe
what happens **outside** the horizon.

::: exempel "Example: How small would the Sun be as a black hole?"
**Calculate the Schwarzschild radius of a black hole with the same mass as
the Sun.**

::: handskrift
typ: schwarzschild
:::

::: textlosning
We use the formula for the Schwarzschild radius.

$$
r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^{2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
G = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N \cdot m^2/kg^2} \\
M = 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg} \\
c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
r_s = \frac{2 \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}}{(3.00 \cdot 10^{8})^{2}} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{3}\ \mathrm{m}
$$

**Answer:** About 3.0 km. The whole mass of the Sun, just over $1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, would thus fit inside an event horizon the size
of a small town.
:::
:::

## How black holes form

Most of the black holes we know of form when a **very massive star
dies**. As long as a star shines, the radiation pressure from
fusion in the core holds out against gravity and the star keeps its size.
When the fuel runs out, the pressure stops, and gravity wins.

- For stars lighter than about 8 solar masses, it ends with a
  **white dwarf**.
- For heavier stars the core collapses in a flash in a
  [[supernova|A huge explosion when the core of a massive star collapses. For a short time it shines brighter than an entire galaxy.]], and what remains in the middle becomes either
  a [[neutron star|An extremely compact remnant of a collapsed star, made up entirely of neutrons. A teaspoonful weighs billions of tonnes.]] or, if the remnant is heavy enough, a
  **black hole**.

How small a neutron star actually is (the whole star fits within the area
of a large city, yet weighs more than the Sun) you can see in the simulation
[The neutron star to scale](fysik2-neutronstjarna.html), where it is shown
next to the Earth at exactly the same scale.

There is no known force that can stop the collapse when the remnant is heavy
enough. The matter is squeezed towards a single point of infinite density,
a **[[singularity|The point at the centre of a black hole where, according to theory, the mass is compressed to infinite density.]]**, surrounded by its event horizon.

## Different types of black holes

| Type | Mass | Where they are found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stellar-mass | about 3–100 solar masses | The remnant of a collapsed massive star |
| Supermassive | millions–billions of solar masses | At the centres of galaxies |

At the centre of our own galaxy, the **Milky Way**, there is a supermassive
black hole called *Sagittarius A\** with a mass of about **4 million
solar masses**. How such giants have had time to become so heavy is still an
open research question.

## How do you see something invisible?

A black hole does not itself emit any light, yet astronomers can
find them in several ways:

- **Matter falling in** first swirls in a hot, rapidly rotating
  **accretion disc** around the hole. Friction heats the gas so strongly
  that it glows in **X-rays** before it passes the horizon.
- **The orbits of stars.** By following how stars near the centre of the galaxy
  move, you can calculate the mass of the invisible object they orbit
  around. That is how Sagittarius A\* was mapped.
- **Gravitational waves.** When two black holes merge, ripples
  in spacetime itself are sent out through the universe. They were detected
  for the first time by **LIGO in 2015** and directly confirmed that black holes
  exist.

::: kuriosa "The first image of a black hole"
In 2019 the **Event Horizon Telescope** collaboration published the first
"image" of a black hole: the supermassive hole in the galaxy *M87*. It
does not show the hole itself (it is black) but the glowing accretion disc
around it, shaped like a bright ring around a dark shadow. The telescope consisted
of radio telescopes spread across the whole Earth, linked together so that
together they worked as one telescope the size of the entire planet.
:::

::: kuriosa "Time slows down at the horizon"
According to Einstein's general theory of relativity, time runs more slowly where
gravity is stronger. To a distant observer, an object
falling in towards a black hole appears to **move more and more slowly** the closer
to the horizon it gets, and never quite seems to pass it, while
its light becomes more and more redshifted and fades out. For the object
itself, however, time passes completely normally, and it falls straight in.
:::
