# Period of a spring oscillation

::: demo "Investigate the period"
In the simulation below you can change the mass, the spring constant and the amplitude
while the weight oscillates, or pull the weight up or down and release it. The dimension line in the graph shows how long the latest
oscillation took. What happens to the period when you change the amplitude?

::: minisim
typ: periodtid
:::
:::

How long an oscillation takes (the [[period|The time for one complete oscillation or one complete wave: *T* = 1 / *f*.]]) depends **only** on the mass of
the oscillating object and on the spring constant. The period does **not** depend
on the [[amplitude|The maximum displacement of a wave from the equilibrium position.]] of the oscillation.

::: formel "Period of a weight on a spring"
$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}
$$

where

- *T* = period (s) (time for one complete oscillation)
- *m* = mass of the weight (kg)
- *k* = spring constant (N/m)

::: härledning "Derivation: Period of a weight on a spring"
We solve for the period *T* from the formula for angular velocity
$\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}$:

$$
T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} \qquad (1)
$$

We now need an expression for the angular velocity ω. The resultant
[[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] in a spring oscillation gives an [[acceleration|How quickly the velocity changes. SI unit m/s². A vector quantity.]] according to [[Newton's second law|Resultant force = mass × acceleration: *F* = *m* · *a*.]]:

$$
F_R = m \cdot a \qquad (2)
$$

The acceleration at each position in a [[harmonic oscillation|An oscillation where the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from the equilibrium position.]] is
$a = -\omega^{2} \cdot y$. Substituting this into (2) gives

$$
F_R = m \cdot (-\omega^{2} \cdot y) = -m \cdot \omega^{2} \cdot y \qquad (3)
$$

From before we also have that the [[resultant force|The sum of all forces acting on an object (vector sum). Gives the object's acceleration.]] at each position is

$$
F_R = -k \cdot y \qquad (4)
$$

We set (3) and (4) equal:

$$
-m \cdot \omega^{2} \cdot y = -k \cdot y
$$

We divide both sides by $-y$ and solve for ω.

$$
\begin{array}{c} \displaystyle m \cdot \omega^{2} = k \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \end{array}
$$

(the negative solution is ignored)

Substituting $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ into (1) gives

$$
T = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{k/m}} = 2\pi \cdot \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}
$$

QED.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Period of a 100-gram weight"
**A 100-gram weight is hung on a spring, which is then extended by 9.0 cm. It is pulled down
a further 5.0 cm and released. Determine the period.**

::: handskrift
typ: periodfjader
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for the period.

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \qquad (1)
$$

The spring constant *k* is not given, so we calculate it in the bracket with Hooke's law $F = k \cdot \Delta l$. The force that stretches the spring is the weight of the mass, $F = m \cdot g$.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
m = 100\ \mathrm{g} = 0.100\ \mathrm{kg} \\
\Delta l = 9.0\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.090\ \mathrm{m} \\
F = k \cdot \Delta l \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m \cdot g = k \cdot \Delta l \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad k = \dfrac{m \cdot g}{\Delta l} \\[6pt]
k = \dfrac{0.100\ \mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.82\ \mathrm{N/kg}}{0.090\ \mathrm{m}} = 10.911\ldots\ \mathrm{N/m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

Substituting $m = 0.100$ kg and $k = 10.911\ldots$ N/m into (1) gives

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{0.100}{10.911\ldots}} = 0.601\ldots\ \mathrm{s} \approx 0.60\ \mathrm{s}
$$

**Answer:** 0.60 s
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Which mass gives the desired period?"
**A spring has a spring constant of 0.14 kN/m. How large a mass should it
be loaded with for the period to be 0.20 s?**

::: handskrift
typ: massafjader
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the formula for the period and solve for the mass *m*.

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}
$$

We square both sides to get rid of the square root and solve for *m*.

$$
T^{2} = 4\pi^{2} \cdot \frac{m}{k} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m = \frac{T^{2} \cdot k}{4\pi^{2}}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
T = 0.20\ \mathrm{s} \\
k = 0.14\ \mathrm{kN/m} = 140\ \mathrm{N/m}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
m = \frac{0.20^{2} \cdot 140}{4\pi^{2}} = 0.141\ldots\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 0.14\ \mathrm{kg} = 140\ \mathrm{g}
$$

**Answer:** 0.14 kg (140 g)
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The period of a spring pendulum"
- $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$
- Depends **only** on the mass and the spring constant.
- **The amplitude does not matter.** A large oscillation takes as long
  as a small one.
:::

::: sampunkt "What affects it"
- A **heavier** weight gives a **longer** period.
- A **stiffer** spring, that is, a greater $k$, gives a **shorter** period.
:::

::: sampunkt "Where the formula comes from"
- Set $F_R = -k y$ equal to
  $F_R = m a = -m \omega^{2} y$.
- Then $y$ cancels and what remains is
  $\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$.
- Substitute into $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "If k is unknown"
- If the weight hangs at rest, there is equilibrium of forces:
  $mg = k \cdot \Delta l$.
- So $k = \dfrac{mg}{\Delta l}$, where $\Delta l$ is the extension that
  the weight causes.
:::

::: sampunkt "Solving for the mass"
- Square both sides: $T^{2} = 4\pi^{2} \cdot \dfrac{m}{k}$
- Solve for the mass: $m = \dfrac{k T^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}$
- Remember to square the whole period.
:::
:::
