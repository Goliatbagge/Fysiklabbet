# Refraction of light

We already know that the [[velocity|Displacement per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of light in a vacuum is [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]]
$2.998\ldots \cdot 10^{8}$ m/s, whether the observer is at rest or
moving, according to the theory of relativity. The speed of light can, however, be
**different in different materials**!

Light rays that pass the boundary between two different transparent media
change direction: they are **refracted**. The number that determines how much light is refracted in a
material is called the **refractive index**, which in turn depends on the speed of
light in the material. Refractive indices are looked up in a table.

The **higher** the refractive index, the **optically denser** the medium. The
**lower** the refractive index, the **optically less dense** the medium.

::: formel "Refractive index"
$$
n = \frac{c}{v}
$$

where

- *n* = refractive index of the substance
- *c* = speed of light in a vacuum ($2.998\ldots \cdot 10^{8}$ m/s)
- *v* = speed of light in the substance (m/s)
:::

::: tips "Examples of refractive indices"
| Medium | Refractive index, *n* |
| --- | --- |
| Vacuum | 1 |
| Air | 1.00003 (= 1 in our calculations) |
| Water | 1.33 |
| Glass | 1.52 |
| Diamond | 2.42 |
:::

A ray striking a transparent surface is both **refracted** and **reflected**. We often
do not draw the reflected ray, but it is always there. A
[[line|A straight path between two points; here a line drawn at right angles to the surface.]] perpendicular to the boundary is called the **normal**. Angles are always measured
between the ray and the normal. Learn the names of the different rays and
angles in the figure below.

Ray path into a denser medium. The angle of incidence *i*, the angle of reflection *r* and the angle of refraction *b* are always measured between the ray and the normal.
:::

::: formel "The law of refraction for light (Snell's law)"
$$
n_1 \cdot \sin i = n_2 \cdot \sin b
$$

where

- $n_1$ = refractive index of medium 1
- *i* = angle of incidence in medium 1
- $n_2$ = refractive index of medium 2
- *b* = angle of refraction in medium 2

**NOTE!** The angles are measured from the **normal to the ray**.
:::

::: kuriosa "Fermat's principle"
The law of refraction means that light takes the path between two points that takes the
**shortest time** (!).
:::

::: tips "Refraction towards or away from the normal"
A light ray that travels from an optically

- **less dense medium into an optically denser one** (for example from air to glass)
  is refracted **towards the normal**.
- **denser medium into an optically less dense one** (for example from glass to air)
  is refracted **away from the normal**.

If the angle of incidence $i = 0°$, the ray passes straight through the surface without being
refracted, that is $b = 0°$.
:::

We also revise the law of reflection from our earlier work on waves.

::: formel "The law of reflection"
$$
i = r
$$

where

- *i* = angle of incidence
- *r* = angle of reflection
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Angle of refraction from air to glass"
**A light ray strikes glass from air with an angle of incidence of 50°. Determine
the angle of refraction if the refractive index of the glass is 1.50.**

::: handskrift
typ: brytningglas
:::

::: textlosning
We set up the law of refraction and solve for the angle of refraction *b*.

$$
n_1 \cdot \sin i = n_2 \cdot \sin b \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \sin b = \frac{n_1 \cdot \sin i}{n_2} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad b = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{n_1 \cdot \sin i}{n_2}\right)
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
n_1 = 1.00\ (\text{air}) \\
i = 50^\circ \\
n_2 = 1.50\ (\text{glass})
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
b = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{1.00 \cdot \sin 50^\circ}{1.50}\right) = 30.710\ldots^\circ \approx 31^\circ
$$

**Answer:** 31°
:::
:::

::: kuriosa "Invisibility"
If you lower a transparent object into a transparent liquid with
**the same refractive index**, the object becomes **invisible**. One
example is polymer balls with the same refractive index as water: they become
completely invisible when they end up in the water.
:::

::: kuriosa "Why the speed of light decreases"
What makes light slow down in matter, for example in
glass or water? And how can light increase its speed, and seemingly
"gain" energy, when it passes from an optically denser medium into an
optically less dense one, for example when it leaves glass and enters air?

The explanation requires us to see light as a **wave**. When light passes
through a material, the light changes the electromagnetic field inside the material.
Since we have a material (unlike a vacuum), it contains atoms with
electrons. When these electrons are affected by the electromagnetic
field from the light, they in turn create an electromagnetic field
(moving charges create an electromagnetic field, from an earlier part
of the course). When these two waves are **superposed**, they give a
resultant wave with a lower speed, and it is this resultant
wave speed that gives the speed in the medium!

When the light leaves the medium, the interfering wave from the electrons stops
and the speed of the wave becomes the same again as before it struck the
medium.
:::

::: kuriosa "Why light bends"
What makes light bend when it strikes a surface obliquely
with a different optical density? Unfortunately this is hard to answer simply within
the scope of this course, because it comes from **Maxwell's equations**
for describing electromagnetic waves, whose theory we do not have.

But according to Maxwell's equations the [[electric field|Region around a charge where another charge is affected by an electric force.]], which is
perpendicular to the direction of the light, has one component that is **parallel** to
the boundary and one component that is **perpendicular** to the boundary. The
parallel component is the same in both media, because light that travels
perpendicularly into the boundary is not affected by the materials'
refractive indices. The perpendicular component, on the other hand, is affected by the
materials' refractive indices and makes the resultant field in the new
medium change direction, exactly according to the law of refraction above.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The speed of light in materials"
- In a vacuum $c$ is constant, but in **materials light travels more slowly**.
- **Refractive index**: $n = \dfrac{c}{v}$, a number without a unit.
- A vacuum has $n = 1$, water 1.33, glass 1.52 and diamond 2.42. Air is taken
  as 1.
:::

::: sampunkt "Optically dense and less dense"
- A **higher** refractive index means an **optically denser** medium.
- A **lower** one means optically less dense.
:::

::: sampunkt "Snell's law"
- $n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin b$
- The angles are always measured **from the normal to the ray**, never from the surface.
- A ray striking a transparent surface is **both refracted and reflected**, even
  if the reflected one is rarely drawn.
:::

::: sampunkt "Which way it bends"
- **Less dense to denser**, like air to glass: bent **towards** the normal.
- **Denser to less dense**, like glass to air: bent **away from** the normal.
- If $i = 0^\circ$ the ray passes straight through without being refracted.
:::

::: sampunkt "The law of reflection"
- $i = r$, that is, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why light slows down"
- The field of the light sets the electrons of the material in motion, and they in
  turn create their own field.
- The two waves are **superposed** into a resultant wave with a lower
  speed.
- When the light leaves the medium the effect stops and the speed returns. No
  energy has been created or lost.
:::
:::
