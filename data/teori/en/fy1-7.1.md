# Charge and electrostatic induction

What gives [[electric charge|A fundamental property of matter, can be positive or negative. SI unit coulomb (C).]] is electrons, which are negatively charged, and
protons, which are positively charged. How large an excess or deficit of
electrons an object has is called its **charge**.

## Forces between charges

::: demo "Forces between charges"
We charge a glass rod positively and place it on a compass stand. Then
we charge an ebonite rod negatively and bring it close to the glass rod. We see that they
are drawn towards each other.

We then replace the positively charged glass rod with a negatively charged
ebonite rod. We approach it with another negatively charged ebonite rod. We
see that they are pushed away from each other.
:::

Objects with

- **opposite** charges affect each other with a [[force|An influence that can change the motion or shape of an object. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]] that pulls them towards
  each other. When forces pull towards each other they are called **attractive**.
- **like** charges affect each other with a force that pushes them away from
  each other. When forces push away from each other they are called **repulsive**.

In a material the protons are more fixed in their positions than the
mobile electrons. So it is mainly the electrons that determine
what charge an object has. Electrons that are transported in a material
are called **conduction electrons**.

An

- **excess of electrons** leads to a **negative charge**
- **deficit of electrons** leads to a **positive charge**
- equal number of electrons and protons leads to a **neutral charge**
  (there is no net charge)

## Conductors and insulators

::: demo "Electron conduction"
We lay a metal rod on a beaker. We hang an aluminium ball on a thread
so that it touches one end of the metal rod. We then charge the metal rod
and see that the ball is pushed away. Electrons have been transported through
the metal rod to the ball.

We replace the metal rod with a plastic drinking straw and repeat the experiment. When
we charge the straw, nothing happens to the ball. Electrons are not transported
through the straw.
:::

Different materials conduct charges with different ease. Materials are divided into three different
categories depending on how easily they conduct electrons.

- **Conductors:** Electrons move easily in the material. Examples: metals and
  salt solutions.
- **Insulators:** Have no electrons that move easily, because the electrons are
  tightly bound to the atoms. Examples: glass, plastic and wood.
- **Semiconductors:** Have both properties. They are insulators at low temperatures
  and conductors at high ones. Examples: silicon and selenium.

::: demo "The Van de Graaff generator"
We place uncharged aluminium cake cases on an uncharged Van de Graaff generator, which we
then charge negatively. We see that the aluminium cases fly off. This is
because excess electrons from the generator's dome are transferred to
the aluminium cases (which are good conductors). Then both the generator's dome
and the aluminium cases become negatively charged, and they repel!
:::

## Electrostatic induction

::: demo "Forces between neutral objects and charges"
We bring a negatively charged object close to an uncharged

- piece of paper, using a plastic comb. The neutral piece of paper
  is attracted by the comb.
- stream of water, using an ebonite rod. The neutral stream of water
  bends towards the rod.
- aluminium can, using a charged ebonite rod. The neutral aluminium can
  starts to roll towards the rod.
:::

Even though an object is neutral, electric attractive forces can arise.
This is because the charges in a neutral object are redistributed when
a charged object is brought close to it. A redistribution of charges
caused by an external [[electric field|The region around a charge where another charge is acted on by an electric force.]] is called **electrostatic induction**.

## Induction in a neutral conductor

In a conductor the electrons move easily in the object while the protons are fixed
in their positions. When we approach a neutral conductor with a negatively
charged rod from the right, electrons will be repelled to the left. The
right end then gets a deficit of electrons, becomes positively charged, and
an attractive force arises between the positive end and the negative
rod.

## Measuring charge

Charge is written *Q* (for large charges) or *q* (for small/single
charges) and has the unit C (coulomb).

An electron has the charge −1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. The proton has the same charge,
but positive. So charges are built up of a certain number of
electron/proton charges. That is why the charge 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C is called
the **elementary charge** (the smallest unit of charge).

The atom also contains the neutron. It has the charge 0 C and is therefore
neutral, hence its name.

The "extra" electrons a charged object has (how many more electrons than
protons the object has) are called **excess electrons**.

Because charges of the same sign repel each other, charges
tend to spread out evenly.

| Particle | Charge (C) |
| --- | --- |
| electron | (−)1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ |
| proton | (+)1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ |
| neutron | 0 |

::: formel "Law of conservation of charge"
**1.** Charges cannot be created or destroyed, only redistributed.

**2.** The total charge in a closed system is [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the gravitational acceleration *g* or the speed of light *c*.]].
:::

**a) many excess electrons does the negative sphere have?**
**b) large is their final charge?**

::: handskrift
typ: metallkulor

::: textlosning
**a)** We divide the total charge *Q* by the charge of the electron
$q_e$ to get the number of electrons *n*. To make the calculation easier
we ignore the sign of the charge.

$$
n = \frac{Q}{q_e}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
Q = 3.0\ \mathrm{nC} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C} \\
q_e \approx 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
n = \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}} = 1.872\ldots \cdot 10^{10}
$$

**Answer:** 1.9 · 10¹⁰ or 19 billion (a lot!)

**b)** Since both spheres are equally large and conducting, the charges will
spread out evenly between them. Their final charge *Q* is given by
the mean of their original charges.

$$
Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
Q_1 = +5.0\ \mathrm{nC} \\
Q_2 = -3.0\ \mathrm{nC}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
Q = \frac{+5.0 + (-3.0)}{2} = \frac{+2.0}{2} = +1.0\ \mathrm{nC}
$$

**Answer:** +1.0 nC
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Charge"
- **Charge** is an excess or deficit of electrons. Written $Q$
  (large) or $q$ (small), with the unit C (coulomb).
- An **excess** of electrons gives a **negative** charge, a **deficit** gives a
  **positive** one, and as many as the protons gives **neutral**.
- The protons are fixed. It is the mobile **electrons** that
  determine the charge.
:::

::: sampunkt "Forces between charges"
- **Like** charges **repel**, that is push away.
- **Opposite** charges **attract**, that is pull towards each other.
- Charges tend to spread out evenly, precisely because like
  charges push each other away.
:::

::: sampunkt "Conductor, insulator, semiconductor"
- **Conductor**: the electrons move easily. Metals, salt solutions.
- **Insulator**: the electrons are tightly bound. Glass, plastic, wood.
- **Semiconductor**: insulator at low temperature, conductor at high. Silicon,
  selenium.
:::

::: sampunkt "Electrostatic induction"
- **Induction** is the redistribution of charges caused by an external
  electric field.
- That is why even **neutral** objects are attracted to a charged rod: paper,
  a stream of water, an aluminium can.
- **In a conductor** the electrons are pushed away, so the nearest end gets
  the opposite charge.
- **In an insulator** molecular **dipoles** rotate instead.
- The attraction always wins, since the opposite charges end up
  **closer** than the like ones.
:::

::: sampunkt "The elementary charge"
- $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, the smallest unit of charge.
- The electron has it as negative, the proton as positive, and the neutron has $0\ \mathrm{C}$.
- Number of excess electrons: $n = \dfrac{Q}{e}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Conservation of charge"
- Charges cannot be created or destroyed, only **redistributed**.
- The total charge in a closed system is **constant**.
- Two equally large spheres that touch each other therefore share the charge evenly:
  $+5.0$ nC and $-3.0$ nC become $+1.0$ nC each.
:::
:::
