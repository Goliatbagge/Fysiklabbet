# Electric current

::: tips "Vocabulary: electrodynamics and electrostatics"
The study of charges at rest is called *electrostatics*. The study of charges in
motion, which we are now going to look at, is called *electrodynamics*.
:::

The charge, usually in the form of electrons, that passes through a cross-section in
a certain time is called the **electric [[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]]**, written *I*, and has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] A
(ampere). So to get a current we need mobile charges and an
[[electric field|The region around a charge where another charge is acted on by an electric force.]] that sets them in motion. An electric field is obtained by
connecting a conductor to a voltage source, for example a battery, which
generates the field in the conductor.

The direction of the current is defined as the same direction as the electric field,
which in turn is defined from plus to minus (this is because
early experiments used positive hydrogen nuclei, which moved from plus to
minus). This means that the direction of the current is **opposite** to the
direction of the electrons. **So the direction of the current is drawn from the positive terminal to the negative terminal.**

::: formel "Electric current"
$$
I = \frac{Q}{t}
$$

where

- *I* = current (A)
- *Q* = charge (C)
- *t* = time (s)
:::

::: kuriosa "The speed of electrons"
The drift velocity of the electrons is about 0.1 mm/s (slow), but the electric
field spreads at the [[speed|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of light and gets all the electrons [[moving|The magnitude of velocity, without direction (a scalar). Measured in m/s or km/h.]]
almost at the same time.
:::

The charge in a battery is often given in Ah (ampere-hours). It
can therefore be useful to be able to switch between Ah and the SI unit C (coulomb).

::: formel "Other units of charge"
- 1 As (ampere-second) = 1 C
- 1 Ah (ampere-hour) = 3 600 C **(NOTE! Not in the formula sheet.)**

::: härledning "Derivation: units of charge"
We already know that the SI unit of charge is C (coulomb). If we
solve for the charge *Q* from the formula for electric current, we get

$$
Q = I \cdot t
$$

If we do a unit analysis, we see that charge expressed in base units is

$$
Q = I \cdot t = [1\ \mathrm{A}] \cdot [1\ \mathrm{s}] = [1\ \mathrm{As}] = [1\ \mathrm{C}]
$$

So **1 As (ampere-second) = 1 C**.

If we want to convert a charge in Ah to C, we do the conversion below.

$$
[1\ \mathrm{Ah}] = [1\ \mathrm{A}] \cdot [1\ \mathrm{h}]
= [1\ \mathrm{A}] \cdot [3\,600\ \mathrm{s}]
= [3\,600\ \mathrm{As}] = [3\,600\ \mathrm{C}]
$$

So **1 Ah = 3 600 C**.
:::
:::

Currents can never "get stuck" in a circuit; they have an even flow into and out
of a point, which is summarised in **Kirchhoff's first law**.

::: formel "Kirchhoff's first law"
The sum of all currents into a point is equal to the sum of all
currents out of the same point.

$$
\sum I_\text{in} = \sum I_\text{out}
$$
:::

At a junction the sum of the currents in is equal to the sum out.
:::

A conductor that carries all the current is called the **main line**. A conductor that has
branched off from a main line is called a **branch**.

::: kuriosa "Current and danger"
| Current | Effect on the body |
| --- | --- |
| 0–1 mA | Barely noticeable |
| 1–10 mA | The hands tremble, hard to let go |
| 10–40 mA | Painful cramp and raised blood pressure |
| 40–400 mA | Severe shock, disturbances of the heart |
| > 400 mA | Cardiac arrest and burns |
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Number of electrons per second"
**How many electrons pass through a cross-section every second when the current is
0.24 A?**

::: handskrift
typ: elektronflode
:::

::: textlosning
We start by calculating the total charge that passes every second.

$$
I = \frac{Q}{t} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Q = I \cdot t
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
I = 0.24\ \mathrm{A} \\
t = 1.00\ \mathrm{s}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
Q = 0.24 \cdot 1.00 = 0.24\ \mathrm{C}
$$

The number of electrons *n* is obtained by dividing the total charge *Q* by
the charge of one electron $q_e$.

$$
n = \frac{Q}{q_e}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
Q = 0.24\ \mathrm{C} \\
q_e \approx 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
n = \frac{0.24}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}} = 1.498\ldots \cdot 10^{18}
\approx 1.5 \cdot 10^{18}
$$

**Answer:** 1.5 · 10¹⁸ electrons
:::
:::

*I*₁ in from the left, *I*₂ out to the right, *I*₃ down at the junction.
:::

::: handskrift
typ: kirchhoff
:::

::: textlosning
According to Kirchhoff's first law, the sum of the currents into the point must equal the sum of
the currents out of the point. We
set up that relationship and solve for the current $I_2$.

$$
I_1 = I_2 + I_3 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad I_2 = I_1 - I_3
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
I_1 = 0.25\ \mathrm{A} \\
I_3 = 0.08\ \mathrm{A}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
I_2 = 0.25 - 0.08 = 0.17\ \mathrm{A}
$$

**Answer:** 0.17 A
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Electric current"
- The charge that passes through a cross-section per unit time.
- $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$, with the unit A (ampere).
- Both **mobile charges** and an **electric field** that sets
  them moving are needed, that is a voltage source.
:::

::: sampunkt "The direction of the current"
- Always drawn **from the positive terminal to the negative terminal**, the same direction as the
  electric field.
- So the electrons move **in the opposite direction**.
- Historical reason: early experiments used positive hydrogen nuclei.
:::

::: sampunkt "The speed of the electrons"
- The drift velocity of the electrons is only about
  $0.1\ \mathrm{mm/s}$.
- Yet the lamp lights up immediately: the **field** spreads at the speed of
  light and gets all the electrons moving almost at the same time.
:::

::: sampunkt "Units of charge"
- $1\ \mathrm{As} = 1\ \mathrm{C}$
- $1\ \mathrm{Ah} = 3\,600\ \mathrm{C}$, useful for batteries.
- The ampere-hour is not in the formula sheet, so it is worth remembering.
:::

::: sampunkt "Kirchhoff's first law"
- $\sum I_\text{in} = \sum I_\text{out}$
- Current can never collect or get stuck at a point.
- **Main line**: where all the current flows. **Branch**: a line branching off from
  the main line.
:::

::: sampunkt "Current and the body"
- Just a few milliamperes make the hands tremble and make it hard to
  let go.
- Over $400\ \mathrm{mA}$ gives cardiac arrest and burns. It is the **current**, not
  the voltage, that is dangerous.
:::
:::
