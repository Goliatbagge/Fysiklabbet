# Perpendicular lines

::: härledning "Extension"
This section is an **extension**. Perpendicular lines are not part of the
core content of Mathematics level 1c, but everything builds on things you
already know: the *k*-value and the slope step. The relationship returns in
later courses, where, among other things, you look for the line that is perpendicular to
a curve.
:::

Two lines that intersect at a **right angle**, that is 90°, are called
**perpendicular**. The squares on squared paper, the corners of a phone screen and two
streets that cross each other straight are examples of perpendicular lines.

In the previous section we saw that parallel lines have the same *k*-value. Perpendicular
lines also have a simple relationship between their *k*-values. Look at
the lines in the figure below.

The blue line $y = 2x - 1$ has *k*-value 2, and the green line
$y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 4$ has *k*-value $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The lines intersect
at the point (2, 3), and the angle between them is a right angle. If we multiply
the two *k*-values we get

$$
2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -1
$$

That is no coincidence. The product of the *k*-values is $-1$ for **every** pair of
perpendicular lines, as long as neither of them is horizontal or vertical.

## Why is the product −1?

The answer lies in the slope step. The blue line goes 2 steps up for every
step to the right. If we rotate the whole figure a quarter turn anticlockwise about
the point of intersection, the blue line lands exactly on the green one, and the step
follows along in the rotation:

- the step **1 to the right** becomes **1 step up**
- the step **2 up** becomes **2 steps to the left**

So the green line goes 2 steps to the left when it goes 1 step up. To the
left is the negative direction in the *x*-direction, so $\Delta x = -2$ and

$$
k_2 = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{-2} = -\frac{1}{2}
$$

The two parts of the step have **swapped places**, and one of them has **changed sign**.
This holds however steep the blue line is, and that is why the product
is always $-1$.

::: formel "Perpendicular lines"
$$
k_1 \cdot k_2 = -1
$$

where $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the gradients of the two lines.

::: härledning "Derivation: the product of the *k*-values"
The first line has the slope step 1 step to the right and $k_1$ steps up.
When the step is rotated a quarter turn anticlockwise, the step to the right becomes a step
up, and the step up becomes a step to the left. The perpendicular line therefore goes
$k_1$ steps to the left, $\Delta x = -k_1$, when it goes 1 step up,
$\Delta y = 1$. This gives

$$
k_2 = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{-k_1} = -\frac{1}{k_1}
$$

Multiplying both sides by $k_1$ gives

$$
k_1 \cdot k_2 = k_1 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{k_1}\right) = -1
$$

We have divided by $k_1$, so the derivation requires that $k_1 \neq 0$, that is,
that the first line is not horizontal.
:::
:::

## The *k*-value of the perpendicular line

The derivation shows how to go from the *k*-value of one line to that of the
other:

$$
k_2 = -\frac{1}{k_1}
$$

In words: **turn the fraction upside down and change the sign**. A whole number is a fraction
with denominator 1, for example $2 = \dfrac{2}{1}$, so a line that is
perpendicular to a line with $k = 2$ has $k = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.

| $k_1$ | $2$ | $-3$ | $\dfrac{2}{3}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $k_2$ | $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{3}$ | $-\dfrac{3}{2}$ | $4$ | $-1$ |

Notice that one line always has a positive *k*-value and the
other a negative one: one line slopes upwards and the other downwards.

::: tips "The exception: horizontal and vertical"
A horizontal line, $y = b$, and a vertical line, $x = a$, are also
perpendicular. But the rule $k_1 \cdot k_2 = -1$ cannot be used for them:
the horizontal line has $k = 0$, and 0 times a number is never $-1$. The
vertical line has no *k*-value at all. Try making the blue line
horizontal in the simulation.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Are the lines perpendicular?"
**Decide whether the lines are perpendicular.<br>a)&nbsp;$y = 3x + 1$ and $y = -\dfrac{1}{3}x + 5$&emsp;&emsp;b)&nbsp;$y = 4x - 2$ and $y = -4x + 1$**

::: handskrift
typ: vinkelkoll
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The *k*-value is the number in front of $x$, so $k_1 = 3$ and
$k_2 = -\dfrac{1}{3}$. We multiply the *k*-values:

$$
k_1 \cdot k_2 = 3 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{3}\right) = -1
$$

The product is $-1$, so the lines are perpendicular.

**Answer:** Yes, the lines are perpendicular.

**b)** Here $k_1 = 4$ and $k_2 = -4$. We multiply the *k*-values:

$$
k_1 \cdot k_2 = 4 \cdot (-4) = -16 \neq -1
$$

The product is not $-1$. The lines slope in different directions, but they do not
intersect at a right angle.

**Answer:** No, the lines are not perpendicular.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Perpendicular line through a point"
**Find the equation of the line that is perpendicular to $y = 2x + 3$ and passes
through the point (4, 1).**

::: handskrift
typ: vinkelratlinje
:::

::: textlosning
The line we are looking for is perpendicular to $y = 2x + 3$, so the product of the
*k*-values is $-1$:

$$
k_1 \cdot k_2 = -1
$$

The given line has $k_1 = 2$. Substitution gives

$$
2 \cdot k_2 = -1
$$

Divide both sides by 2:

$$
\frac{2 \cdot k_2}{2} = \frac{-1}{2}
$$

$$
k_2 = -\frac{1}{2}
$$

Substituting $k = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ into RLE gives

$$
y = -\frac{1}{2}x + m
$$

Since (4, 1) lies on the line,

$$
1 = -\frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 + m
$$

$$
1 = -2 + m
$$

Add 2 to both sides:

$$
1 + 2 = -2 + m + 2
$$

$$
3 = m
$$

Substituting $k = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $m = 3$ into RLE gives
$y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 3$.

**Answer:** $y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 3$
:::
:::

::: textlosning
The angle at A is a right angle if the sides AB and AC are perpendicular. We find
their *k*-values with the formula
$k = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$, with A as point 1:

$$
k_\mathrm{AB} = \frac{3 - 1}{7 - 3} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

$$
k_\mathrm{AC} = \frac{5 - 1}{1 - 3} = \frac{4}{-2} = -2
$$

The product of the *k*-values is

$$
k_\mathrm{AB} \cdot k_\mathrm{AC} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot (-2) = -1
$$

The product is $-1$, so the sides AB and AC are perpendicular. The angle at A is
therefore a right angle.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Perpendicular lines"
- Two lines that intersect at a **right angle**, 90°.
- The product of the *k*-values is $-1$: $k_1 \cdot k_2 = -1$.
- One line slopes upwards and the other downwards.
:::

::: sampunkt "Why the product is −1"
- The step 1 to the right and $k_1$ up is rotated a quarter turn.
- It becomes 1 step up and $k_1$ steps to the left.
- $k_2 = \dfrac{1}{-k_1} = -\dfrac{1}{k_1}$
:::

::: sampunkt "Turn over and change the sign"
- $k_2 = -\dfrac{1}{k_1}$: turn the fraction upside down and change the sign.
- $k_1 = 2$ gives $k_2 = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, and $k_1 = -\dfrac{2}{3}$ gives
  $k_2 = \dfrac{3}{2}$.
- Exception: a horizontal and a vertical line are perpendicular but do not have
  such *k*-values.
:::

::: sampunkt "Perpendicular line through a point"
- Find $k_2$ from $k_1 \cdot k_2 = -1$.
- Substitute the point into $y = k_2 x + m$ and solve for $m$.
- Right angle in a figure: show that the *k*-values of the sides have the product $-1$.
:::
