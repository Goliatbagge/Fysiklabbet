# Sound frequency and the Doppler effect

The number of oscillations the particles in a sound wave make each second is called
the **sound frequency**. The higher the sound frequency, the higher the pitch. The human
ear can perceive frequencies between about **20 Hz and 20 000 Hz**.

Sound **above 20 000 Hz** is called **ultrasound**, which is used in, for example,
prenatal scanning and kidney stone treatment. Bats use
ultrasound to find their way in the dark and to locate insects
(**echolocation**). Ultrasound can only bounce off objects that are larger
than the [[wavelength|The distance between two crests (or other identical points) in a wave. Denoted *λ*. SI unit metre (m).]] of the sound.

Sound **below 20 Hz** is called **infrasound**. It is generated by, for example,
earthquakes and explosions, but also by fans in ventilation systems.

The human ear hears different frequencies differently well. At very low or
high frequencies close to the limits of hearing, a high sound level is needed for us
to perceive the sound. Frequencies around **4 000 Hz** are perceived as the loudest
even at the same volume, since our ear is most sensitive to these frequencies.

::: kuriosa "Human hearing"
The human ear is not linear: we do not perceive all frequencies
equally strongly even if the loudness (decibels) is the same.

**The "golden" interval:** We are by far most sensitive in the range between
**2 000 Hz and 4 000 Hz**. In this interval our threshold of hearing is at its lowest.
A sound here can be extremely faint (close to 0 dB, or even just below)
and we still register it.

Our **ear canal** is a tube that is closed at one end (at the eardrum).
This tube creates a natural **resonance** that amplifies sound waves precisely
in the interval 2 500–4 000 Hz. The ear therefore works as a passive
amplifier for exactly these frequencies.

**Why has evolution "tuned" our ears to exactly these frequencies?**
It comes down to two main factors: **speech** and **warning**.

**Human speech and consonants** is the most important factor:

- **Vowels (a, o, u)** are lower in frequency (250–1 000 Hz) and carry
  the *strength* of the sound.
- **Consonants (s, f, t, k, p, sh sounds)** are higher, often in the range
  **2 000–8 000 Hz**, and it is these that make speech understandable. If you
  hear someone shout but do not catch the treble, you cannot tell whether
  they said "sat", "cat" or "gnat".
- Conclusion: we hear best where the **clarity** and information of speech lie,
  not where the strength of speech lies.

**In evolutionary terms** it has also been vital to detect dangers
or prey before you see them:

- **Rustling and cracking:** the sound of a twig snapping or dry leaves
  rustling contains high-frequency components within our most sensitive
  range.
- **A baby crying:** an infant's scream is designed by nature to be
  impossible to ignore. The most piercing parts are often around
  **3 000 Hz**, exactly where the parent's ear is most sensitive.
:::

## The Doppler effect

When **the source and the receiver move relative to each other**, the
frequency changes. This phenomenon is called **the Doppler effect**. An
observer **in front of** an object moving at high [[velocity|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]], for example an
ambulance, perceives the frequency of the siren as **higher** than an observer
**behind** it.

## Supersonic speed

If an object, for example a fighter jet, exceeds the speed of
sound, it will "catch up with" its own sound waves and reinforce
them. A large **sound pressure** builds up, which an observer outside perceives
as a **"sonic boom"**. The pilots inside the plane, however, will not perceive
any boom, since the sound cannot catch up with the plane.

::: tips "Where does the sonic boom happen?"
The sonic boom does **not only happen at the moment** an object breaks
the sound barrier: it follows the object for as long as it moves faster than
sound. The cone of compressed sound waves (the Mach cone) sweeps across the ground
as the plane passes, and everyone under this cone hears the sonic boom in
turn.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Sound frequency and pitch"
- More oscillations per second means a **higher** pitch.
- The human ear hears roughly
  $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Ultrasound and infrasound"
- **Ultrasound**, above $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$: prenatal scanning,
  kidney stone treatment, bats' echolocation.
- Ultrasound only bounces off objects that are **larger than the wavelength**.
- **Infrasound**, below $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$: earthquakes, explosions,
  ventilation fans.
:::

::: sampunkt "The ear is not linear"
- We are most sensitive between about
  $2\,000$ and $4\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
- The ear canal is a tube that is closed at the eardrum and gives
  **resonance** in exactly that interval.
- That is where the **consonants** of speech, which carry the clarity, lie, and an
  infant's scream.
:::

::: sampunkt "The Doppler effect"
- If the source and the receiver move relative to each other, **the
  frequency changes**.
- **In front of** the approaching ambulance a **higher** pitch is heard.
- **Behind** it a **lower** pitch is heard.
- The waves are squeezed together ahead and stretched apart behind.
:::

::: sampunkt "Supersonic speed"
- If the object exceeds the speed of sound, it catches up with its own sound waves,
  which reinforce each other.
- The large sound pressure is perceived as a **sonic boom**.
- **The pilot hears no boom**, since the sound never catches up with the plane.
- The boom does not only happen at the moment of breaking through: the **Mach cone** follows
  the plane and sweeps across the ground.
:::
:::
