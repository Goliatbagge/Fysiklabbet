# Radioactive decay

When certain atomic nuclei become too large, the strong nuclear force cannot
hold them together. The nuclei then become unstable and decay into
nuclei with lower energy states. These unstable nuclei are called
**radioactive**, and they undergo **radioactive decay**.

In the decay, energy is given off in the form of radiation that is so energetic that
it knocks electrons out of other atoms, so-called **ionising
radiation**.

Before we get to the decays, we first have to get to know two new
particles:

A **positron** is denoted e⁺ and has the same mass as an electron, but is
positively charged. The positron is the electron's **antiparticle**. If an e⁻ and
an e⁺ react with each other, they are destroyed (annihilated) and become energy
(two [[photons|A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Carries energy *E* = *h* · *f*, where *h* is Planck's constant.]]).

A **neutrino** is denoted $\nu$ (the small Greek letter "nu"). It
has no charge and an extremely small mass, less than a millionth of
the electron's. It moves at close to the [[speed|Change of position per unit time, with direction (a vector). SI unit m/s.]] of light and hardly interacts
at all with matter. If ten trillion (10¹³) neutrinos were fired at
the Earth, one (!) would be absorbed. The rest would pass straight through.
Neutrinos are common particles that are everywhere. 100 billion neutrinos
pass through your thumbnail every second!

There are different types of decay. In this subject you should be familiar
with three of them: alpha, beta and gamma decay.

::: formel "Decay equations"
Decay equations are balanced in the same way as equations in mathematics.
The decay is written with a right arrow, →, where the sum of the mass numbers and
of the atomic numbers must be the same on both sides of the arrow.

| Particle | electron | positron | neutron | proton | photon | neutrino |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Symbol | ${_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e}$ | ${_1^0} \mathrm{e}$ | ${_0^1} \mathrm{n}$ | ${_1^1} \mathrm{p}$ | $\gamma$ | $\nu$ |
:::

In **alpha/α decay**, a helium nucleus $_2^4 \mathrm{He}$ is emitted.

::: formel "α decay"
$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-2}^{A-4}} \mathrm{Y} + {_2^4} \mathrm{He}
$$

where

- *A* = [[mass number|The total number of protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus. Denoted *A*.]]/number of neutrons + protons
- *Z* = [[atomic number|The number of protons in an atomic nucleus. Determines which element it is. Denoted *Z*.]]/number of protons
:::

In **beta/β decay**, the number of nucleons (protons and neutrons) is
[[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]], but they are redistributed. There are two types: β⁻ decay and
β⁺ decay.

In β⁻ decay, a neutron is transformed and becomes a proton, an electron and
a neutrino. In β⁺ decay, a proton is transformed and becomes a neutron, a
positron and a neutrino. The electron or positron that is emitted
is called a **beta particle**.

::: formel "β decay"
**β⁻ decay:**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z+1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} + \nu
$$

**β⁺ decay:**

$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_1^0} \mathrm{e} + \nu
$$

Strictly speaking, an **antineutrino** ($\bar{\nu}$) is emitted in β⁻ decay
and a neutrino in β⁺ decay. In the formulas we write $\nu$
for both, for simplicity.
:::

A close relative of β⁺ decay is **electron capture**: the nucleus
captures one of the atom's own electrons, usually from the innermost shell,
and a proton is transformed into a neutron while a neutrino is
emitted. The result is the same as in β⁺ decay, the atomic number decreases by
1, but no positron leaves the nucleus.

::: formel "Electron capture"
$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X} + {_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} \rightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + \nu
$$
:::

In **gamma/γ decay**, there is no change in the nucleons, but
electromagnetic radiation in the form of photons is emitted when the **atomic nucleus**
is de-excited, that is goes from an excited state to a lower
energy state. The nucleus often ends up in such an excited state
directly after an alpha or beta decay. Note that it is the nucleus,
not the atom's electrons, that changes energy level: when the electrons jump
between their levels, light is emitted (section 9.1); when the nucleus does it,
gamma radiation is emitted.

::: formel "γ decay"
$$
_Z^A \mathrm{X}^* \rightarrow {_Z^A} \mathrm{X} + \gamma
$$

where $_Z^A \mathrm{X}^*$ is an excited state of $_Z^A \mathrm{X}$.
:::

::: demo "Decay in a cloud chamber"
We place the radioactive sample thorium (an alpha emitter) in a cooled
chamber with propanol and in this way form a **cloud chamber**. When
decays occur in the sample, we see them as tracks in
the mist.
:::

::: exempel "Example 1: Write decay equations"
**Write the decay equation for**

**a) Radium-226, which undergoes α decay.**
**b) Carbon-14, which undergoes β⁻ decay.**
**c) Aluminium-25, which undergoes β⁺ decay.**
**d) Cobalt-60, which undergoes γ decay when it is de-excited from 1.33 MeV to 1.17 MeV.**
**e) Cobalt-59, which absorbs a neutron and then decays to nickel-60.**

::: handskrift
typ: sonderfallsformler
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** Radium has atomic number 88. That it undergoes α decay means that it
emits a helium nucleus.

**Answer:**

$$
_{88}^{226} \mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow {_2^4} \mathrm{He} + {_{86}^{222}} \mathrm{Rn}
$$

**b)** Carbon has atomic number 6. In β⁻ decay the atomic number increases by 1.

**Answer:**

$$
_6^{14} \mathrm{C} \rightarrow {_7^{14}} \mathrm{N} + {_{-1}^0} \mathrm{e} + \nu
$$

**c)** Aluminium has atomic number 13. In β⁺ decay the atomic number decreases
by 1.

**Answer:**

$$
_{13}^{25} \mathrm{Al} \rightarrow {_{12}^{25}} \mathrm{Mg} + {_1^0} \mathrm{e} + \nu
$$

**d)** In γ decay there is no change in the nucleons, only
the emission of a photon. The photon gets the difference in energy, that is
(1.33 − 1.17) MeV = 0.16 MeV.

**Answer:**

$$
^{60}\mathrm{Co}\ (1.33\ \mathrm{MeV}) \rightarrow {^{60}\mathrm{Co}}\ (1.17\ \mathrm{MeV}) + \gamma\ (0.16\ \mathrm{MeV})
$$

**e)** We write down what we know about the decay and balance it.

$$
_{27}^{59} \mathrm{Co} + {_0^1} \mathrm{n} \rightarrow {_{28}^{60}} \mathrm{Ni} + {_{-1}^0} \mathrm{e}
$$

**Answer:** As above.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: Energy in the β⁺ decay of nitrogen-13"
**Nitrogen-13 undergoes β⁺ decay. Determine the energy released in the decay.**

::: handskrift
typ: betaenergi
:::

::: textlosning
In β decay the number of electrons changes, so we must take into account
the masses of the electrons in the nuclide masses (this does not apply to α or
γ decay).

We start by writing the equation for the decay.

$$
_7^{13} \mathrm{N} \rightarrow {_6^{13}} \mathrm{C} + {_1^0} \mathrm{e} + \nu
$$

We calculate the total mass on the left-hand side, that is the mass of the nucleus
$_7^{13} \mathrm{N}$. We read off the mass of the nuclide (the atom including
electrons) from a table and then subtract the mass of the electrons.

$$
m_\text{left} = 13.005739 - 7 \cdot 0.000549 = 13.001896\ \mathrm{u}
$$

We then calculate the total mass on the right-hand side, that is the mass of the nucleus
$_6^{13} \mathrm{C}$ and of a positron (the same as the electron). The neutrino
has negligible mass.

$$
m_\text{right} = (13.003355 - 6 \cdot 0.000549) + 0.000549 = 13.000610\ \mathrm{u}
$$

The [[mass defect|The difference between the mass of an atomic nucleus and the sum of the masses of its building blocks. Corresponds to the binding energy via *E* = *m* · *c*².]] $\Delta m$ gives the released energy.

$$
\Delta m = m_\text{left} - m_\text{right}
= 13.001896 - 13.000610 = 0.001286\ \mathrm{u}
$$

$$
E = 0.001286 \cdot 931.49\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.197\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV}
\approx 1.20\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

**Answer:** 1.20 MeV
:::
:::

::: kuriosa "Detecting the neutrino"
One of the most successful methods for detecting neutrinos makes use
of what is called **Cherenkov radiation**. When a neutrino
interacts with an atomic nucleus in water, it can create a secondary particle
(for example an electron) that moves faster than the speed of light in
water. This phenomenon causes a kind of light shock wave, similar to the sonic boom
from a supersonic aircraft, but in the form of visible light. This light can
then be detected by photomultiplier tubes that line the inside of a
gigantic water tank deep underground or under water. One example of
such a facility is Super-Kamiokande in Japan.
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Radioactive decay"
- If the nucleus becomes too large, the strong nuclear force is not enough. The nucleus
  becomes **unstable** and decays to a lower energy state.
- The radiation is so energetic that it knocks electrons out of other atoms.
  It is therefore called **ionising**.
:::

::: sampunkt "Two new particles"
- **Positron** (e⁺): the electron's antiparticle, the same mass but positive.
  If it meets an electron, both are **annihilated** into two photons.
- **Neutrino** ($\nu$): has no charge, an extremely small mass and
  hardly interacts at all. Of ten trillion fired at the Earth, about
  one is absorbed.
:::

::: sampunkt "Alpha decay"
- A helium nucleus is emitted.
- $_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-2}^{A-4}} \mathrm{Y} + {_2^4} \mathrm{He}$
- The mass number decreases by 4 and the atomic number by 2.
:::

::: sampunkt "Beta decay"
- The number of nucleons is **constant**; they are only redistributed. The mass number $A$
  therefore does not change. The emitted electron or positron is called
  a **beta particle**.
- $\beta^-$: $_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z+1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_{-1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} + \nu$
- $\beta^+$: $_Z^A \mathrm{X} \rightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}} \mathrm{Y} + {_{1}^{0}} \mathrm{e} + \nu$
- **Electron capture**: the nucleus captures one of its own electrons, the same
  result as $\beta^+$ but no positron is emitted.
:::

::: sampunkt "Gamma decay"
- The nucleons do **not change at all**. An excited **nucleus** emits
  a photon when it goes down to a lower energy state.
- $_Z^A \mathrm{X}^* \rightarrow {_Z^A} \mathrm{X} + \gamma$
:::

::: sampunkt "Balancing the equation"
- The sum of the **mass numbers** must be the same on both sides of the arrow.
- The sum of the **atomic numbers** must also be the same.
- Just like balancing an equation in mathematics.
:::
:::
