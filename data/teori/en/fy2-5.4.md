# The colour of the Sun

Why is the Sun **white** in the middle of the day but **glowing red** at sunset?
And why is **the sky blue**? Both phenomena have the same cause:
**Rayleigh scattering** of sunlight in the Earth's atmosphere.

## The Sun's "true" colour

The Sun's surface has a [[temperature|A measure of how fast the atoms/molecules in a substance move. SI unit kelvin (K).]] of about **5 800 K** and radiates, to a very
good approximation, as a **black body** (see section 4.3). According to Wien's
displacement law, the Sun's spectrum has its maximum at about 500 nm,
in the middle of the visible range, between blue and green (cyan). Since the Sun
at the same time emits all wavelengths in the visible spectrum, the
light is perceived as **white** when we look at it from outside the atmosphere (for
example from the International Space Station, ISS).

## Rayleigh scattering

When sunlight passes through the Earth's atmosphere, the photons collide with
the molecules of the air (mostly nitrogen and oxygen). Short wavelengths (blue light)
are scattered **much more** than long wavelengths (red light). The probability
that a [[photon|A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. Carries the energy *E* = *h* · *f*, where *h* is Planck's constant.]] is scattered is roughly proportional to $1/\lambda^{4}$:

::: formel "The wavelength dependence of Rayleigh scattering"
$$
\sigma \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}
$$

where

- *σ* = probability/intensity of scattering
- *λ* = [[wavelength|The distance between two crests (or other identical points) in a wave. Denoted *λ*. SI unit metre (m).]] of the light (m)
:::

Since *λ* appears to the fourth power, the scattering becomes very different
for different colours:

::: tips "How much is the light scattered?"
- **Violet light** ($\lambda \approx 400$ nm) is scattered about **10 times
  more** than red light ($\lambda \approx 700$ nm), since
  $(700/400)^{4} \approx 9.4$.
- **Blue light** ($\lambda \approx 450$ nm) is scattered about **6 times
  more** than red light.
:::

## That is why the sky is blue

The blue light that is scattered out of the direct sunbeam has to go
somewhere: it reaches us from **the whole sky** instead of from the Sun.
That is why we see a blue sky in almost every direction during the day. The fact that we
do not see violet (which is scattered even more) is because the Sun emits
less violet light to begin with, and our eyes are more sensitive to
blue.

## That is why the Sun turns red at sunset

When the Sun is **high** in the sky, the light passes through about **one
air mass** of atmosphere. A moderate number of blue photons is then scattered out of the
direct beam, not enough to noticeably colour the Sun.

When the Sun approaches the **horizon**, however, the light has to cross **much
more** air: up to about **38 air masses**! Over such a long path
almost all the blue and much of the green is scattered away, while the **red light
gets through**. The direct sunbeam then becomes orange, red or
dark red, and the same thing happens to the Moon near the horizon.

## How the colour is calculated

In the simulation we start from the Sun's spectrum approximated as
**[[black-body radiation|The thermal radiation emitted by an ideal absorbing and emitting object, with a spectrum that depends only on temperature.]] at 5 778 K**. For each wavelength in the interval
380–720 nm we calculate how large a fraction survives the atmosphere:

$$
T(\lambda) = e^{-\tau(\lambda) \cdot M}
$$

where $\tau(\lambda) \propto 1/\lambda^{4}$ is the optical thickness of the atmosphere
and *M* is the air mass (1 at the zenith, up to about 38 at the horizon). The
**filtered spectrum** is then integrated against the eye's CIE
1931 colour matching functions to obtain the colour we would actually
see. The result is the Sun's real colour, not an artistic interpretation.

::: kuriosa "Why is the sunset not always equally red?"
The colour at sunset also depends on:

- **Dust and water droplets** in the air: more particles give a deeper red.
- **Volcanic eruptions**: can give spectacularly red sunsets for months
  afterwards, since dust is thrown up into high layers of the atmosphere.
- **Air pollution** and haze: can both strengthen and dampen the colour.

On high mountains, where the atmosphere is thinner, the Sun is whiter even when it
is low in the sky.
:::
