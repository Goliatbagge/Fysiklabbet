# Applications of the chain rule

We look at a new way of writing the chain rule using Leibniz
notation, which makes it easier to solve problems where several quantities change
at the same time.

We already have the chain rule, which says that if $y = f(g(x))$ then

$$
y' = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)
$$

If we give the inner function its own name, for example $z$, we can write
this as

$$
y' = f'(z)\cdot z'(x)
$$

In Leibniz notation the same thing is written as

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dz}\cdot \frac{dz}{dx}
$$

So we first differentiate the outer function $y$ with respect to $z$, and
then the inner function $z$ with respect to $x$.

::: formel "The chain rule in another form"
If $y = f(z)$ and $z = g(x)$, then
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dz}\cdot \frac{dz}{dx}
$$
:::

To remember the different parts, you can see it as a "fraction". If we
cancel the $dz$ terms on the right-hand side, we see that it becomes the same thing as the
left-hand side.

## Related rates of change

The notation is particularly useful in applications where two quantities change
with time. If we know one rate of change, we can often find
the other by linking them with the chain rule.

::: exempel "Example 1: A growing oil slick"
**An oil tanker is leaking oil into the sea, where the oil spreads out in a circular shape
on the water surface. The radius of the oil slick grows at the constant rate
$0.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$. How fast is the area $A$ of the oil slick increasing at the moment
when the radius is $10\ \mathrm{m}$?**

::: handskrift
typ: oljeflack
:::

::: textlosning
Wanted: how the area changes per unit of time, that is $\dfrac{dA}{dt}$.

Given: $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 0.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $r = 10\ \mathrm{m}$.

We set up the chain rule:

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dr}\cdot \frac{dr}{dt}
$$

Here $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 0.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and the area of a circle is
$A(r) = \pi r^2$, which gives

$$
\frac{dA}{dr} = 2\pi r = 2\pi\cdot 10\ \mathrm{m} = 20\pi\ \mathrm{m}
$$

Substituted into the chain rule:

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = 20\pi\cdot 0.5 = 10\pi = 31.415\ldots\ \mathrm{m^2/s} \approx 31\ \mathrm{m^2/s}
$$

**Answer:** The area is increasing by about $31\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$.
:::
:::

::: exempel "Example 2: A balloon being filled"
**A spherical balloon is being filled with helium. The gas flows into the balloon at
the constant rate of $6$ litres per minute. How fast is the balloon's
radius increasing at the moment when the radius is $2\ \mathrm{dm}$? (Hint: remember that
$1$ litre is the same thing as $1\ \mathrm{dm^3}$.)**

::: handskrift
typ: ballongradie
:::

::: textlosning
Wanted: how the balloon's radius changes with time, that is $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$.

Given: $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 6\ \mathrm{litres/minute} = 6\ \mathrm{dm^3/minute}$ and
$r = 2\ \mathrm{dm}$.

We set up the chain rule and solve for $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$:

$$
\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{dV}{dr}\cdot \frac{dr}{dt}
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{\;\dfrac{dV}{dt}\;}{\;\dfrac{dV}{dr}\;}
$$

Here $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 6\ \mathrm{dm^3/minute}$, and the volume of a sphere is
$V(r) = \dfrac{4\pi r^3}{3}$, which gives

$$
\frac{dV}{dr} = \frac{3\cdot 4\pi r^2}{3} = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi\cdot (2\ \mathrm{dm})^2 = 16\pi\ \mathrm{dm^2}
$$

Substituted:

$$
\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{6}{16\pi} = 0.119\ldots\ \mathrm{dm/minute} \approx 0.12\ \mathrm{dm/minute} = 1.2\ \mathrm{cm/minute}
$$

**Answer:** The radius is increasing by about $1.2\ \mathrm{cm/minute}$.
:::
:::

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The chain rule in Leibniz form"
- If $y = f(z)$ and $z = g(x)$, then $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{dz} \cdot \dfrac{dz}{dx}$.
- Memory trick: $dz$ on the right-hand side "cancels", just as in a fraction.
- The form is particularly useful when several quantities change at the same time.
:::

::: sampunkt "Related rates of change"
- If you know one rate of change, you can find the other.
- The link between them is a formula, for example $A = \pi r^2$ or $V = \dfrac{4\pi r^3}{3}$.
:::

::: sampunkt "Method"
1. Write down what is **wanted** and what is **given**, with the right notation.
2. Set up the chain rule with the three quotients.
3. Differentiate the formula that links the quantities.
4. Substitute and solve for the wanted quotient.
:::

::: sampunkt "Typical examples"
- Oil slick: $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 0.5$ m/s and $\dfrac{dA}{dr} = 2\pi r$ give $\dfrac{dA}{dt} \approx 31\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ at $r = 10$ m.
- Balloon: $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 6\ \mathrm{dm^3/min}$ and $\dfrac{dV}{dr} = 4\pi r^2$ give $\dfrac{dr}{dt} \approx 1.2$ cm per minute.
- Keep track of the units. 1 litre is 1 dm³.
:::

:::
