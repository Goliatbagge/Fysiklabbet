# Induction and induced voltage (emf)

We have shown earlier that charges moving in a [[magnetic field|Region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where other magnetic objects are acted on by a force.]] are acted on
by a magnetic [[force|An influence that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit newton (N). A vector quantity.]]. This principle means that we can **generate a [[current|Amount of charge per unit time: *I* = *Q* / *t*. SI unit ampere (A).]]
by moving a conductor in a magnetic field**! Generating currents with the
help of magnetic fields is called **[[induction|When a changing magnetic field generates a voltage in a conductor. The basis of generators.]]**.

::: demo "Demonstration: Coil + galvanometer + bar magnet"
We connect a galvanometer (a sensitive ammeter) to a coil and
investigate how we can create currents with a magnet.

| Experiment                          | Result         |
|-------------------------------------|----------------|
| magnet through coil                 | → deflection   |
| reverse direction and repeat        | → deflection   |
| magnet at rest in coil              | → no deflection |
| pull magnet out                     | → deflection   |
| hold magnet still and move coil     | → deflection   |
:::

A current is produced **only when the magnet and the coil move relative to
each other**. To understand the principle we imagine a conductor being pulled through
a magnetic field.

When we pull the conductor downwards, the conduction electrons move in the magnetic field.
Using the right-hand rule we see that the magnetic force $F_m$ on the electrons
is **to the left** (remember: electrons moving downwards correspond to
a current upwards).

When electrons are pulled to the left, the left end of the conductor becomes **negatively
charged**. The right end gets a deficit of electrons and becomes
**positively charged**. So a **[[voltage|Energy difference per charge between two points. SI unit volt (V).]]** arises between the ends of the
conductor.

A voltage that arises when a conductor moves in a magnetic field is called an
**induced voltage** or **emf** (electromotive force),
is written *e* and, just like voltage, has the [[SI unit|The International System of Units, with seven base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).]] **V (volt)**.

The induced voltage gives an **opposing electric force** $F_e$ on
the electrons. When the electric force becomes as large as the magnetic
force (this happens quickly), $F_e = F_m$ and the motion of the electrons stops
because **force equilibrium** is reached.

::: formel "The law of induction (for a straight conductor)"
If *l*, *v* and *B* are perpendicular to each other,

$$
e = l \cdot v \cdot B
$$

where

- *e* = induced voltage (V)
- *l* = length of conductor (m)
- *v* = velocity of conductor (m/s)
- *B* = magnetic flux density (T)

::: härledning "Derivation: The law of induction"
We start from the conductor being pulled at [[constant|A value that does not change. For example the acceleration due to gravity *g* or the speed of light *c*.]] velocity *v*. At force equilibrium

$$
F_e = F_m \qquad (1)
$$

From an earlier topic we know that $F_e = q \cdot \mathbb{E}$, where $\mathbb{E}$
is the electric field strength, and from an earlier section in this topic
that $F_m = q \cdot v \cdot B$ (where *q* stands for the electron charge).
Substituting into (1) gives

$$
q \cdot \mathbb{E} = q \cdot v \cdot B
$$

Divide both sides by *q*:

$$
\mathbb{E} = v \cdot B \qquad (2)
$$

From an earlier topic we also know that the electric field strength is
$\mathbb{E} = \dfrac{U}{d}$. The voltage *U* corresponds to the induced
voltage *e*, and the distance *d* between the poles corresponds to the conductor's
length *l*. This means that $\mathbb{E} = \dfrac{e}{l}$. Substituting into (2)
gives

$$
\frac{e}{l} = v \cdot B
$$

We solve for the induced voltage *e* and get

$$
e = l \cdot v \cdot B
$$

Q.E.D.
:::
:::

::: textlosning
**a)** The direction of the field is **in/out** (perpendicular to the plane), the direction
of the conductor is **left/right** and the direction of the velocity is **up/down**.
All three are perpendicular to each other, so an emf is induced.

$$
e = l \cdot v \cdot B
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{l}
l = 20\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.20\ \mathrm{m} \\
v = 80\ \mathrm{cm/s} = 0.80\ \mathrm{m/s} \\
B = 0.85\ \mathrm{mT} = 0.85 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{T}
\end{array} \right]
$$

$$
e = 0.20 \cdot 0.80 \cdot 0.85 \cdot 10^{-3} = 1.36 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{V} = 0.136\ \mathrm{mV} \approx 0.14\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

**Answer:** 0.14 mV

**b)** The direction of the field is **in/out**, the direction of the conductor is **left/right**
and the direction of the velocity is also **left/right**. Since the direction of the conductor
and the direction of the velocity are **the same**, the electrons move
**along** the conductor and not across it. No emf is induced.

**Answer:** 0 V

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "Induction"
- Generating current with the help of magnetic fields.
- A voltage arises **only when the magnet and the coil move relative to
  each other**. If they are at rest nothing happens, however strong the field is.
- It does not matter which of them moves.
:::

::: sampunkt "What happens in the conductor"
- When the conductor is pulled through the field the conduction electrons move, and the
  magnetic force pushes them in one direction.
- One end becomes **negative** and the other **positive**, so a
  voltage arises.
- Remember: electrons moving downwards correspond to a **current upwards** in
  the right-hand rule.
:::

::: sampunkt "Emf"
- The voltage that arises is called an **induced voltage** or **emf**,
  electromotive force. Written $e$, with the unit V.
- $e = l \cdot v \cdot B$
- Applies when $l$, $v$ and $B$ are **perpendicular to each other**.
- If the conductor moves **along** the field, nothing at all is induced.
:::

::: sampunkt "Force equilibrium"
- The induced voltage gives an **electric force** $F_e$ on
  the electrons, opposite to the magnetic force.
- When $F_e = F_m$ the motion of the electrons stops. This happens almost immediately.
- It is from this equilibrium that the formula is derived.
:::
