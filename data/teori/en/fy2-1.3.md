# Stability

::: härledning "Extension"
This section is an **extension**. The content lies outside the core of the course, but anyone who
wants to know why things stand firmly, balance or topple over will find the answers
here. Everything builds on two concepts from the previous sections: the moment of a force
and the centre of gravity.
:::

Why does a low, wide stool stand firmly, while a tall, narrow bookcase
topples over as soon as it is tilted? And how can a structure of two forks balance on
the point of a needle without falling? The answers are about where the **centre of gravity** is in
relation to whatever supports the object.

## The base of support decides whether the object stays standing

The **base of support** is the area enclosed by the object's points of contact with the
surface. A box on the floor has its whole bottom surface as its base of support, a chair has
the quadrilateral between its four legs, and a person standing on one leg has only their
own foot.

To decide whether the object stays standing, two concepts are needed. The **centre of gravity**
is the point where we can imagine the whole gravitational force $F_G$ on the object
acting. The **plumb line** is the vertical line straight down through the centre of gravity,
that is, the line along which the gravitational force acts. The name comes from the plumb bob,
a small weight on a string that builders use to make walls straight:
the string always hangs straight down. If you hang an object from a point and
let it settle, the centre of gravity always lies on the plumb line through
the point of suspension.

In the simulation below, a board hangs from a nail, and a plumb bob hangs from the same
nail. Grab the board and turn it: the plumb line through the centre of gravity stays
vertical, but it ends up to the side of the point of suspension. Let go, and
the board swings back until the plumb line coincides with the string of the plumb bob. Trace
the plumb line, change the point of suspension and trace again, and you will see that the lines cross
each other at the centre of gravity.

::: minisim
typ: lodlinje
:::

The rule is simple: **follow the plumb line.** If it meets
the ground **inside** the base of support, the object stays standing. If it meets the ground **outside**
the base of support, the gravitational force gets a moment about the edge of the base of support, and
the moment turns the object over.

The rule also explains balancing tricks that look impossible. The bottle holder
below rests against the table with a small base of support, and the bottle sticks out far
in the other direction. But the common centre of gravity of board and bottle
lies straight above the base of support, and then nothing topples over.

## Toppling

How far can an object tilt before it topples over? The same rule applies: it
topples over when the centre of gravity ends up outside the base of support. When the object is tilted
about an edge, the base of support shrinks to just that edge. The edge becomes
the **pivot**, and what matters is whether the plumb line through the centre of gravity
meets the ground inside or outside it.

::: demo "Demonstration: Toppling"
How far can you tilt something before it topples over? We investigate this by
tilting a block with a metal pointer at its centre of gravity, which illustrates
the direction of the gravitational force. We see that when the centre of gravity ends up outside
the pivot, the block topples over! Try it yourself below: grab the
top edge of the block and tilt it in either direction (or use the slider). The arrow from
the centre of gravity is the gravitational force $F_G$: it always points straight down and is
the same length however much you tilt the block. When you let go, the block
is released, and you see whether it falls back or topples over. Compare the tall
block with the low one.

::: minisim
typ: valtning
:::
:::

For a rectangular block, the limit can be calculated.

::: formel "The toppling angle for a block"

the middle. If it is tilted about an edge, it topples over when the angle of tilt $\alpha$
satisfies

$$
\tan \alpha = \frac{b}{h}
$$

where

- *b* = width (m)
- *h* = height (m)
- $\alpha$ = angle of tilt at the toppling limit (°)

## Stable, unstable and neutral equilibrium

Not all equilibria are equally steady. Physics distinguishes between three cases, and
the test is the same in all three: **disturb the object slightly and see what
happens.**

- **Stable equilibrium**: the object moves **back** to the equilibrium position.
  The ball in the bowl rolls up a little, turns and rolls down again.
- **Unstable equilibrium**: the disturbance **grows** and the object leaves
  the equilibrium position. The ball on top of the hill just keeps rolling down.
- **Neutral equilibrium**: the object **stays** in its new position, which is
  just as good an equilibrium position as the old one. The ball on the flat floor lies
  still where it ends up.

The difference lies in what happens to the **height of the centre of gravity**. In the bowl,
the centre of gravity must be **raised** for the ball to leave the bottom, and
the gravitational force then turns the ball back: the equilibrium is stable. On the hill,
the centre of gravity is **lowered** whichever way the ball rolls, so the gravitational force
amplifies every disturbance: the equilibrium is unstable. On the floor,
the centre of gravity stays at the **same height** wherever the ball ends up, and no position is
better than any other: the equilibrium is neutral.

The block on the table is in stable equilibrium as long as it is only tilted a little:
the centre of gravity is raised when the block tips up onto its edge, and the block falls
back when it is released. The toppling limit itself, on the other hand, is an unstable
equilibrium. There the block balances on its edge with its centre of gravity in its highest
position, and the slightest disturbance decides which way it falls. A pencil
balanced on its point is for the same reason almost impossible to make stand:
the centre of gravity lies high above a tiny base of support, and every disturbance
lowers it.

## Balance with the centre of gravity below the point of support

There is a sure way to get a stable equilibrium: make sure that
the centre of gravity ends up **below** the point of support. Then every tilt raises
the centre of gravity, and the gravitational force always turns the object back. A lamp hanging
from its cord and a swing hanging from its chains can therefore never topple over.
They can only swing back towards the equilibrium position.

::: demo "Demonstration: Balancing on the point of a needle"
Here is a balancing trick that looks impossible: two forks are pushed into a
cork, a needle is pushed through the cork, and the whole structure balances on the point of the needle
against the extremely narrow edge of a coin. Nothing holds it in place, yet
it stays standing. Why does it not topple over?

Look at where the centre of gravity ends up. Almost all the mass is in the forks'
heavy handles, and they hang downwards and outwards. Therefore the structure's
common centre of gravity is pulled down to a point just **below** the point of support. If
the structure tilts in any direction, the gravitational force $F_G$ gets a lever arm that turns it
**back**: the moment corrects the tilt instead of amplifying
it. This is exactly the opposite of the block above, where the centre of gravity lies above
the pivot.

Try it yourself: press "Push" and watch the structure rock back like a
pendulum about the point of the needle, or set it spinning and see that it keeps its
balance even then. Drag in the scene to turn the camera, and click directly on
the cork (push) or a fork handle (spin). With the slider you raise
the forks, and in the end the centre of gravity ends up above the point of support: then
the structure falls just as surely as the block topples over. In full-screen mode there are more
tools: show the gravitational force and the lever arm, or remove the forks completely and
see the difference.

::: minisim
typ: gaffelbalans3d
:::

It is the same principle that a tightrope walker uses with a long balancing pole.
The pole is heavy and bends downwards at the ends, so that the common
centre of gravity of walker and pole sinks closer to the rope, and sometimes even
below it. Every small tilt then becomes something that the gravitational force
corrects instead of amplifies, just as for the forks. The long
pole also makes the whole body turn more slowly, so the walker
has time to react before the tilt has become large.
:::

## The centre of gravity seeks its lowest position

Stable equilibrium can be summed up in a single sentence: **an object that can
move freely eventually comes to rest where the centre of gravity is as low as
possible.** The ball comes to rest at the bottom of the bowl, the lamp hangs straight down below its
hook and the block falls back down onto its wide side. This rule can
also be used to build a demonstration that seems to defy
gravity.

::: demo "Demonstration: The double cone that rolls uphill"
Here is a demonstration that seems to break the laws of physics. A track
is built from two rails that meet at a point: the point lies low, and the top edges of the rails
rise towards the wide end. A **double cone**, two cones
joined with their thick bases against each other, is placed at the low
point. When it is released, it rolls by itself up towards the high end!
If an ordinary cylinder is placed on the track instead, it rolls downwards, just as
expected.

The explanation lies in the **centre of gravity**. An object that rolls freely always moves
to where its centre of gravity is lowest, and what decides the height of the centre of gravity
is where on the object the rails support it. At the point, the rails are
close together and support the double cone near its thick middle, so that the centre of gravity
ends up high. Towards the wide end, the rails move apart: the points of contact
travel out towards the narrow tips of the cone, and the cone sinks down between
the rails. The centre of gravity therefore sinks all the way, even though the top edges of the rails
rise. The cylinder is equally thick everywhere, so its centre of gravity
follows the slope of the rails and it rolls downwards.

Try it yourself below: drag the object wherever you like on the track, let go and follow
the yellow trail of the centre of gravity. Switch to the cylinder and compare, and make the track
steeper with the slider until even the double cone cannot make it. In
full-screen mode there are more tools: show the points of contact travelling out
towards the tips, compare with the horizontal starting level and change the opening angle of the
rails.

::: sammanfattning "Summary"

::: sampunkt "The base of support decides"
- The **base of support** is the area enclosed by the object's points of contact with the
  surface.
- The **plumb line** is the vertical line through the centre of gravity. The object
  **stays standing** when it meets the ground **inside** the base of support.
- It **topples over** when the plumb line ends up **outside**: the moment of the gravitational force
  turns it about the edge of the base of support.
:::

::: sampunkt "The toppling angle"
- A block with width $b$ and height $h$ topples over at the tilt
  $\tan \alpha = \dfrac{b}{h}$.
- At the limit, $F_G$ points exactly at the pivot.
- Low and wide can take a large tilt. Tall and narrow topples over easily.
:::

::: sampunkt "Three kinds of equilibrium"
- **Stable**: the object is turned back. The centre of gravity is raised when disturbed
  (the ball in the bowl).
- **Unstable**: the disturbance grows. The centre of gravity is lowered (the ball on the hill).
- **Neutral**: the object stays in the new position. The centre of gravity stays at
  the same height (the ball on the floor).
:::

::: sampunkt "Centre of gravity below the point of support"
- If the centre of gravity lies **below** the point of support, the equilibrium is always stable:
  every tilt raises the centre of gravity and $F_G$ turns it back.
- The forks on the point of the needle and the tightrope walker's balancing pole rely on this.
:::

::: sampunkt "The lowest position wins"
- An object that can move freely comes to rest where the centre of gravity is
  lowest.
- The double cone rolls "uphill" on the track because its centre of gravity still sinks.
- The bottom of the bowl, the lamp straight below the hook: the same principle.
:::
:::
