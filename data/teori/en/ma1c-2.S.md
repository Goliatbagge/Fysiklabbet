# Summary: Algebra and equations

This chapter was about writing, interpreting, simplifying, expanding and
factorising algebraic expressions, and about solving equations and inequalities of
several different kinds: linear equations, equations with denominators, simple
quadratic and cubic equations, power equations and inequalities. You have
also practised solving for a variable in a formula and finding a closed
formula for a sequence. After the chapter you should be able to solve these
types of equation methodically, never by trial and error, and determine
how many solutions an equation or inequality has.

## Concepts to know

- **variable**: a letter (for example $x$) that represents an unknown or
  varying number.
- **coefficient**: the number that multiplies a variable in a term
  (for example 150 in $150x$).
- **variable term / constant term**: a term that contains a variable
  and a term that consists only of a number, respectively.
- **value of the expression**: the number you get when you substitute a value for
  the variable and evaluate the expression.
- **simplify expressions**: combine like terms ($x$ terms separately,
  $x^2$ terms separately, constant terms separately and so on).
- **expand expressions**: rewrite an expression with brackets without brackets
  by multiplying the factor into each term.
- **the distributive law**: the rule $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ that lies behind all
  multiplication with a bracket.
- **factorise expressions**: write an expression as a multiplication by
  taking out a common factor. The factor can be a number, a variable
  or a whole bracket expression that recurs in all the terms.
- **equation**: an equality between two expressions that contains a variable;
  solving it means solving for the variable.
- **inequality**: a comparison between two expressions with an inequality sign
  (<, >, ≤, ≥) instead of an equals sign.
- **interval**: a connected set of numbers on the number line, for example
  all numbers between −2 and 2, $-2 < x < 2$. The solution to an inequality is
  usually one or more intervals.
- **quadratic equation / cubic equation**: an equation where the variable's
  highest exponent is 2 and 3, respectively.
- **power equation**: an equation where the variable is raised to an
  arbitrary exponent, $x^n = a$.
- **sequence and term**: a series of numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$; each
  number in the series is called a term.
- **closed formula**: a formula that gives an arbitrary term $a_n$ in a
  sequence directly, without calculating all the previous terms.
- **quantity and formula**: something measurable, for example length or speed
  (quantity), and an equation that describes a relationship between
  quantities (formula), respectively.

## Formulas

::: formel "The chapter's formulas"
**The distributive law**

$$
a(b + c) = ab + ac
$$

**The basics of solving equations**

The same operation is done on both sides, with the opposite operation to
remove a term or factor:

- addition is removed by subtraction, and vice versa
- multiplication is removed by division, and vice versa

**Simple quadratic and cubic equations**

$$
x^2 = a\ (a \geq 0) \implies x = \pm\sqrt{a}
$$

$$
x^3 = a \implies x = \sqrt[3]{a}
$$

where *a* is the number on the right-hand side of the equation. In the quadratic case there are no real
solutions if $a < 0$.

**Power equations**

$$
x^n = a \implies x = a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}
$$

where an even integer $n$ gives $\pm$ in front of the root (two, one or zero
solutions) and an odd integer gives one solution without $\pm$. With a fraction or
a decimal number in the exponent, $x$ must be positive, and then no $\pm$ is written.

**Inequalities**

Solved like equations, but the inequality sign is **reversed** when multiplying
or dividing by a negative number.
:::

## Important relationships and methods

- Equations are solved by doing the same operation on both sides,
  NEVER by trial (guessing and testing your way to a solution).
- A minus sign in front of a bracket changes the sign of all the terms inside
  the bracket when the bracket is removed; a plus sign leaves the signs
  unchanged.
- When multiplying two brackets, each term in the first
  bracket must be multiplied by each term in the second.
- When factorising, you take out the greatest common numerical factor and
  the lowest power of each variable that appears in all the terms. If
  the same bracket appears in all the terms, the whole bracket is taken out:
  $3(x + 2) - x(x + 2) = (x + 2)(3 - x)$.
- Even numbers are written $2k$, odd numbers $2k + 1$ and three consecutive
  integers $n$, $n + 1$ and $n + 2$. Showing that a sum is always divisible
  by 3 is done by writing the sum, simplifying and factorising so that
  the factor 3 is visible: $n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3(n + 1)$.
- If the equation has variables on both sides: collect the variable terms on the side
  that has the smallest coefficient, which makes the rest of the calculation simpler.
- If the variable disappears during the solution, the equality that remains decides: a
  false equality (for example $-12 = 63$) means that the equation has no
  solution, a true equality (for example $3 = 3$) that it has infinitely
  many solutions, all numbers.
- In equations with the variable in the denominator you must first state which values
  make the denominator zero (and so are not allowed); if the equation has one
  fraction term on each side you solve by cross-multiplication, and with
  three or more fraction terms you multiply both sides by the lowest
  common denominator (LCD).
- Problem solving is done in three steps: translate (introduce a notation and set
  up an equation), solve the equation, and interpret and answer with a unit,
  and check that the answer is reasonable.
- Quadratic inequalities are solved by first investigating the boundary case
  (the corresponding equation) and then determining which interval satisfies
  the inequality with the help of a number line.
- In power equations, whether the exponent is even or odd decides the number of solutions: an even
  exponent gives up to two solutions (with $\pm$), an odd exponent gives
  always exactly one solution.
- A closed formula for a sequence is found by guessing a term from
  the difference between the terms, testing against $a_1$, and adjusting with a
  constant until the formula is correct for several terms.
- When using formulas, the units must be consistent throughout the whole
  calculation (for example converting Swedish miles to km, and km/h to the same unit of time
  as the other values) before substituting them into the formula.

## Before the test

- Can you write and interpret an expression (variable, coefficient,
  variable term, constant term), and simplify it by combining
  like terms and handling signs in front of a bracket?
- Can you expand a bracket expression with the distributive law, even when two
  brackets are multiplied together?
- Can you factorise an expression by taking out a common factor
  (a number, a variable or a whole bracket expression)?
- Can you write even numbers, odd numbers and consecutive numbers with a
  variable, and show that a sum is always divisible by a certain number?
- Can you solve a linear equation step by step, without trial and error?
- Do you know how to solve an equation with variables on both sides, and do you
  recognise when an equation has no solution?
- Can you solve an equation with denominators, state which values are not
  allowed, and choose the right method based on the number of fraction terms?
- Can you solve a problem by introducing a notation, setting up an
  equation, solving it and interpreting the answer with the right unit?
- Can you solve simple quadratic and cubic equations and a
  power equation, and determine the number of solutions based on whether the exponent
  is even or odd?
- Can you solve an inequality and remember to reverse the inequality sign when
  multiplying or dividing by a negative number?
- Can you solve a quadratic inequality by investigating the boundary case and
  drawing the solution on a number line?
- Can you solve for a variable in a formula and substitute values with the right
  units?
- Can you find a closed formula for a sequence and use it to
  calculate a term far along in the sequence?
